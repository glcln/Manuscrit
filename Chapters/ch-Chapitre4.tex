\chapter{Search for Heavy Stable Charged Particles using the Run--3 data}
\chaptermark{Search for Heavy Stable Charged Particles using the Run--3 data}
\label{ch:chapitre4}
\minitoc


HSCPs differ from most long-lived searches conducted within CMS in that they do not focus on particle decays or reconstruction of final-state object cascades. Instead, the signature remains conceptually straightforward as it is inherent in the candidate track itself. The methodology relies on the Bethe-Bloch formula for energy loss: particles with low $\beta\gamma$ values are more likely to ionise, and since HSCPs are highly massive, they are produced with low $\beta\gamma$ across nearly the entire accessible momentum range. Consequently, a heavy stable candidate produces a track characterised by both high momentum and anomalously high ionisation, and the combination of these measurements yields a reconstructed mass well beyond the Standard Model spectrum.

The straightforward nature of the HSCP signature is reflected in the analytical tools employed. The analysis utilises a limited set of direct track-level selection criteria and a data-driven background estimation.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The motivation for this search arises from the larger Run--3 dataset collected at a higher centre-of-mass energy, and the limitations identified in its Run--2 predecessor~\cite{HSCP_Run2}. The previous analysis relied on muon triggers, which are not optimal for slow-moving R-hadrons. In addition, this search was confined to the barrel region ($|\eta|<1$), further restricting the signal acceptance.

The present analysis targets R-hadrons produced via gluino pair production, whose experimental signature consists of a high-momentum, highly ionising track accompanied by MET. This missing energy may originate from intrinsic signal properties, mis-reconstruction of these particles, or detector effects. Triggering on MET therefore recovers the neutral-escape topology missed by the muon-based selection. Meanwhile, the tracker acceptance is extended to its full pseudorapidity range so that no signal track is excluded for geometrical reasons.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

This study uses data collected in 2024, which offers an optimal balance between integrated luminosity (109~fb$^{-1}$ out of a total of 310~fb$^{-1}$ for Run--3), detector quality and calibration. All MC and signal samples presented in \cref{sec:dataset} have also been produced using the 2024 detector configurations.

Within this analysis, my contributions include defining the adopted MET trigger strategy (\cref{sec:HSCPpresel}), developing the data-driven background estimation method (\cref{sec:bkgmethod}), extracting the systematic uncertainties (\cref{sec:systematics}) and deriving the exclusion limits (\cref{sec:limits}). The event selection closely follows the Run--2 analysis, while other members of the international team managed the generation of signal samples.



\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:dataset}

The data analysed were collected in 2024 at a centre-of-mass energy of $\sqrt{s}=13.6$~TeV, spanning seven eras (C through I) and corresponding to a total integrated luminosity of 109\,fb$^{-1}$. This selection follows the recommendations of the CMS Physics Performance and Dataset Project (PPD) for proton-proton analysis, from the ten recorded eras (A through J). The analysis considers events that pass a MET trigger and are stored in the MET dataset.

The signal samples (\cref{tab:signal_samples}) consist of pair-produced gluinos that hadronise into R-hadrons, generated using \textsc{MadGraph} at $\sqrt{s}=13.6$~TeV for ten mass hypotheses between 1.1 and 2.6~TeV. R-hadrons were produced with a gluinoball fraction\footnote{The gluinoball fraction is the proportion of R-hadrons composed of $\tilde{g}g$ pairs; such bound states are neutral.} of 10\%, and were assigned an infinite gluino lifetime, indicating that the R-hadron remains stable over time (except for charge flipping). The theoretical production cross section, calculated at NNLO+NNLL \cite{10.21468/SciPostPhysCore.7.4.072}, decreases as the gluino mass increases. This mass range exceeds the exclusion limits set by the previous CMS search ($m_{\tilde{g}}^\textrm{lim}=2.08$~TeV)~\cite{HSCP_Run2}.

The main background, after the analysis pre-selection, primarily consists of high-$p_T$ muons produced in addition to MET. The dominant processes are $W$ boson production in association with jets in the muonic decay channel, $W(\to\mu\nu)+\text{jets}$; top-quark pair production in the semi- and di-leptonic channel, $t\bar{t}\to \ell \nu qq$ and $t\bar{t}\to 2\ell 2\nu$. Additionally, QCD multijet production is modelled using twelve slices of muon-enriched samples spanning $\hat{p}_T$\footnote{$\hat{p}_T$ is the transverse momentum of the outgoing partons in the hard scattering at generator level. QCD samples are binned in $\hat{p}_T$ because the cross section falls steeply with $\hat{p}_T$} from 15~GeV to above 1~TeV. These MC samples, listed in \cref{tab:mc_samples_2024}, are primarily utilised to guide the HSCP pre-selection stage. These samples are not incorporated into the background prediction method.

All signal and background MC samples are reweighted to match the 2024 luminosity, from which the corresponding weight is given in the tables and calculated as follows:
\begin{equation}
    w = \frac{\sigma \times \mathcal{L}}{\rm Total\,\,number\,\,of\,\,events} \, .
\end{equation}

\section{d\textit{E}/d\textit{x} discriminators}
\label{sec:discriminators}

The identification of HSCPs relies on the anomalously large ionisation produced along their trajectory. The CMS tracker provides this information using two technologically distinct subdetectors. Rather than combining all measurements into a single estimator, this analysis utilises them separately through a pixel discriminator $F_{\text{pixel}}$ and a strip estimator $I_h$.

In addition to their complementarity, the selection of these two estimators is justified by their uncorrelated nature for the SM tracks analysed in this study. Ionisation measurements along the same track are typically correlated because they depend on the average energy loss, which is determined by the particle's momentum and mass. However, at $p\gg 1$~GeV, all SM species are highly relativistic, and their expected energy loss becomes largely independent of these variables, making the mean energy deposit uniform across all tracks in the sample. Because $F_{\text{pixel}}$ and $I_h$ are constructed from separate sets of hits in the pixel and strip layers, respectively, these fluctuations are sampled independently, rendering the two estimators uncorrelated. In contrast, for HSCPs, both estimators respond to the same substantial energy deposit and are therefore strongly correlated. Any residual correlations, which do not vanish entirely, are detailed in \cref{sec:systematics}.


\subsection{The d\textit{E}/d\textit{x} pixel discriminator \texorpdfstring{$F_{\text{pixel}}$}{Fpixel}}

In this analysis, the pixel information is exploited via $F_{\text{pixel}}$, which quantifies the compatibility of a track with a MIP deposit. The pixel cluster shape and the charge distribution of MIPs are modelled from \texttt{PixelAV} simulations~\cite{1495807}, which reproduce charge deposition, transport, and collection processes in pixel sensors. 
These simulations are periodically tuned to data using large-$\eta$ tracks to provide angle-dependent models that yield different Vavilov function\footnote{Alreay discussed in \cref{sec:interactionWithMatter}, the Vavilov distribution is the exact description of these energy loss fluctuations for an absorber of finite thickness. It depends on $\beta^2$ and on
$\kappa = \xi/E_{\max}$, the ratio of the mean energy loss to the maximum single-collision transfer, and reduces to the Landau distribution for $\kappa \to 0$~\cite{VavilovFunctionPixels}.} $V(Q)$ templates to model the charge distribution of MIPs.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

For a given pixel cluster carrying a charge $Q_{\text{cluster}}$, we define the tail probability 
\begin{equation}
p_Q = \int_{Q_{\text{cluster}}}^{+\infty} V(Q)\,\mathrm{d}Q,
\end{equation}
which quantifies the probability of depositing a charge greater than $Q_{\text{cluster}}$ for a MIP (see~\cref{fig:clustQ}). According to the Probability Integral Transform theorem~\cite{PITtheorem}, if $Q_{\text{cluster}}$ follows a Vavilov distribution $V(Q)$, then $p_Q$ is uniformly distributed on the interval $[0,1]$. In other words, assuming the particle is a MIP and the model $V(Q)$ is accurate, $p_Q$ is uniformly distributed by construction.

To complete the description of the four (three) layers of the barrel (endcap) pixel subdetector, a joint coefficient is built using the different tail probabilities $p_{Q_i}$ associated with the charge deposits $Q_{\text{cluster}_i}$ in each pixel layer $i$:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_n = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_{Q_{\text{cluster}_i}}.
\end{equation}
The quantity $\alpha_n$ decreases if a single layer is strongly ionising, and becomes significantly smaller if all layers are.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.7\textwidth]{Images/Ch4/vavilov_charge.pdf}
    \caption{Example of charge deposits in pixel sensors at $\eta=2.4$, with the distribution (black) fitted using a Vavilov function (red). The tail distribution $p_Q$ is shown in filled grey.}
    \label{fig:clustQ}
\end{figure}

A difficulty arises because $\alpha_n$ is not uniformly distributed, even for a MIP. Its distribution depends strongly on $n$, which prevents a meaningful comparison between, for instance, the $n=3$ and $n=4$ cases. A different variable must therefore be constructed to obtain a behaviour subject to universal selection.

To do so, the variable $-\ln(\alpha_n)$ is taken as it follows a Gamma distribution with parameters $(n,1)$, if and only if each $p_{Q_{\text{cluster}_i}}$ is uniformly distributed on $[0,1]$. The corresponding probability density function is then
\begin{equation}
\Gamma(n,1) = \frac{x^{\,n-1} e^{-x}}{(n-1)!}, \qquad x = -\ln(\alpha_n).
\end{equation}

The associated cumulative distribution function (CDF) $\beta_n$ can be expressed analytically as
\begin{equation}
\beta_n = \alpha_n \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} \frac{\left[-\ln(\alpha_n)\right]^m}{m!}.
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Since $\beta_n$ is the exact CDF of $\alpha_n$ under the MIP hypothesis, the Probability Integral Transform theorem guarantees that $\beta_n$ is uniformly distributed on [0,1] for MIPs, and the $n$-dependence that affected the raw coefficient $\alpha_n$ is fully absorbed into its CDF. This restores the uniformity required for a universal selection.

We therefore define:
\begin{equation}
    F_{\text{pixel}} = 1 - \beta_n \, ,
\end{equation}
which is also uniformly distributed on $[0,1]$ for MIPs, regardless of the number of pixel layers considered.

However, for strongly ionising particles, such as gluinos, the charge deposited in each layer exceeds that expected for a MIP. This leads to small $p_{Q_i}$ values, hence to a small $\alpha_n$ and a $\beta_n$ close to zero, so that $F_{\text{pixel}}$ approaches unity.

The variable $F_{\text{pixel}}$ therefore constitutes an effective discriminator between Standard Model and BSM processes.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

In this analysis, hits from the first layer of the pixel barrel detector were excluded from the computation of $F_{\text{pixel}}$ due to premature ageing caused by high radiation levels in this layer of the Phase--1 detector. \cref{fig:FpixelInSignalData} (left) shows the $F_{\text{pixel}}$ distribution for data collected in 2024, in a region free from signal contamination (all analysis pre-selection criteria, listed in \cref{tab:preselection}, and $p_T < 55$~GeV). The measured $F_{\text{pixel}}$ distribution exhibits deviations from perfect uniformity. The latter is achieved only if the Vavilov templates accurately represent charge collection in every layer. Because the first barrel layer is excluded, mismodelling in any individual layer has a greater impact on the product $\alpha_n$. On the other hand, the $F_{\text{pixel}}$ distribution for a simulated HSCP gluino at $m_{\tilde{g}}=2000$~GeV after the analysis pre-selection criteria (right) shows that most of the signal is concentrated around unity. In both plots, a lower threshold of 0.3 is applied to $F_\textrm{pixel}$ to exclude broken pixel clusters.

A single set of templates is used on the 2024 MC and signal samples, so it does not reflect changes in pixel response due to accumulated irradiation. Both effects are accounted for within the associated systematic uncertainty, as discussed in \cref{subsec:systSignal}.

\subsection{The d\textit{E}/d\textit{x} strip estimator \texorpdfstring{$I_h$}{Ih}}

The search for HSCPs relies on tracking anomalous d$E$/d$x$ deposits within detector materials. The average energy deposit per track across the silicon tracker layers is quantified using the $I_h$ estimator, presented in \cref{sec:ihpres}. Owing to their greater mass, HSCPs generate higher energy deposits than MIPs produced by SM processes. These results are evident in \cref{fig:dedx_vs_p_2024}, where SM processes exhibit $I_h$ values close to the MIP level for $p\gtrsim5$~GeV. In contrast, as shown in \cref{fig:Ih_vs_p_HSCP}, the signal processes maintain high $I_h$ values even in the high-momentum regime. Therefore, $I_h$ enables effective discrimination between SM processes and HSCPs. This approach is complementary to the pixel-based estimator $F_{\text{pixel}}$, which deals with the charge distribution in the pixel layers, whereas $I_h$ aggregates ionisation measurements across the strip tracker.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\cref{fig:IhSignalData} demonstrates this separation. The upper panel displays the normalised $I_h$ distribution after applying analysis pre-selection criteria (see \cref{tab:preselection}), with an additional restrictive cut of $F_{\text{pixel}}<0.9$ applied to the data (left) to mitigate signal contamination, and for the signal (right). The data distribution exhibits a sharp peak at low ionisation, approximately $3.2$~MeV/cm, consistent with the MIP expectation, and decreases rapidly at higher values. In contrast, a gluino with $m_{\tilde{g}}=2000$~GeV gives rise to higher $\langle\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x\rangle$ values. The lower panel shows the corresponding cumulative distribution functions: the data CDF rises to unity almost immediately after the MIP level, while the gluino CDF increases gradually, highlighting the strong separation between the two populations across most of the $I_h$ range.

\subsection{Stability of d\textit{E}/d\textit{x} variables over the year}

The stability of the two ionisation observables used in this analysis is monitored throughout 2024 by computing, for each run, the mean values of $I_h$ and $F_\text{pixel}$ for tracks that pass the analysis pre-selection. The evolution of $\langle I_h \rangle$ as a function of run number is presented in \cref{fig:dedx_stability} (top left), along with the distribution of the per-run means (top right). Following the application of the charge calibration described in \cref{app:calibration}, the strip-based estimator remains stable over the year. The distribution of the mean is centred around $\langle I_h \rangle \approx 3.37$~MeV/cm, with a spread at the per cent level for tracks that pass the analysis pre-selection step.

A similar monitoring procedure is applied to the pixel-based discriminant (in the bottom row). The distribution of the mean of $F_\text{pixel}$ is centred around 0.65 for tracks that pass the analysis pre-selection step, with a relative dispersion of a few per cent. Because $F_\text{pixel}$ is constructed from the pixel charge templates, the slight decrease observed over the year reflects the progressive degradation of the pixel sensors due to irradiation, which reduces the collected charge. However, this drift remains modest compared to the width of the $F_\text{pixel}$ distribution, allowing the variable to be used consistently throughout the 2024 dataset.

These checks confirm that both ionisation variables can be used consistently over the full 2024 dataset without any time-dependent correction beyond the charge calibration already applied.

\section{Selecting signal-like candidates}
\label{sec:HSCPpresel}

\subsection{Trigger event selection}
\label{sec:trigger}

The Run--3 analysis targets primarily hadron-like HSCPs, for which the single-muon trigger strategy adopted in Run--2 is not optimal. R-hadrons travel slowly within the detector and may reach the muon chambers too late to be reconstructed in the event. The $\beta=v/c$ distributions for different signal mass points illustrate this behaviour. Although these particles can be produced with high momentum, their substantial mass reduces their velocity, resulting in distribution cores located at $\beta\approx 0.55$ (see \cref{fig:betaHSCP} left).

To evaluate the performance of a muon-based trigger, such as \texttt{HLT\_Mu50} (which requires online $p_T^\mu>50$~GeV at the HLT level), the fraction of generated gluino events passing this trigger relative to the total number of events can be examined as a function of $\beta$, as shown in \cref{fig:betaHSCP} (right), where the turn-on point is reached at $\beta\approx 0.6$. This results in a low trigger efficiency of approximately 11\%. Consequently, the use of a muon trigger significantly reduces signal selection efficiency and diminishes the sensitivity of this analysis.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{Images/Ch4/c_BetaDistribution__TriggEffBeta_bis.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{Images/Ch4/c_TriggerEfficiencyBeta__TriggEffBeta_bis.pdf}
    \caption{Distribution of the generated beta for different gluino mass points (left) and muon trigger efficiency as a function of the generated beta (right).}
    \label{fig:betaHSCP}
\end{figure}

Similarly, the composition of R-hadron pairs complicates the study because the fractions of neutral-neutral, neutral-charged, and charged-charged R-hadrons change between candidate generation and after matching with event tracks, as shown in \cref{fig:HSCPfractionPair}.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{Images/Ch4/PairTypeStages_SingleMass__Notrigger_2000__PairTypeStages_bis.pdf}
    \caption{Evolution of the HSCP pair-type composition across the analysis chain, for a gluino signal with $m_{\tilde{g}}=2000$~GeV: at the generated level (black dots), further split by the number of matched HSCP candidates (0 in blue up-triangles, 1 in green down-triangles, or 2 in pink stars).}
    \label{fig:HSCPfractionPair}
\end{figure}

At the generator level, signal events contain two generated HSCPs, distributed among neutral-neutral, charged-neutral, and charged-charged pair types with respective fractions of approximately 30\%, 50\%, and 20\% (black dots in \cref{fig:HSCPfractionPair}). After requiring a track match between the generated HSCPs and the event-reconstructed tracks, the observed total fractions for 0-matched-track (blue up triangles), 1-matched-track (green down triangles), and 2-matched-track (pink stars) events, summed over all pair categories, are 40\%, 46\%, and 14\%, respectively. Moreover, the neutral-neutral category is populated exclusively with 0 matched tracks, and the charged-neutral category predominantly with 1 matched track and, to a lesser extent, with 2-matched tracks. In comparison, the charged-charged category consists of approximately two-thirds 2-matched tracks and one-third 1-matched tracks. Furthermore, the total fractions indicate that the 1-matched-track fraction closely aligns with the generated charged-neutral fraction (46\% compared to 50\%). In contrast, the 2-matched-track fraction is lower than the generated charged-charged fraction (14\% compared to 20\%). In contrast, the 0-matched-track fraction exceeds the generated neutral-neutral fraction (40\% compared to 30\%). This shift toward lower matched-track multiplicities indicates that HSCPs are predominantly lost due to charge flipping of charged R-hadrons to neutral ones.

Additionally, before the pre-selection, the track associated with the charged R-hadron is identified as a PF muon in 99\% of cases (specifically as global and tracker muons) while less than 1\% are classified as PF charged hadrons. These are the only two PF candidate types observed. This outcome is attributed to charge flipping, which occurs predominantly in denser materials (such as the calorimeters), resulting in hits primarily in the silicon tracker and, to a lesser extent, in the muon chamber, without leaving energy deposits in the calorimeters.


\vspace{0.5 cm}

Therefore, to recover a fraction of the neutral-charged pair-produced R-hadrons that would miss the muon trigger window, the analysis employs momentum imbalance to improve signal selection. This also includes, to a lesser extent, pair-produced R-hadrons accompanied by initial-state radiation (ISR). As a result, a dedicated MET trigger strategy was developed, which combines four HLT paths:

\begin{itemize}
    \item[] \texttt{HLT\_PFMET120\_PFMHT120\_IDTight},
    \item[\texttt{OR}] \texttt{HLT\_PFMETNoMu120\_PFMHTNoMu120\_IDTight\_PFHT60},
    \item[\texttt{OR}] \texttt{HLT\_PFHT500\_PFMET100\_PFMHT100\_IDTight},
    \item[\texttt{OR}] \texttt{HLT\_MET105\_IsoTrk50}.
\end{itemize}

The naming convention of each HLT path directly encodes the online-like cuts applied at that stage: the algorithm prefix (\texttt{PFMET}, \texttt{PFMHT}, \texttt{PFHT}, all defined in \cref{subsubsec:METdef}; and \texttt{IsoTrk}) identifies the reconstructed quantity being thresholded, while the number gives the corresponding $p_T$ (or $H_T$) cut in GeV. The MET prefix refers to the online HLT Calo MET variable. \texttt{IDTight} signifies that tight jet identification is applied to the jets entering the MHT and MET sum, \texttt{NoMu} indicates that muon contributions are subtracted from the PF MET/MHT computation, and \texttt{IsoTrk50} requires an isolated track with $p_T>50$~GeV. 

Furthermore, at the L1 trigger level, the seed lists consist of a logical \texttt{OR} of several \texttt{L1\_ETMHF} thresholds that compute MET using both the central and forward calorimeters. In addition, at the online HLT level, all triggers include a filter on the Calo MET (Calo MET > 90~GeV), while only the first three triggers apply a filter on the PF MET value.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\cref{fig:HSCPfractionPairTrigger} (left) presents the individual MET trigger efficiency, the efficiency after the logical \texttt{OR} combination of these triggers, and the efficiency after applying the muon trigger for several signal mass points. The MET triggers demonstrate a higher event selection efficiency ($\approx30\%$) than muon triggers ($\approx11\%$).

\cref{fig:HSCPfractionPairTrigger} (right) presents the HSCP-generated pair-type distribution (black dots, identical to \cref{fig:HSCPfractionPair}) following the application of MET triggers (red squares) and, for comparison, the \texttt{HLT\_Mu50} triggers (dark yellow crosses) for the $m_{\tilde{g}}=2000$~GeV point. The reduction from the generated composition to the post MET trigger composition remains essentially uniform across all three pair-type categories: summing the red squares over the three categories recovers the same $\approx$30\% efficiency obtained for the $m_{\tilde{g}}=2000$~GeV point of \cref{fig:HSCPfractionPairTrigger} (left). This efficiency is higher than that achieved with the muon trigger, because only events containing a charged HSCP and an associated track are selected, thus retaining only the neutral-charged and charged-charged categories.

Consequently, MET triggers are designated for this analysis, as they demonstrate the highest efficiency for the relevant R-hadron topologies.

\subsubsection{HSCP MET topologies}

The MET signature arising from the distinct properties of R-hadrons deserves further investigation, as PF MET and Calo MET measurements vary with the specific production topology. PUppi MET, the Run 3 recommended MET estimator, which is similar to PF MET but incorporates more robust pile-up mitigation, will be used in place of PF MET. PUppi MET is only produced when the R-hadron pair consists of one charged and one neutral state, since a charged-charged or neutral-neutral pair leaves no net $p_T$ imbalance. In contrast, an ISR gluon, which would hadronise into a jet, or a photon recoiling against the pair production, produces ECAL or HCAL deposits. Since charged or neutral HSCPs leave minimal calorimeter information, this results in an imbalance in the calorimeter towers and consequently leads to non-zero Calo MET. Five kinematic topologies, depending on the nature of the pair-produced HSCP, were identified on this basis and are illustrated in \cref{fig:METtopology}, ranging from configurations with negligible MET to those dominated by either PUppi or Calo MET, thereby motivating the use of several complementary MET observables rather than a single variable.

\subsubsection{MET trigger strategy}

The trigger decision relies on online quantities, specifically Calo MET at the L1 level and both Calo MET and PF MET at the HLT level. In contrast, the analysis selection employs their offline counterparts, namely Calo MET and PUppi MET. The finite-resolution difference between these quantities causes efficiency to increase gradually rather than exhibit a step function at the nominal threshold. The width and position of this efficiency rise are determined by the online resolution and the calibration of the online objects, neither of which is precisely reproduced in simulation. Consequently, even a small offset between data and simulation can result in a discrepancy in efficiency where the curve is steep, making it unreliable to predict the number of signal events accepted by the trigger from simulation within the Calo MET or PUppi MET range. Beyond the turn-on, in the \textit{trigger plateau}, the efficiency saturates and becomes flat. On this plateau, the ratio of the trigger efficiency in data to that in simulation, expressed as a function of one MET variable and known as the SF, is constant. Regions where the SFs are large and approximately constant are selected to characterise the trigger response. In contrast, small or non-constant SFs suggest a strong dependence of the data-to-simulation correction on the MET variables, rendering the correction of signal selection efficiency from simulation less reliable.

This requirement is especially critical for the HSCP analysis, where, if no excess is observed, limits on the theoretical model are computed using a shape analysis. A flat plateau efficiency multiplies the mass spectrum by a constant, affecting only its overall normalisation and leaving both the signal shape and the data-driven background prediction unchanged. However, without scale-factor corrections and a selection applied to the plateau, the signal's accuracy near the turn-on point is uncertain, leading to uncertainty in counting signal events and, consequently, to unreliable exclusion limits.

The adopted strategy follows directly from these considerations. The turn-on curves are first measured in parallel in data and simulation as functions of the offline PUppi MET and Calo MET, and the corresponding data-to-simulation scale factors are extracted. The offline thresholds are then set sufficiently high to place the analysis close to the trigger plateau, where these scale factors are constant and well controlled.


\subsubsection{MET trigger efficiency}

With the transition of the HSCP analysis from the AOD to the \textsc{MiniAOD} framework for Run-3 data, several variables have been modified. Notably, in \textsc{MiniAOD}, the offline calculation of Calo MET now includes muon energy deposits in the calorimeters, introducing a bias in the value. Since trigger efficiency is determined online at the L1 and HLT levels using Calo MET, which differs from its offline value in \textsc{MiniAOD}, it is not possible to evaluate trigger efficiency solely based on Calo MET. To recover the MET deposits within the calo towers, one could then build a proxy observable that mimics the AOD Calo MET. Therefore, a pseudo-MET variable is reconstructed offline from the calorimeter jet (CaloJets) collection as the magnitude of the vectorial transverse-momentum sum of all calorimeter jets in the event,
\begin{equation}
   \mathrm{Pseudo~MET} = \sqrt{\left\|\sum_{\text{CaloJets}} \overrightarrow{p_{x_j}}\right\|^2 + \left\|\sum_{\text{CaloJets}} \overrightarrow{p_{y_j}} \right\|^2 } .
\end{equation}
The comparison between AOD Calo MET and \textsc{MiniAOD} Pseudo MET was validated on the same Run--3 MC dataset, which showed a similar distribution after the analysis pre-selection step. Since Run--3 corrections for the CaloJet collection have not been established by the CMS Jet Energy Resolution and Corrections (JERC) group, the Pseudo MET value remains uncorrected. This discrepancy is not addressed in the trigger efficiency analysis because the Pseudo MET is used solely to identify the trigger plateau and to extract the corresponding scale factors, which depend on the shape of the turn-on curve rather than on absolute energy calibration. Nevertheless, applying an offline selection to this uncalibrated variable presents significant challenges.

\subsubsection{Trigger strategy adopted}

The trigger's response to pair-produced HSCPs depends on their electric charge. This study presents a strategy for selecting HSCP gluinos using MET triggers to address the imbalance for the three production scenarios: charged-charged, neutral-charged, and neutral-neutral. Strategy (2) provides an effective compromise for signal acceptance, given that the uncalibrated Pseudo MET variable requires additional caution. Events are selected using a PUppi MET threshold greater than 150~GeV after passing one of the four MET triggers, with additional checks on the $H_T$, MHT, and MHT$^\mathrm{NoMu}$ variables. The trigger efficiency is corrected in MC and signal samples using one-dimensional scale factors, defined as the ratio of trigger efficiencies as a function of PUppi MET in data and MC simulations from an orthogonal dataset that produces similar MET and signal signatures. The upward and downward variations in these scale factors, resulting from statistical uncertainties, will subsequently be applied as systematic uncertainties in the trigger selection, as discussed in \cref{sec:systematics}.



\subsection{HSCP pre-selection}
\label{sec:preselection}

A pre-selection based on events and tracks requirements, in addition to trigger selection, aims to suppress these backgrounds while maintaining high signal efficiency. The primary sources of background in the data are $W(\to \mu\nu)$+jets, $t\bar{t}\to \ell \nu$ and $t\bar{t}\to 2\ell 2\nu$ events, and QCD multijet $\mu$-enriched production, where a high $p_T$ muon can imitate the signature of a slow, highly ionising particle.

The pre-selection stage established for the Run--3 analysis closely follows the approach used in the previous analysis~\cite{HSCP_Run2}. The primary modifications involve the trigger strategy and the expanded acceptance criteria in eta.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Events are selected if at least one of the MET trigger paths with additional offline checks is valid:

\begin{itemize}
    \item[] \texttt{HLT\_PFMET120\_PFMHT120\_IDTight} and $\rm MHT>120$~GeV,
    \item[\texttt{OR}] \texttt{HLT\_PFMETNoMu120\_PFMHTNoMu120\_IDTight\_PFHT60} and $\rm MHT^\textrm{NoMu}>120$~GeV and $H_T>60$~GeV
    \item[\texttt{OR}] \texttt{HLT\_PFHT500\_PFMET100\_PFMHT100\_IDTight} and $H_T>500$~GeV and \\$\rm MHT>100$~GeV,
    \item[\texttt{OR}] \texttt{HLT\_MET105\_IsoTrk50}; 
\end{itemize}
and pass the standard MET noise filters, which remove events where missing transverse momentum arises from detector effects such as noisy calorimeter cells. An offline requirement of PUppi MET > 150~GeV is subsequently applied to ensure robust signal selection while maintaining scale factors close to unity, thereby preserving reliable simulation modelling.

At least one track with both the PF $p_T$ and track $p_T$ larger than 50~GeV (difference discussed in \cref{sec:PFvsTrack}) and $|\eta| < 2.4$ is requested. The $p_T$ requirement is a mandatory selection to retain tracks with d$E$/d$x$ clusters in the \textsc{MiniAOD} datasets. This threshold minimally affects the signal, as $p/m = \beta\gamma$ implies that particles with masses of several hundred GeV possess momenta well above this limit, while the background spectrum decreases sharply. The pseudorapidity range matches the full tracker acceptance, unlike for the Run-2 analysis, which relied on a limited search to $|\eta| < 1.0$. \cref{fig:signalAcceptance} shows the signal acceptance for various signal mass points and MET data after applying the analysis pre-selection. The extension in $\eta$ increases the signal acceptance from approximately 8.5\% at $|\eta|<1$ to about 11.5\% at $|\eta|<2.4$. A similar trend is observed in the data, accompanied by an increase in background events.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{Images/Ch4/SignalAcceptance_EtaSlice_bis.pdf}
    \caption{Acceptance as a function of the detector $\eta$ range for various signal samples (colored) and the MET datasets (black dots scaled by a factor of 400) after applying the analysis pre-selection.}
    \label{fig:signalAcceptance}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

A first group of criteria targets the quality of the track reconstruction and of the ionisation measurement:
\begin{itemize}
  \item at least two valid pixel hits are required, so that the $F_\textrm{pixel}$ discriminant is built from a minimum of two independent cluster probabilities;
  \item at least ten $\dedx$ measurements must enter the estimators, the first barrel pixel layer being excluded as it is the most exposed to radiation effects. All measurements must also satisfy upstream cleaning \footnote{Strip clusters entering the $I_h$ computation are required to pass a shape-based cleaning: the cluster charge profile must exhibit a maximum or several side-by-side maxima, and the amplitudes of the neighbouring strips must be compatible with the capacitive cross-talk. This rejects merged clusters produced by overlapping tracks.}. For signal tracks, the number of measurements is around fifteen, so this requirement mainly removes short tracks;
  \item the fraction of valid hits, i.e.\ the fraction of hits compatible with the propagated trajectory among the crossed modules, must exceed 0.8, rejecting misreconstructed tracks;
  \item the track must be identified as \textit{HighPurity} (defined in \cref{sec:final_track_fit}) and satisfy $\chi^{2}/N_{\mathrm{dof}} < 5$, the latter being largely redundant with the former but removing a residual population with anomalous fit quality.
  \item Compatibility with the primary vertex is enforced through $|d_{z}| < 0.1$~cm and $|d_{xy}| < 0.02$~cm, restricting the search to promptly produced particles.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Two isolation criteria (defined in \cref{sec:muons}) are applied to reject non-prompt particles coming from the pile-up, or within jets produced by QCD background contributions. The relative isolation, calculated from particle-flow candidates surrounding the track, must satisfy $I_\textrm{rel}^\textrm{PF} < 0.02$. Additionally, the tracker-based isolation $I_\textrm{trk}$, defined for all tracks within a cone of $\Delta R < 0.3$, must remain below 15~GeV. As shown in the cutflow of \cref{tab:preselection}, the particle-flow isolation requirement is among the most effective offline discriminants in the data. This criterion reduces the selected sample by nearly an order of magnitude, while the associated signal loss remains at the per cent level.

Due to the relativistic rise of the Bethe-Bloch curve, electrons in this region can produce ionisation signatures similar to HSCPs. However, electrons deposit nearly all their energy in the electromagnetic calorimeter. Therefore, requiring that the calorimetric energy be less than the track momentum ($E/p < 0.3$) effectively removes this background without affecting the signal.

Tracks with poorly measured momentum, which may result from incorrectly assigned hits or hit sharing between nearby particles, are excluded by requiring $\sigma_{p_T}/p_T^{2}$ to be lower than 0.0008~GeV$^{-1}$ and $\sigma_{p_T}/p_T < 1$. The latter criterion specifically addresses the high-momentum region, where the relative resolution deteriorates.

Two additional requirements are imposed directly on the ionisation estimators. Broken clusters, which occur when a long pixel cluster is split into multiple clusters in the readout chips or due to irradiation effects, bias the charge estimate toward lower values. Applying a cut at $F_\textrm{pixel} > 0.3$ removes these affected tracks, while the signal distribution peaks near unity. A minimum ionisation requirement of $I_h > C_\mathrm{mass}$ is also imposed, where $C_\mathrm{mass}$ is a parameter used in the subsequent mass reconstruction (see \cref{sec:massreco}). Its value sits near the MIP level ($\approx3$~MeV/cm).

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Finally, if multiple candidates are retained in the same event, the candidate with the highest $I_h$ is selected.

\section{Mass reconstruction}
\label{sec:massreco}

The final observable in this analysis is the candidate mass, which is reconstructed from the candidate momentum $p$ and its average ionisation $I_h$. Within the momentum range of the preselected candidates, the Bethe-Bloch formula is dominated by the $1/\beta^2$ term for the signal. Therefore, the average energy loss of a singly charged particle can be approximated as follows:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fitKC}
    f^{\rm KC}: \quad I_h \approx K_\textrm{mass}\cdot \frac{1}{(\beta\gamma)^2} + C_\textrm{mass} =      K_\textrm{mass}\cdot \frac{m^2}{p^2}          + C_\textrm{mass} \, ,
\end{equation}
$C_\textrm{mass}$ represents the value of the ionisation plateau for MIP, while $K_\textrm{mass}$ denotes the slope of the low-velocity rise. Both are fitted in the $(I_h, p)$ plane, similarly to \cref{fig:FitData_atlas_vs_KC}. Inversion of this relation yields a closed-form mass estimate:

\begin{equation}
    M = p \cdot \sqrt{ \frac{I_h - C_\textrm{mass}}{K_\textrm{mass}} }.
    \label{eq:massHSCPanalysis}
\end{equation}

Alternatively, the five-parameter empirical parametrisation described in \cref{eq:fitAtlas} and introduced in \cref{sec:dedxperf} models the complete $\beta\gamma$ dependence of energy loss, with mass values computed numerically using the Brent method (labelled $f^{\rm 5p}$).

Both fit functions are evaluated using low-momentum 2024 data and $W$+jets MC samples, employing the same selection criteria as in \cref{fig:dedx_vs_p_2024} and applying the upstream saturation correction using the shape method. The results presented in \cref{fig:FitData_atlas_vs_KC}, which overlay the $W$+jets distribution of the $I_h$ estimator as a function of $p$ (left), demonstrate close agreement between the data (solid lines) and MC (dashed lines) for each parametrisation. However, the $f^{\rm KC}$ parametrisation of \cref{eq:fitKC} fails to reproduce the observed behaviour: the two degrees of freedom are insufficient, resulting in a fit that diverges in the low-momentum region. Furthermore, both parametrisations derived from the $W$+jets sample fail to represent the gluino sample accurately. In particular, the high-ionisation region is, surprisingly, inadequately described.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

This difference should not be observed, as the mean energy loss of a singly charged particle is determined exclusively by its velocity. Consequently, the $I_h$ curves for protons, kaons, and gluinos are expected to align on a universal curve when plotted as a function of $\beta\gamma$. However, \cref{fig:TIBandALLbetagamma} (left) demonstrates a clear separation between the signal and SM particles for $\beta\gamma$ values below approximately 1.

One possible explanation involves the differing track topologies of the two samples. Signal tracks are produced with high momentum and intersect the modules at near-normal incidence. In contrast, SM particles in the low $\beta\gamma$ region are low in momentum, thus exhibiting a higher curvature: for example, a proton with $p \approx 0.6$~GeV has a curvature radius of approximately 50~cm and reaches the outer-layer strip modules at flattening angles. This difference in path length within the sensor, correlated with the $\beta\gamma$ range probed by each sample, may account for the observed discrepancy.

These observations impose constraints on the calibration protocol. The parametrisation can be fitted either using only SM particles, in both data and MC, or using a combination of signal and SM particles. Both approaches were evaluated using the $f^\mathrm{KC}$ parametrisation and carried through to the computation of the signal limits (the method is later detailed in \cref{sec:limits}). The signal and background yields obtained in both cases are consistent, and the resulting exclusion limits are nearly identical.

As the choice does not affect the final sensitivity, the parametrisation fitted solely on SM particles was adopted, separately for data and MC. This approach enables a direct comparison between the data and the MC distributions. However, a known limitation remains: a parametrisation derived exclusively from data or from the $W$+jets sample introduces a bias, causing the reconstructed signal mass to be systematically lower than the generated value.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The remaining choice between $f^{\rm 5p}$ and $f^{\rm KC}$ is determined by the mass resolution. \cref{fig:massrel} presents the reconstructed mass for signal passing the pre-selection criteria, obtained with both parametrisations (fitted in a $W$+jets sample) for each gluino mass hypothesis, while \cref{tab:mass_resolution} summarises their mean, width, and relative bias. The $f^{\rm 5p}$ parametrisation yields a consistently larger bias and a broader distribution, with the disparity between the two increasing with mass. The bias becomes relevant only when interpreting a potential excess in the background mass spectrum. For instance, an excess observed near a reconstructed mass of $1500$~GeV corresponds to a true mass of approximately $1750$~GeV. \cref{tab:mass_resolution} presents the mapping necessary to translate between reconstructed and true mass values.

\begin{itemize}

    \item $K_\textrm{mass}^\textrm{MC}=2.84$ and $C_\textrm{mass}^\textrm{MC}=3.02$.
\end{itemize}
The differences $\Delta K_\textrm{mass}$ and $\Delta C_\textrm{mass}$ between data and simulation are propagated as a systematic uncertainty on the signal mass scale, as described in
\cref{sec:systematics}.

\section{Background estimation method}
\label{sec:bkgmethod}

The background estimation relies on the mass reconstruction of HSCP candidates. It allows prediction of the mass spectrum in the signal region. 

\subsection{Method description}

Background estimation is performed using the ABCD method, in which the phase space is partitioned according to the $F_\textrm{pixel}$ variable and transverse momentum $p_T$. This plane exhibits two different behaviours for SM MC processes (sum of the four MC processes) compared to the signal (top row), as shown in \cref{fig:2D_mc_fpix_pt}. For SM processes, the $p_T$ distribution shows no significant dependence on $F_\textrm{pixel}$: the linear correlation between the two variables is negligible (bottom). Indeed, a first-degree polynomial fit to the profile of $p_T$ as a function of $F_\textrm{pixel}$ over the range $F_\textrm{pixel} \in [0.3, 1]$ yields a slope of $-2.58 \pm 1.10~\mathrm{GeV}$. Over the full $F_\textrm{pixel}$ range, this corresponds to a variation of the mean $p_T$ of less than $2~\mathrm{GeV}$, below $2\%$ of its central value of $123.7 \pm 0.8~\mathrm{GeV}$. This is further confirmed by the linear correlation factor of the two-dimensional distribution, $r = -4.8 \times 10^{-3}$, consistent with zero. Therefore, for background events, the two variables are considered uncorrelated. In contrast, for the signal, the phase space is mostly populated at higher $p_T$ values and when $F_\textrm{pixel}$ is close to unity. For a gluino sample with $m_{\tilde{g}}=2000$~GeV, applying a threshold of $F_\textrm{pixel} = 0.9$ yields approximately 96\% signal efficiency while maintaining background rejection at nearly 10\%. Additionally, 2\% of this signal population falls within $0.8 < F_\mathrm{pixel} \leq 0.9$, and another 2\% has $F_\mathrm{pixel} \leq 0.8$.

\cref{fig:scheme_ABCDmass} illustrates the defined regions. The control region (CR), corresponding to region $C$, is defined for candidates with high $p_T$ and low $F_\textrm{pixel}$ values. The signal region (SR), denoted as region $D$, comprises HSCP candidates with both high $p_T$ and high $F_\textrm{pixel}$ values. Regions $A$ and $B$ correspond to candidates with low $p_T$ and low $F_\textrm{pixel}$ values, and to candidates with low $p_T$ and high $F_\textrm{pixel}$ values, respectively. A validation region (VR) is introduced to test the background prediction method by designating an intermediate $D'$ region with high $p_T$ and $0.8<F_\textrm{pixel}\leq0.9$.

The number of events in the SR $D$ is computed from the event counts in regions $A$, $B$, and $C$ as follows:
\begin{equation}
	{\rm yield}_D= {\rm yield}_B\times {\rm yield}_C/{\rm yield}_A. 
\label{eq:normalisation}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Because the $\eta$ distribution in the CR $C$ does not accurately represent the $\eta$ distribution in region $D$ (as shown in \cref{fig:eta_reweighting}), a rescaling procedure is implemented to align the $\eta$ distribution with that of the SR. The distribution in CR $C$ is modified as follows:
\begin{equation}
  \eta_C \rightarrow \eta_C \times \frac{\eta_B}{\eta_A} \, .
\end{equation}
These weights are subsequently applied to the $1/p$ and $I_h$ distributions in CR $C$ to achieve consistency with the distribution observed in the signal region.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Furthermore, the method predicts the shape of the mass distribution in the SR $D$ using information from the distributions of the $\langle\dedx\rangle$ estimator $I_h$ and the inverse momentum $1/p$ (to include high $p$ values) taken from the CR $C$.

Due to the correlation between $p$, $p_T$, and $\eta$ (and to a lesser extent between $I_h$ and $\eta$), the mass distribution is reconstructed in bins of $\eta$. To facilitate this, templates in the CR are constructed in two dimensions: ($1/p$, $\eta$) and ($\eta$, $I_h$). The $\eta$ distribution is divided into 24 bins ($\Delta_{\eta} = 0.1$), $I_h$ into 100 bins ($\Delta_{I_h} = 0.05~\mathrm{MeV/cm}$), and $1/p$ into 500 bins ($\Delta_{1/p} = 0.4$~GeV$^{-1}$).

An example of these templates for $1.20\leq\eta<1.44$ is presented in \cref{fig:ih_oP_fit}. Fits are applied to the tails of these templates to minimise the influence of statistical fluctuations. A Gaussian fit is applied to describe the tail of the $I_h$ distribution. For bins with fewer than 100 tracks and situated at least 10\% above the x-value of the maximum, the fitted function value replaces the measured bin. The lower tail of the $1/p$ distribution is modelled using an Error function\footnote{The error function is defined as $\mathrm{erf}(x) = \frac{ \displaystyle2}{ \displaystyle\sqrt{\pi}} \displaystyle \int_{0}^{x} e^{-t^{2}} \, \mathrm{d}t$. The function actually fitted is $f(x) = \frac{ \displaystyle p_{0}}{ \displaystyle2}\left[1 + \mathrm{erf}\!\left(\frac{ \displaystyle x - p_{1}}{ \displaystyle \sqrt{2}\,p_{2}}\right)\right]$, i.e.\ the CDF of a Gaussian of mean $p_{1}$ and width $p_{2}$, scaled by $p_{0}$.}, which is fitted to the bins below 90\% of the x-value of the distribution's maximum. For $1/p$ values below 20\% of this x-value, the fitted value replaces the measured value for that bin. For the latter, the fit exhibits a higher $\chi^2/N_\textrm{dof}$, primarily due to fluctuations near the distribution's maximum.

\clearpage

The mass distribution is filled using a triple loop: the outer loop iterates over $\eta$ bins, the middle loop over $1/p$ bins, and the inner loop over $I_h$ bins. Within this structure, the indices ($i$, $j$, $k$) correspond to the ($\eta$, $1/p$, $I_h$) bin, where the relevant computations are performed:

\begin{itemize}
  \item the mass value $M_{i,j,k}$ as
  \begin{equation}
    M_{i,j,k} = p_{j,i} \times \sqrt{\frac{I_{h_{i,k}}-C_\mathrm{mass}}{K_\mathrm{mass}} }
  \end{equation}
  where $p_{j,i}$ is the momentum value taken as the inverse of the $1/p$ value in the $(j,i)$ bin of the $(1/p,\,\eta)$ template, and $I_{h_{i,k}}$ is the $\langle\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x\rangle$ value of the $(i,k)$ bin of the $(\eta,\,I_h)$ template;
  
  \item and the associated weight $w_{i,j,k}$
  \begin{equation}
    w_{i,j,k} = \left(\frac{n_{ji}}{\sum_m n_{mi}}\right) \cdot h_{ik}
  \end{equation}
  where $n_{ji}$ is the number of observed events in the $(j,i)$ bin of the $(1/p,\,\eta)$ distribution and $h_{ik}$ is the number of observed events in the $(i,k)$ bin of the $(\eta,\,I_h)$ distribution.
\end{itemize}

The mass distribution is then constructed and rescaled to the expected global normalisation given by equation (\ref{eq:normalisation}). Statistical uncertainty associated with the prediction is estimated using 200 pseudo-experiments. For each pseudo-experiment, new $I_h$ and $1/p$ templates are generated by randomly sampling each bin from a Poisson distribution with a mean equal to the yield of the corresponding original template bin. This randomisation is also applied to the yield in the CR used for global normalisation. The mean of the 200 pseudo-mass predictions serves as the central value for the mass prediction, while the RMS defines the statistical uncertainty envelope.


\subsection{Preliminary remarks on the mass reconstruction method}
\label{sec:abcd_remarks}

This method has been employed in several previous iterations of the HSCP analysis. However, multiple modifications have been implemented since the most recent CMS public results~\cite{HSCP_Run2}. The key changes are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A new mapping for the phase space, $F_\textrm{pixel}$, is employed in place of $G_\textrm{strip}$. The $G_\textrm{strip}$ estimator relies on d$E$/d$x$ deposits within strip sensors to determine whether a HSCP candidate is compatible with a track depositing at the MIP level. $G_\textrm{strip}$ increases exponentially towards 1 for events that are incompatible with the MIP hypothesis. However, this estimator correlates with $p_T$: higher $p_T$ values yield greater d$E$/d$x$ deposits, introducing bias in mass-spectrum predictions.
  \item Eta reweighting is now conducted using all three alternative regions ($A$, $B$, and $C$) of the ABCD method, rather than relying solely on regions $B$ and $C$ as in the previous version of the analysis.
  \item The templates are taken from the same CR.
  \item The fits of the $I_h$ and $1/p$ tail templates have been refined to accommodate the expanded $\eta$ acceptance. Indeed, \cref{fig:Ihand1oPinEtaslices} (left) shows that $1/p$ templates vary with pseudorapidity, exhibiting a larger distribution and smaller values in the barrel region than in the endcap region. In contrast, the $I_h$ distributions (right) exhibit less variation across $\eta$ bins.
\end{itemize}

\section{Systematics}
\label{sec:systematics}

This analysis addresses two categories of systematic uncertainties in the final interpretation: uncertainties associated with the background prediction method and those associated with signal modelling.

\subsection{Systematics related to the background prediction}
\label{subsec:systBkgPred}

The background prediction is based on several methodological choices, including the binning of input distributions, the modelling of fit tail templates, and the assumption that the $1/p$ and $I_h$ distributions are independent of $F_\textrm{pixel}$. Each choice introduces a systematic uncertainty in the predicted mass spectrum. These uncertainties are quantified by repeating the full prediction procedure with modified configurations and comparing the resulting mass spectra to the nominal spectrum. For each source, both upward and downward variations are defined, and the bin-by-bin ratios between the varied and nominal spectra are used to determine the corresponding uncertainties. The considered sources are the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The binning of the $\eta$, $I_h$, and $10^{4}/p$ distributions used in the convolution employs nominal bin widths of $\Delta_{\eta} = 0.1$, $\Delta_{I_h} = 0.05~\mathrm{MeV/cm}$, and $\Delta_{1/p} = 0.4$~GeV$^{-1}$, respectively. For each variable, upward (downward) variation is implemented by doubling (halving) the nominal bin width, while all other aspects of the procedure remain unchanged.
    \item The fits used to model the tails of the $I_h$ and $1/p$ templates are varied by shifting the fitted parameters upward and downward by their respective uncertainties. The prediction is then recalculated using these modified templates.
    \item The method assumes that the $I_h$ and $1/p$ templates are independent of the $F_\textrm{pixel}$ variable. Violation of this assumption would invalidate the prediction of the shape in the SR, which is fundamental to the method. The residual correlations are evaluated by taking the ratio of the $I_h$ and $1/p$ templates in the CR to that in the VR. For the latter, the distributions almost coincide, as the rescaling in $\eta$ already corrects the dependence between $1/p$ and $F_\textrm{pixel}$.  However, as shown in \cref{fig:ih_correlation}, the correlation between $I_h$ and $F_\textrm{pixel}$ remains. This ratio is fitted with a linear function, and the resulting bias is corrected in the $I_h$ template in the CR before mass spectrum construction. The systematic uncertainty is estimated by omitting this correction and comparing the resulting mass spectrum with the nominal one, the observed deviation being symmetrised around the nominal prediction to define the up and down variations. The values of this linear correlation are similar between VR and CR for the MC samples.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Systematics related to the signal}
\label{subsec:systSignal}

Uncertainties in the simulation affect the signal predictions. These uncertainties are estimated using the same methodology as for the background. Two types of sources are distinguished. The luminosity and $F_\textrm{pixel}$ uncertainties are fixed yield uncertainties, affecting only the normalisation of the signal prediction and leaving the shape of the mass spectrum unchanged. All the remaining sources are varied upward and downward, the complete analysis chain is rerun, and the bin-by-bin ratios between the varied and nominal mass spectra are used to quantify the corresponding shape uncertainties. The total systematic uncertainty is calculated as the quadratic sum of the individual sources. The considered sources are the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The uncertainty associated with the luminosity measurement is estimated to be 1.4\% for 2024~\cite{CMS-DP-2026-003}. It is applied as a normalisation-only uncertainty on the signal yield.
    \item The systematic uncertainty associated with pile-up modelling is assessed by varying the total inelastic proton-proton cross section used to reweight the simulated pile-up profile to match that observed in data. The nominal value of $69200\,\mu\textrm{b}$ is adjusted by $\pm4.6\%$. For each variation, the data pile-up distribution is recalculated, and the corresponding event weights are applied to the simulation. The bin-by-bin deviation from the nominal mass spectrum is considered as the systematic uncertainty.
    \item Trigger scale factors related to the PUppi MET shown in \cref{fig:triggerSF} (left) are varied within their respective statistical uncertainties.
    \item The $K_\textrm{mass}$ and $C_\textrm{mass}$ calibration factors used in mass reconstruction are varied based on the observed differences between data and simulation. The upward and downward variations are defined as $K_\textrm{mass}^\textrm{MC} \pm |K_\textrm{mass}^\textrm{data} - K_\textrm{mass}^\textrm{MC}|$, and in the same way for $C_\textrm{mass}$.
    \item Jet energy uncertainties, provided by the CMS Jet MET working group, are propagated upward and downward in the computation of $H_T$, MHT, and MHT$^\mathrm{NoMu}$ for the trigger requirement, as well as for the PUppi MET.
    \item The value of $F_\textrm{pixel}$ is sensitive to pixel hit reconstruction, particularly to angle corrections coming from high-$p$ tracks, and the charge templates applied during reconstruction. To evaluate the impact of pixel calibration, a dedicated sample of 100,000 gluino events ($m_{\tilde{g}} = 1800$~GeV) was simulated using an updated Global Tag\footnote{A Global Tag serves as a versioned identifier that defines the coherent set of calibration, alignment, and detector-condition records utilised by the CMS software during dataset processing.} that reflects pixel conditions at the end of 2024, following significant irradiation, and processed with the standard analysis chain. \cref{fig:fi_syst} presents the $F_\textrm{pixel}$ distributions for the nominal and systematic samples, after the analysis pre-selection step. The ratio of yields in the region $F_\textrm{pixel} > 0.9$ between the two samples is $1.016$ after pre-selection. This $1.6\%$ difference is treated as a normalisation-only uncertainty on the signal yield.
\end{itemize}

\section{Statistical interpretation and expected limits}
\label{sec:limits}

The background prediction derived in the previous sections is combined with signal mass distributions to construct a statistical model from which upper limits on the gluino pair-production cross section are extracted. Because the signal region remains blinded at this stage of the analysis, the background expectation replaces the observed data entering the model, and all results presented below are expected limits. These expected limits serve two purposes: they quantify the sensitivity of the search before unblinding and allow for comparison of different analysis configurations, including selection thresholds and the treatment of pseudorapidity acceptance regimes.


\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{CL$_\text{s}$}{CLs} method}
\label{sec:limits:cls}

Upper limits are determined using the modified frequentist CL$_\text{s}$ method~\cite{CLSpaper}. This approach represents the standard procedure for searches at the LHC. Two hypotheses are considered: the background-only hypothesis $b$, in which the data are fully described by known SM processes, and the signal-plus-background hypothesis $s+b$, in which a gluino contribution of strength $r \equiv \sigma/ \sigma_\mathrm{th}$ is included in addition to the background. The comparison between these hypotheses is based on the profile likelihood ratio test statistic, defined as follows:
\begin{equation}
    q_r = -2 \ln \frac{\mathcal{L}\left(\text{data} \,\middle|\, r s + b,\, \hat{\hat{\theta}}_r\right)}{\mathcal{L}\left(\text{data} \,\middle|\, \hat{r} s + b,\, \hat{\theta}\right)},
    \qquad 0 \leq \hat{r} \leq r\,,
    \label{eq:limits:qmu}
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ represents the set of nuisance parameters that encode systematic uncertainties. The parameters $\hat{r}$ and $\hat{\theta}$ globally maximise the likelihood, while $\hat{\hat{\theta}}_r$ maximises the likelihood for a fixed signal strength $r$. The distributions of $q_r$ under the two hypotheses determine the corresponding $p$-values: $\text{CL}_{s+b}$ is the probability, under the signal-plus-background hypothesis, of obtaining a value of $q_r$ at least as large as the observed value. Similarly, $\text{CL}_b$ is the probability computed under the background-only hypothesis. The quantity CL$_\text{s}$ is defined as the ratio of the $p$-values for the two hypotheses, $\textrm{CL$_\text{s}$} = \text{CL}_{s+b}/\text{CL}_b$. This construction prevents the exclusion of signal hypotheses that the analysis lacks sensitivity to. The $95\%$ confidence level upper limit on the cross section corresponds to the value of $r$ for which $\textrm{CL$_\text{s}$} = 0.05$.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

All limits in this section are computed using the asymptotic approximation~\cite{AsymptoticPaper}, where the distributions of $q_r$ under both hypotheses assume analytic forms. The median expected limit, along with its $\pm1\sigma$ and $\pm2\sigma$ bands, is derived from a single Asimov dataset\footnote{The Asimov dataset is an artificial construct in which each observable is assigned its expected value under the background-only hypothesis. This approach eliminates statistical fluctuations, enabling a single evaluation of the test statistic to reproduce the median expected result.}. This approach eliminates the need to generate pseudo-experiments. It reduces computation time to a few minutes per mass hypothesis, in contrast to several hours required for parallel toy generation in the full CL$_\text{s}$ construction. Limit extraction is performed using the \textsc{Combine} package developed by the CMS collaboration~\cite{CombinePaper}.


\subsection{Construction of the statistical models}
\label{sec:limits:models}

This study compares two statistical approaches to analyse the mass spectrum, both constructed from identical inputs: a counting experiment within a defined mass window, referred to as the \textit{cut-and-count method}, and a binned maximum-likelihood fit of the entire spectrum, known as \textit{shape analysis}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

In the cut-and-count method, a mass window is defined for each signal hypothesis as $[\mu_m - \sigma_m,\, \mu_m + 2\sigma_m]$, where $\mu_m$ and $\sigma_m$ represent the mean and standard deviation of the reconstructed mass distribution for a signal of mass $m$. The lower edge of the window is constrained to remain above $300$~GeV to avoid the region with the highest background. The asymmetry of the window relative to $\mu_m$ accounts for the steeply falling background: extending the window downward increases the background rate significantly more than the signal, while the upper side remains nearly free of background. Signal and background yields are calculated as the integrals of their respective spectra within the defined window. The statistical model is thereby reduced to a single-bin Poisson counting experiment. Systematic uncertainties are included as log-normal nuisance parameters, with scaling factors determined by integrating the systematically varied templates over the same window and normalising to the nominal yield. Two-sided variations are treated as asymmetric log-normal uncertainties, with separate down- and up-scaling factors, while a single multiplicative factor represents one-sided variations.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The shape analysis incorporates the full mass spectrum into the likelihood. Spectra are rebinned to the same adaptive mass binning as for the background estimation method. Each systematic uncertainty is represented by a pair of up and down templates, which \textsc{Combine} interpolates continuously; for one-sided uncertainties, the same template is symmetrised in both directions. Background normalisation is left unconstrained in the fit. Consequently, the limit uses the predicted background shape, while its normalisation is determined from the mass spectrum. Statistical uncertainties of the templates are incorporated on a bin-by-bin basis using the Barlow-Beeston lite method~\cite{Cranmer:1456844}. The systematic uncertainties, described in \cref{subsec:systSignal}, entering the models are summarised in \cref{tab:limits:systs}.

\subsection{Acceptance configurations}
\label{sec:limits:categories}

The extension of track acceptance from $|\eta|<1$ to $|\eta|<2.4$ necessitates accounting for the endcap region in the statistical model. Three eta regimes are evaluated: the first utilises only the barrel region ($|\eta|<1$), the second treats the full acceptance ($|\eta|<2.4$) as a single inclusive channel, and the third configuration incorporates the barrel and endcap ($1\leq|\eta|<2.4$) regions as two distinct channels. Signal templates, background predictions, and all systematic variations are generated independently for each regime, and the channels are combined in a unified likelihood. All nuisance parameters are correlated between the channels, reflecting their derivation from the same dataset, calibration, and prediction framework. The background rate parameters are the only exceptions, allowed to vary independently in each channel.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The procedure described in Section~\ref{sec:limits:cls} is applied independently to each gluino mass hypothesis ranging from $1100$ to $2600$~GeV and for both the cut-and-count and shape methods. In both scenarios, the likelihood is constructed as a product of Poisson terms across the analysis bins: the full rebinned spectrum for the shape analysis and a single bin for the cut-and-count method, with both the prediction $r s_i + b_i$ in each bin. This is further extended over the $\eta$ categories. Systematic sources are incorporated through their associated nuisance parameters. For a given trial value of $r$, two fits are performed: one with $r$ fixed and the nuisance parameters profiled, and another with all parameters free, resulting in the observed test statistic $q_r$. In the asymptotic regime, the test statistic takes the simple form
\begin{equation}
    q_r \simeq \frac{\left(r - \hat{r}\right)^2}{\zeta^2},
    \label{eq:limits:wald}
\end{equation}
where $\zeta$ denotes the standard deviation of the estimator $\hat{r}$ and serves as a measure of the analysis sensitivity. When \cref{eq:limits:wald} is evaluated using the background-only Asimov dataset, where $\hat{r} = 0$ by definition, it yields $\zeta^2 = r^2/q_{r,A}$, thereby determining the only parameter. Consequently, on an Asimov dataset, the two $p$-values can be expressed in closed form as
\begin{equation}
    \text{CL}_{s+b} = 1 - \Phi\!\left(\sqrt{q_r}\right)
    \qquad \mathrm{and} \qquad
    \text{CL}_{b} = \Phi\!\left(\frac{r}{\zeta} - \sqrt{q_r}\right),
    \label{eq:limits:pvalues}
\end{equation}
with $\Phi$ denoting the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the Normal distribution. The parameter $r$ is varied until $\text{CL}_\text{s} = 0.05$, which defines the upper limit $r_\text{up}$. The expected bands are derived from the same $\zeta$ using the following expression:
\begin{equation}
    r_\text{up}^{N} = \zeta \left[ \Phi^{-1}\!\left(1 - 0.05\cdot\Phi(N)\right) + N \right].
    \label{eq:limits:bands}
\end{equation}
Here, $N = 0$ corresponds to the median expected limit, while $N = \pm1$ and $N = \pm2$ define the associated bands. As the signal templates are normalised to the NNLO+NNLL production cross section, the upper limit on the cross section is given by $\sigma_\text{up} = r_\text{up} \times \sigma_\text{theo}$, with $\sigma_\text{theo}$ taken in Ref.~\cite{10.21468/SciPostPhysCore.7.4.072}. A mass hypothesis is excluded when $r_\text{up} < 1$.

In the inclusive channel, increased endcap background reduces the purity of the barrel region. \subsection{Expected discovery significance}
\label{sec:limits:significance}

In addition to the exclusion limits, the expected discovery significance is evaluated for each gluino mass hypothesis in the split configuration ($|\eta|<1$ and $1\leq|\eta|<2.4$), which offers the best sensitivity. The significance is calculated using the \textsc{Combine} \texttt{Significance} method applied to the Asimov dataset, with a nominal-strength signal injected ($r=1$). For each mass point, the profile likelihood ratio $q_0$ is calculated to test the background-only hypothesis against the best-fit signal-plus-background hypothesis. Under the asymptotic approximation, $q_0$ is then converted into a Gaussian expected median significance, $Z=\sqrt{q_0}$. This value represents the number of standard deviations by which the background-only hypothesis would be disfavoured. Thus, the expected significance serves as the counterpart to the expected limits: the exclusion reach indicates the extent to which a signal can be excluded, and the significance quantifies the strength with which a signal, if present, would be observed.

\cref{fig:limits:significance} shows the expected significance as a function of gluino mass. The significance declines with increasing mass, ranging from values well above the $5\sigma$ discovery threshold at low mass to approximately $3\sigma$ near $2600$~GeV. This trend directly reflects the signal and background yields reported in \cref{tab:expectedyield}. At low mass, the barrel channel yields a large signal with minimal background, resulting in a high nominal significance. At higher masses, the decreasing signal cross section reduces the expected yield to only a few events, making it increasingly difficult to distinguish a nominal-strength signal from the background-only hypothesis. The mass at which $Z=5\sigma$ lies close to the $2.4$~TeV exclusion reach obtained from the median expected limits, but this similarity does not reflect a physical equivalence: the two quantities are based on different hypotheses. The significance is evaluated using a signal-injected Asimov dataset ($r=1$) and measures the strength with which a gluino signal at its theoretical cross section would be observed. In contrast, the exclusion reach is determined under the background-only hypothesis ($r=0$), where the $95\%$ confidence level criterion corresponds to a one-sided significance of approximately $1.64\sigma$ (the CL$_\mathrm{s}$ criterion being more conservative), not $5\sigma$.

The interpretation of significance varies across the mass range. For masses below the exclusion reach of the previous search ($m_{\tilde{g}}^{\mathrm{excl.\, Run-2}}=2.08$~TeV), a signal at its nominal cross section has already been excluded. Consequently, the Asimov dataset used to compute $Z$ represents an unviable hypothesis, and the large significances observed at low mass reflect the selection's sensitivity rather than a realistic discovery potential. Only mass points beyond the Run--2 reach provide a physically meaningful discovery statement.

The previous search lacked both the sensitivity to exclude these masses and the yield required for an observation, whereas the present analysis retains a substantial discovery potential: the expected significance is $4.6\sigma$ at $2400$~GeV and remains near $3\sigma$ at $2600$~GeV. This follows directly from the increased signal yield documented in \cref{tab:gain-decomposition}, which is most pronounced in the high-mass region. Even beyond the exclusion reach of this analysis, a gluino R--hadron produced at its theoretical cross section would still be observed with a significance of several standard deviations, a statement that could not be made with the Run--2 dataset.

\subsection{Nuisance parameter impacts}
\label{sec:limits:impacts}

Exclusion limits are influenced by systematic uncertainties solely through nuisance parameters, making it unclear from the limits alone which sources are most significant. The impact of a nuisance parameter $\theta$ on the signal strength $r$ is defined as the shift $\Delta\hat{r}$ observed when $\theta$ is fixed at its post-fit value displaced by $\pm 1$ post-fit standard deviation, with all other parameters profiled. This impact quantifies the extent to which uncertainty in $\hat{r}$ is propagated through a given parameter, rather than reflecting the magnitude of that uncertainty.

The calculation is done using the \texttt{MultiDimFit} method within \textsc{Combine}. An initial fit determines $\hat{r}$ along with the post-fit values and uncertainties for all parameters. Afterwards, a separate likelihood scan is performed for each nuisance parameter, and the results are ranked in decreasing order of $|\Delta\hat{r}|$. The analysis is conducted using the split configuration in the shape analysis and is based on an Asimov dataset with the signal set at its nominal cross section.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\cref{fig:limits:impacts} presents the ranking for four gluino mass hypotheses. Each figure consists of two panels. The left panel displays, for each parameter, the quantity $(\hat{\theta}-\theta_{0})/\sigma_{0}$, where $\hat{\theta}$ and $\theta_{0}$ denote the post-fit and pre-fit values, and $\sigma_{0}$ is the pre-fit uncertainty. The horizontal bar attached to each point represents the ratio of the post-fit to pre-fit uncertainty. A bar shorter than $\pm 1$ indicates that the data constrain the parameter beyond its external prior. Blue crosses indicate the pull $(\hat{\theta}-\theta_{0})/\sqrt{\sigma_{0}^{2}-\sigma^{2}}$, with $\sigma$ as the post-fit uncertainty. The right panel illustrates the impact, with red and blue bands corresponding to $+1\sigma$ and $-1\sigma$ displacements of the parameter. The position of each band indicates whether the parameter is correlated or anti-correlated with the signal strength. The two lowest entries correspond to free background normalisations without prior constraints, for which the fitted value is shown instead of a pull. The value in the upper-right corner indicates the fitted signal strength and its total uncertainty.

Because the fit is performed on an Asimov dataset, all constrained parameters remain at their pre-fit values, resulting in no observed pull, as anticipated. Consequently, the primary information in the left panel is the length of the error bars. Most parameters exhibit post-fit uncertainties consistent with their priors. However, the $1/p$ template binning, the $I_h$ template binning, and the $I_h$ correlation parameters show reduced uncertainties below unity.

The trigger SFs, pile-up reweighting, luminosity, and $F_\textrm{pixel}$ systematic uncertainties directly affect the signal normalisation: their effect on $\hat{r}$ is nearly a one-to-one translation. However, the impact is greater for the trigger SFs because their uncertainties remain relatively high (approximately $4\%$ on average, as shown in \cref{fig:triggerSF} left). On the other hand, adjusting the binning of templates (in $\eta$, $1/p$, and $I_h$) used in the background prediction alters the shape, and the relative magnitude of this effect increases with the signal mass. The \dedx{} calibration constants $K_\textrm{mass}$ and $C_\textrm{mass}$, template-fit variations, $I_h$ binning, and jet energy scale contribute poorly.

Because these results are derived from an Asimov dataset, the fitted values of the free background normalisations (rate) are unity by construction and do not convey additional information. Therefore, only their uncertainties are relevant. For observed data, these unconstrained parameters will be determined by the low-mass bulk and will quantify the ratio of observed to predicted yield in that region. Agreement with unity could validate the absolute normalisation of the background prediction, whereas a significant deviation would indicate a normalisation bias in the method rather than the presence of a signal, since a uniform rescaling cannot reproduce an excess that is localised in the high-mass tail. Allowing these parameters to remain free ensures that the extracted limits are sensitive only to the shape of the predicted spectrum and not to its overall normalisation.

Overall, the largest individual impact is approximately $0.03$ at $2000$~GeV and $0.05$ at $2600$~GeV, while the total uncertainty on $\hat{r}$ is $^{+0.28}_{-0.24}$ and $^{+1.4}_{-0.7}$, respectively. For this lightest mass hypothesis, the signal at nominal strength cannot be distinguished from the background-only hypothesis because the uncertainty becomes comparable to the injected signal strength. This uncertainty should correspond to the quadratic sum of the $\Delta \hat{r}$ values for each nuisance parameter, combined with the statistical uncertainty, under the assumption that all components are uncorrelated (discussed in \cref{sec:limits:correlations}). The ordering of parameters changes only gradually across the four mass points, indicating that the treatment of systematic uncertainties is stable over the considered range. In both scenarios, systematic sources contribute only a small fraction of the total uncertainty, and sensitivity is primarily limited by the number of high-mass events rather than by systematic uncertainties.

\subsection{Correlations between nuisance parameters}
\label{sec:limits:correlations}

The impacts quantify how each parameter shifts the signal strength, but do not indicate whether two parameters represent the same underlying effect. This relationship is characterised by the post-fit correlation matrix, which is obtained by inverting the Hessian of the likelihood at its minimum\footnote{The Hessian is the matrix of second partial derivatives of the negative log-likelihood with respect to the fitted parameters, evaluated at the minimum, and describes the local curvature of the likelihood. Its inverse is the covariance matrix of the estimators.}. \cref{fig:limits:correlation} presents this matrix for $m_{\tilde{g}}=2200$~GeV in the split configuration, evaluated using an Asimov dataset with a nominal-strength injected signal ($r=1$). All nuisance parameters are independent before the fit. Therefore, any observed correlation arises from the data constraining multiple parameters within the same mass-spectrum bins.

No correlation with the signal strength exceeds $0.18$ in magnitude. At this mass, the expected signal yield is small, and the uncertainty on $r$ is dominated by its statistical component, thereby reducing the influence of systematic parameters in determining $r$. The two background normalisations exhibit a correlation of $3\times10^{-3}$, meaning that each channel constrains its own normalisation and the two regimes (barrel and endcap) can be treated as non-correlated. Comparable results are obtained for other signal mass hypotheses (not shown here).

\section{Perspectives}
\label{sec:perspectives}

The analysis presented in this chapter has reached a mature stage: the trigger strategy, pre-selection, data-driven background estimation, and statistical framework are in place, and the expected gluino mass reach of $2.42$~TeV exceeds the previous CMS exclusion by more than $300$~GeV~\cite{HSCP_Run2}. Several directions remain open, ranging from the finalisation of the present search to its long-term evolution at the HL--LHC. They are discussed below in order of increasing time scale.


\subsection{Towards unblinding}

The first objective is to address the residual population of high-mass events observed in the validation region (\cref{fig:abcd_mass_slices_data} and \cref{fig:abcd_mass_mc}). Candidates passing the pre-selection criteria and reconstructed at high mass in the $t\overline{t}\to\ell\nu$ and $t\overline{t}\to2\ell2\nu$ MC samples, which are the two MC samples with the highest statistics, are currently being characterised. If a common feature is identified, an updated or a new selection criterion may be incorporated into the pre-selection. As the sensitivity of the search in the barrel is already constrained by the signal yield rather than the background, such a criterion would primarily benefit the endcap channel, where the signal-to-background ratio is lower, and would enhance the robustness of the background prediction in the region where the limits are determined.

A subsequent step involves refining the statistical treatment. All limits presented in \cref{sec:limits} are based on the asymptotic approximation, which tends to be optimistic when expected event counts are low, as observed in the barrel channel at high mass. After unblinding, the final results should be calculated using the full CL$_\text{s}$ construction with pseudo-experiments, as the low-background expected yields in the barrel region show values below unity, where the Asymptotic CL$_\text{s}$ treatment is less reliable. In the previous iteration of the analysis, the comparison between the asymptotic and full constructions revealed differences of several tens of GeV in the excluded mass range. This comparison should be repeated for the current configuration.

Following unblinding, the observed exclusion limit on the gluino mass should be compared to the result reported by the ATLAS collaboration~\cite{HSCP_ATLAS_2025} based on Run-2 data. Careful interpretation is necessary because the two analysis strategies differ significantly. The search presented in this thesis utilises MET triggers in combination with ionisation measurements from the tracker, whereas the ATLAS analysis employs ionisation measurements from the pixel detector and incorporates time-of-flight data from the calorimeter. The signal models also differ: the present analysis assumes a fully stable gluino, whereas the ATLAS interpretation allows the later to decay into a quark pair and a neutralino $\tilde{{\chi}}_1^0$. However, this distinction is negligible in the long-lifetime regime, where decays within the detector volume are rare, and the signal topology effectively corresponds to a stable gluino. In this regime, the mass limit becomes insensitive to the values of $m_{\tilde{\chi}_1^0}$ and the mass splitting $\Delta m$ with the gluino. Therefore, a comparison is justified: the expected limit obtained in this analysis, $2.42$~TeV, exceeds the $2.27$~TeV reported by ATLAS for large $\tau(\tilde{g})$ (see the bottom plots of \cref{fig:massexclu_atlas_run2}).


\subsection{Expected gains from additional luminosity}

Analysis of nuisance parameter impacts (\cref{sec:limits:impacts}) indicates that the sensitivity of this search is constrained primarily by the number of expected signal events, rather than by systematic uncertainties. Consequently, the most significant improvement arises from increased integrated luminosity, and the associated gain in mass reach can be estimated using a straightforward scaling argument.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The exclusion sensitivity is dominated by the barrel channel, which is effectively background-free (\cref{tab:expectedyield}). In this regime, the counting experiment can be modelled as a Poisson process. Thus the expected signal yield $N_s$ is expressed as: $N_s = \sigma\,\varepsilon\,\mathcal{L}$, where $\varepsilon$ denotes the signal efficiency. In addition, in the exclusion-relevant region, the theoretical cross section $\sigma^{\rm th}(m)$ is locally well approximated by an exponential function: $\sigma^{\rm th}(m) \approx \sigma_0\, e^{-m/m_0}$. The slope parameter is determined from \cref{tab:signal_samples}: between $2200$ and $2600$~GeV, the cross section decreases by a factor of approximately $5.83$:
\begin{equation}
    5.83 = \frac{\sigma^{\rm th}(2200)}{\sigma^{\rm th}(2600)} = \exp\left(\frac{ 400}{m_0} \right) \quad  \Rightarrow \quad m_0 = \frac{400~\textrm{GeV}}{\ln(5.83)} \approx 227~\textrm{GeV} \, ,    
\end{equation}
This result indicates that the cross section decreases by a factor of $e$ for every approximately $227$~GeV increase in mass.

The excluded mass $m_{\rm excl}$ is defined by the intersection of the theoretical and experimental cross section curves. At this point, the expected signal yield is: $N_s^{95\%}=\sigma^{\rm th}(m_{\rm excl})\,\varepsilon\,\mathcal{L}$. Expressing this condition for two luminosities $\mathcal{L}$ and $\mathcal{L}'$ results in:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    &N_s^{95\%}=\sigma^{\rm th}(m_{\rm excl})\,\varepsilon\,\mathcal{L} = \sigma^{\rm th}(m_{\rm excl}')\,\varepsilon\,\mathcal{L}' \\
    \Leftrightarrow \quad & \frac{\mathcal{L}'}{\mathcal{L}} = \exp\left( \frac{m_{\rm excl}'-m_{\rm excl}}{m_0} \right) \\
    \Leftrightarrow \quad & \Delta m_{\tilde{g}} = m_{\rm excl}'-m_{\rm excl} = m_0 \ln\frac{\mathcal{L}'}{\mathcal{L}} \approx 227~\textrm{GeV} \times \ln\frac{\mathcal{L}'}{\mathcal{L}} \, .
    \label{eq:persp:deltam}
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Extending the analysis from the 2024 dataset ($109$~fb$^{-1}$) to the full Run--3 dataset ($310$~fb$^{-1}$), would increase the expected mass reach by about $\Delta m_{\tilde{g}}\approx240$~GeV, bringing it to $2600$--$2700$~GeV.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

This estimate is indicative and relies on several assumptions: the signal efficiency is considered mass-independent (consistent with the flat acceptance observed in \cref{fig:triggerMassAcceptance}) and is expected to remain constant throughout the entire Run--3 dataset, as is the signal yield.

A statistical combination with the Run--2 dataset would yield a further improvement. This gain is constrained by the lower centre-of-mass energy and the muon-based trigger strategy employed in the earlier data.


\subsection{Extending the interpretations and the trigger coverage}

This search is interpreted exclusively in terms of gluino R-hadrons. The same experimental signature, selection criteria, and background prediction are directly applicable to R-hadrons formed from pair-produced top squarks. As a colour triplet, the stop is produced with a cross section approximately two orders of magnitude lower than that of the gluino at equal mass (\cref{fig:xsec_theory}), and the previous CMS search excluded stop masses below $1.47$~TeV~\cite{HSCP_Run2}. Reinterpreting this analysis for the stop hypothesis requires only processing the corresponding signal samples, with the full analysis chain remaining unchanged. Further interpretations for finite lifetimes and charge-suppression scenarios, in which charge flipping of R-hadrons occurs exclusively from the charged to the neutral state, would also broaden the phase-space coverage.

The trigger strategy may also be expanded. The MET triggers employed in this analysis are optimal for R-hadrons. The use of MET-based selections has the advantage of recovering slow-moving R-hadrons, which are often excluded from muon triggers. However, MET triggers are not optimal for lepton-like HSCPs such as tau sleptons, for which muon triggers retain $\approx70\%$ of the signal~\cite{HSCP_Run2}. Run--2 studies demonstrated that a logical OR of the muon and MET paths increases the event selection efficiency to approximately $\approx45\%$ for gluino R-hadrons and $\approx85\%$ for tau sleptons, compared to $\approx30\%$ and $\approx15\%$ respectively with MET triggers alone~\cite{haebe2024}. This combined approach could provide a single analytical framework for targeting both hadron-like and lepton-like candidates. However, it requires handling two partially overlapping datasets (muon and MET) and extending trigger-efficiency and scale-factor measurements to the muon paths. Since sensitivity is statistically limited, recovering otherwise lost signal topologies represents one of the most significant advancements in the search.


\subsection{The HSCP search at the HL--LHC}

Over a longer timescale, the Phase--2 upgrade of CMS will significantly alter the experimental inputs for this search. As detailed in \cref{sec:HLLHC}, the outer tracker will feature a binary readout and will no longer provide charge measurements. Consequently, the \dedx{} information will be limited to the upgraded inner tracker, reducing the average number of ionisation measurements per track from approximately 15 to a maximum of 6. Two new features will partially offset this reduction. First, the PS modules of the outer tracker will implement a programmable threshold, optimised at 1.4 times the MIP level, to flag highly ionising hits and provide a coarse \dedx{} discriminator usable up to the L1 trigger. Second, the MIP Timing Detector, positioned between the tracker and the calorimeters, will measure the time-of-flight of charged particles with a resolution of 30--60~ps, enabling direct determination of $\beta$ at a distance of approximately one metre from the interaction point. Timing is anticipated to become the primary signature for slow particles at the HL--LHC~\cite{CMS:2667167, CMS-DP-2022-025}. It will also enable interpretations of particle lifetime on the order of a few nanoseconds, which are not accessible with the current strategy. Together with the extended tracker acceptance and the $3000$~fb$^{-1}$ expected for the Phase--2 programme, these upgrades will refocus the HSCP search on timing, with ionisation serving a supporting function.

This development underscores the significance of the Run--3 programme. The current analysis utilises a precise, full-tracker \dedx{} measurement, which will not be available in this format following the Phase--2 upgrade. Completing the search using the full Run--3 dataset, along with the aforementioned improvements, therefore constitutes both the culmination of the ionisation-based strategy employed by CMS since Run--1 and the most sensitive investigation of the R-hadron hypothesis achievable before the implementation of timing-based methods of the Phase--2 programme~\cite{CMS-DP-2022-025}.
