\section{Analysis strategy}

This analysis focuses on the search for heavy stable charged particles (HSCPs), specifically R-hadrons produced via gluino pair production. The experimental signature is characterised by a high-momentum, highly ionising track accompanied by missing transverse energy, which originates from ISR jets or from charge flipping during nuclear interactions within the detector. Unlike the previous analysis based on the Muon dataset~\cite{HSCP_Run2}, this search utilises the MET-triggered dataset. R-hadrons are frequently not reconstructed as muons, either due to insufficient velocity or because they become neutral before reaching the muon system. The use of MET trigger paths therefore recovers a significant portion of the signal that would be missed by a muon-based selection. Multiple MET HLT paths are combined, and the tracker acceptance is extended from $|\eta| < 1$ to $|\eta| < 2.4$, relative to the previous analysis.

A pre-selection step requires events to pass MET triggers, in addition to track-level criteria, the full list of which is described in \cref{sec:HSCPpresel}. The candidate mass is reconstructed using the track momentum $p$ and the harmonic mean $I_h$ of its $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x$ deposits in the strip tracker. A Bethe-Bloch parametrisation $I_h = f(\beta\gamma=p/m)$ is then used to compute the mass. Heavy particles are expected to manifest as an excess at large reconstructed mass, while Standard Model tracks predominantly populate lower mass values.

The background is estimated directly from data. The phase space is divided according to transverse momentum and the pixel-based discriminant $F_\text{pixel}$, which is uniformly distributed for Standard Model tracks, peaks at unity for signal, and is uncorrelated with $p_T$. Both high $p_T$ and high $F_\text{pixel}$ correspond to the signal region. The region characterised by high $p_T$ and low $F_\text{pixel}$, where signal contamination is negligible, serves as the control region. Background normalisation in the signal region is determined using an ABCD method, while the mass shape is predicted by convolving, for each pseudo-rapidity slice, the $1/p$ and $I_h$ templates extracted from the control region. The method is validated in an intermediate value of $F_\text{pixel}$, which is kept separate from the blinded signal region. Systematic uncertainties are propagated to the predicted mass spectrum. The comparison between observed and predicted spectra in the signal region is then used to derive exclusion limits on the HSCP production cross section as a function of mass.


\section{Datasets}

The samples utilised in this analysis are summarised in \cref{tab:datalist} (data), \cref{tab:signal_samples} (signal) and \cref{tab:mc_samples_2024} (MCs).

The data were collected in 2024 at a centre-of-mass energy of $\sqrt{s}=13.6$~TeV and are divided into seven eras (C through I), as detailed in \cref{tab:datalist}. The analysis considers events that pass a MET trigger and are stored in the MET dataset. The total integrated luminosity is 109.95\,fb$^{-1}$.

The signal samples comprise pair-produced gluinos that hadronise into R-hadrons, generated using \textsc{MadGraph} at $\sqrt{s}=13.6$\,TeV for ten mass hypotheses between 1.1 and 2.6\,TeV, as detailed in ~\cref{tab:signal_samples}. The theoretical production cross section, computed at NNLO\textsubscript{approx}+NNLL~\cite{10.21468/SciPostPhysCore.7.4.072, Fiaschi_2023}, decreases as the gluino mass increases. This mass range surpasses the exclusion limits established by previous searches ($m_{\tilde{g}}^\textrm{lim}=2.08$~TeV)~\cite{HSCP_Run2}.

The main background primarily consists of high-$p_T$ muons produced in addition to MET. The dominant processes are top-quark pair production in the dileptonic channel, $t\bar{t}\to 2\ell 2\nu$, and $W$ boson production in association with jets in the muonic decay channel, $W(\to\mu\nu)+\text{jets}$. Additionally, QCD multijet production is modelled using twelve exclusive slices of muon-enriched samples spanning $p_T$ from 15~GeV to above 1~TeV. For each sample, \cref{tab:mc_samples_2024} provides the production cross section and its associated uncertainty.

\section{Analysis tools}

\subsection{d\textit{E}/d\textit{x} discriminator $F_{\text{pixel}}$}

In this analysis, the pixel information is exploited via $F_{\text{pixel}}$, which quantifies the compatibility of a track with a MIP deposit. The pixel cluster shape and the charge distribution of MIPs are modelled from \texttt{PixelAV} simulations~\cite{1495807}, which reproduce charge deposition, transport, and collection processes in pixel sensors. 
These simulations are periodically tuned to data using large-$\eta$ tracks to provide angle-dependent models that yield different Vavilov function $V(Q)$ templates to model the charge distribution of MIPs.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

For a given pixel cluster carrying a charge $Q_{\text{cluster}}$, we define the tail probability 
\begin{equation}
p_Q = \int_{Q_{\text{cluster}}}^{+\infty} V(Q)\,\mathrm{d}Q,
\end{equation}
which quantifies the probability of depositing a charge greater than $Q_{\text{cluster}}$ for a MIP (see~\cref{fig:clustQ}). By virtue of the Probability Integral Transform, if $Q_{\text{cluster}} \sim V(Q)$ then $p_Q \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$. In other words, provided that the particle is a MIP and that the model $V(Q)$ is accurate, $p_Q$ is uniformly distributed by construction.

To complete the description of the four layers of the pixel subdetector, a joint coefficient is built using the different tail probabilities $p_{Q_i}$ associated with the charge deposits $Q_{\text{cluster}_i}$ in each pixel layer:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_n = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_{Q_i},
\end{equation}

The quantity $\alpha_n$ represents the joint probability of simultaneously observing charges at least as large in all layers: if a single layer is strongly ionising, $\alpha_n$ decreases; if all layers are, $\alpha_n$ becomes significantly smaller.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.8\textwidth]{Images/Ch4/vavilov_charge.pdf}
    \caption{Example of charge deposits in pixel sensors at $\eta=2.40$, with the distribution fitted using a Vavilov function. The tail distribution $p_Q$ is shown in filled grey.}
    \label{fig:clustQ}
\end{figure}

A difficulty arises because $\alpha_n$ is not uniformly distributed, even for a MIP. Its distribution depends strongly on $n$, which prevents a meaningful comparison between, for instance, the $n=3$ and $n=4$ cases. A different variable must therefore be constructed to obtain a behaviour subject to universal selection.

To do so, the variable $-\ln(\alpha_n)$ is taken as it follows a Gamma distribution with parameters $(n,1)$, if and only if each $p_i$ is uniformly distributed on $[0,1]$. The corresponding probability density function is then
\begin{equation}
\Gamma(n,1) = \frac{x^{\,n-1} e^{-x}}{(n-1)!}, \qquad x = -\ln(\alpha_n).
\end{equation}

The associated cumulative distribution function can be expressed analytically as
\begin{equation}
\beta_n = \alpha_n \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} \frac{\left[-\ln(\alpha_n)\right]^m}{m!}.
\end{equation}
The variable $\beta_n$ thus corresponds to the cumulative probability (CDF) associated with $\alpha_n$ under the hypothesis that the particle is a MIP.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Since $\beta_n$ is the exact cumulative distribution function of $\alpha_n$ under the MIP hypothesis, the Probability Integral Transform guarantees that $\beta_n \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$ for MIPs, and the $n$-dependence that affected the raw coefficient $\alpha_n$ is fully absorbed into its CDF. This restores the uniformity required for a universal selection. We therefore define
\begin{equation}
    F_{\text{pixel}} = 1 - \beta_n.
\end{equation}

By construction, $F_{\text{pixel}}$ is uniformly distributed on $[0,1]$ for MIPs, regardless of the number of pixel layers considered. For strongly ionising particles, however, such as gluinos, the charge deposited in each layer exceeds that expected for a MIP. This leads to small $p_i$ values, hence to a small $\alpha_n$ and a $\beta_n$ close to zero, so that $F_{\text{pixel}}$ approaches unity. The variable $F_{\text{pixel}}$ therefore constitutes an effective discriminator between Standard Model and BSM processes.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

In this analysis, hits from the first layer of the pixel detector were excluded from the computation of $F_{\text{pixel}}$ due to premature ageing caused by high radiation levels in this layer of the Phase-1 detector. The left panel of~\cref{fig:FpixelInSignalData} shows the $F_{\text{pixel}}$ distribution for data collected in 2024 within a control region where all analysis preselection criteria are applied, but with $p_T < 55$ GeV to avoid signal contamination. The right panel displays the distribution for a simulated HSCP gluino at $m=2000$~GeV after the analysis preselection criteria. The distribution derived from the data is relatively flat, while most of the signal is concentrated around unity.

Assuming the data distribution is perfectly flat when it is not introduces systematic uncertainties. These uncertainties were estimated by varying the forenamed probability models and will be discussed in~\cref{sec:fi_syst}.

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{$I_h$}{Ih} estimator}

The search for HSCPs relies on tracking anomalous d$E$/d$x$ deposits within detector materials. The average energy deposit per track across the silicon tracker layers is quantified using the $I_h$ estimator, as detailed in \cref{sec:ihpres}. Owing to their greater mass, HSCPs generate higher energy deposits than MIPs produced by SM processes. MIPs exhibit nearly constant ionisation, resulting in low $I_h$ values for SM tracks, whereas HSCPs deposit substantially more charge per unit length and occupy the high-$I_h$ region. Therefore, $I_h$ enables effective discrimination between SM processes and HSCPs. This approach is complementary to the pixel-based estimator $F_{\text{pixel}}$, which utilises the shape of the charge distribution in the innermost layers, whereas $I_h$ aggregates ionisation measurements across the strip tracker.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\cref{fig:IhSignalData} demonstrates this separation. The upper panel presents the normalised $I_h$ distribution after the analysis preselection criteria. The data distribution (left) exhibits a sharp peak at low ionisation, approximately $3.2$~MeV/cm, consistent with the MIP expectation, and decreases rapidly at higher values. In contrast, a gluino with $m=2000$~GeV (right) gives rise to higher $\langle\dedx\rangle$ values. The lower panel shows the corresponding cumulative distribution functions: the data CDF rises to unity almost immediately, while the gluino CDF increases gradually, highlighting the strong separation between the two populations across most of the $I_h$ range.

\subsection{Stability of d\textit{E}/d\textit{x} variables over the year}

The stability of the two ionisation observables used in this analysis is monitored throughout the 2024 data-taking period by computing, for each run, the mean values of $I_h$ and $F_\text{pixel}$ for tracks without selections and for those that pass the analysis pre-selection. The evolution of $\langle I_h \rangle$ as a function of run number is presented in \cref{fig:dedx_stability} (top left), along with the distribution of the per-run means (top right). Following the application of the charge calibration described in \cref{app:calibration}, the strip-based estimator remains stable over the year. The per-run means are centred around $\langle I_h \rangle \approx 3.37~(3.32)$~MeV/cm, with a spread at the percent level for tracks that do or do not pass the analysis pre-selection step.

A similar monitoring procedure is applied to the pixel-based discriminant (bottom row). The per-run mean of $F_\text{pixel}$ is centred around 0.65 (0.61) for tracks that do or do not pass the analysis pre-selection step, with a relative dispersion of a few percent. Because $F_\text{pixel}$ is constructed from the pixel charge templates, the slight decrease observed over the year reflects the progressive degradation of the pixel sensors due to irradiation, which reduces the collected charge. However, this drift remains modest compared to the width of the $F_\text{pixel}$ distribution, allowing the variable to be used consistently throughout the 2024 dataset.

These checks confirm that both ionisation variables can be used consistently over the full 2024 dataset without any time-dependent correction beyond the charge calibration already applied.

\subsection{Trigger event selection}

The Run~3 HSCP analysis targets primarily hadron-like candidates, for which the $dE/dx$-based single-muon trigger strategy adopted in Run~2 is not optimal. R-hadrons are pair-produced with a charged and a neutral component in roughly half of the events, and the resulting momentum imbalance, together with initial-state radiations (ISRs), gives rise to missing transverse energy. A dedicated MET trigger strategy was therefore developed, combining four HLT paths:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{HLT\_PFMET120\_PFMHT120\_IDTight},
    \item \texttt{HLT\_PFMETNoMu120\_PFMHTNoMu120\_IDTight\_PFHT60},
    \item \texttt{HLT\_PFHT500\_PFMET100\_PFMHT100\_IDTight},
    \item \texttt{HLT\_MET105\_IsoTrk50}.
\end{itemize}
The naming convention of each HLT path directly encodes the offline-like cuts applied at that stage: the algorithm prefix (\texttt{PFMET}, \texttt{PFMHT}, \texttt{PFHT}, \texttt{CaloMET}, \texttt{IsoTrk}) identifies the reconstructed quantity being thresholded, while the number gives the corresponding $p_T$ (or $H_T$) cut in GeV. \texttt{IDTight} signifies that tight jet identification is applied to the jets entering the MHT sum, \texttt{NoMu} indicates that muon contributions are subtracted from the MET/MHT computation, and \texttt{IsoTrk50} requires an isolated track with $p_T>50$~GeV. 

At the L1 trigger level, the seed lists are a logical OR of several \texttt{L1\_ETMHF} thresholds (MET computing using the central and forward calorimeters). Since \texttt{ETMHF} is a calorimeter-based quantity, the initial L1 decision is essentially always driven by the Calo MET, with the PF MET/MHT only becoming the discriminating variable at the HLT stage.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Particle-flow (PF) MET is only produced when the R-hadron pair consists of one charged and one neutral state, since a charged-charged or neutral-neutral pair leaves no net PF imbalance. On the other hand, calorimetric MET (Calo MET) still arise from an ISR jet or photon recoiling against the pair. Five kinematic topologies, depending on the nature of the pair-produced HSCP, were identified on this basis and are illustrated in \cref{fig:METtopology}, ranging from configurations with negligible MET to those dominated by either PF or Calo MET, thereby motivating the use of several complementary MET observables rather than a single variable.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Beyond the pair-type composition itself, the way each HSCP track is reconstructed by the PF algorithm also matters for the trigger strategy. \cref{fig:pftypeprop} (Left) shows, for each gluino mass point, the fraction of PF candidates matched to a generated charged HSCP track that are identified either as a PF muon or as PF charged pions (these are the only two PF candidates observed), before and after the OR of the four MET triggers. Because the highly-ionising, slow-moving HSCP track can mimic a minimum-ionising muon signature going through the whole calorimeters, a large fraction of true charged HSCPs are reconstructed as PF muons rather than as charged pions. 

Rather than being primarily driven by this muon mis-identification, the efficiency of the individual MET triggers (\cref{fig:pftypeprop} Right, all below $\sim$30\%) should instead be read in light of the pair-type composition itself: since PF MET is only produced for charged--neutral pairs and Calo MET only for configurations with significant ISR (\cref{fig:METtopology}), a significant fraction of signal events carries little to no MET regardless of trigger thresholds. This connection between pair-type composition and trigger efficiency is examined quantitatively in~\cref{fig:pairtypestages}.

\cref{fig:pairtypestages} follows the HSCP pair-type distribution through the analysis chain for a representative signal point ($m_{\tilde{g}}=2000$~GeV), comparing the generated composition to that obtained after the OR of the four MET triggers.

The reduction from the generated composition (black dots, summing to unity) to the post-trigger composition (red squares) is essentially uniform across the three pair-type categories, and simply reflects the overall OR-trigger efficiency already measured in \cref{fig:pftypeprop} (Right plot, bin~5): summing the red squares over the three categories recovers the same $\sim$30\% efficiency obtained for the $m_{\tilde{g}}=2000$~GeV point, confirming that the OR trigger is not preferentially selecting a given pair type at this stage, but rather applies an approximately flat acceptance across all three configurations.

Among triggered events, the fractions instead change significantly depending on the number of HSCP candidates matched with a track: neutral--neutral events populate almost exclusively the 0-matched category, since the neutral R-hadron leaves no track, while charged--charged events dominate the 2-matched category and charged--neutral events mainly populate the 1-matched category.

This correspondence can be quantified further. At generator level, essentially all signal events contain two generated HSCPs, distributed among the charged--charged, charged--neutral, and neutral--neutral pair types with respective fractions of about 20\%, 50\%, and 30\% (black dots in~\cref{fig:pairtypestages}). After requiring a track match between the generated HSCPs and the reconstructed tracks, the observed 0-, 1-, and 2-matched-track fractions are instead 41\%, 46\%, and 14\%, respectively. While the 1-matched-track fraction closely follows the generated charged--neutral fraction, the 2-matched-track fraction falls below the generated charged--charged fraction (14\% vs 20\%), and the 0-matched-track fraction correspondingly exceeds the generated neutral--neutral fraction (41\% vs 30\%). This shift towards lower matched-track multiplicities indicates that HSCPs are predominantly lost through charge flipping of a charged R-hadron to a neutral state.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The selected MET triggers are determined by the Calo MET and PF MET at the L1 level, and the efficiency of selecting offline events using these triggers can be assessed as a function of these two variables. This trigger-efficiency procedure must be carefully performed on both simulation and data. Near the turn-on region, the efficiency depends on the thresholded observable, and this dependence is governed by the MET resolution, which is not perfectly modelled in simulation. As a result, data and simulation diverge in this region, and the signal efficiency cannot be considered reliable across the whole Calo MET or PF MET range. Beyond the turn-on, in the \textit{trigger plateau}, the efficiency saturates and becomes flat. In this regime, the ratio of the trigger efficiency in data to that in simulation, expressed as a function of one of the MET variables and known as the \textit{scale factor}, is close to unity. The trigger response should therefore be studied on the plateau rather than integrated over the full turn-on, since the signal acceptance used in the final result is taken from simulation and its trigger contribution is reliable only if the simulated trigger accurately reproduces the one observed in data.

This requirement is especially critical for the HSCP analysis, where, if no excess is observed, limits on the theoretical model are computed using a shape analysis. A flat plateau efficiency multiplies the mass spectrum by a constant, affecting only its overall normalisation and leaving both the signal shape and the data-driven background prediction unchanged. However, without scale-factor corrections and a selection applied to the plateau, the signal's accuracy near the turn-on point is uncertain, leading to uncertainty in counting signal events and, consequently, to unreliable exclusion limits. 

The adopted strategy follows directly from these considerations. The turn-on curves are first measured in parallel in data and simulation as functions of the offline PUppi MET (the recommended MET estimator for Run--3 physics analyses, instead of PF MET) and Calo MET, and the corresponding data-to-simulation scale factors are extracted. The offline thresholds are then set sufficiently high to place the analysis on the plateau, where these scale factors are flat and well controlled.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

As the HSCP analysis moved from an \textsc{AOD} to a \textsc{MiniAOD} framework for analysing Run--3 data, some variables have changed. This is the case for the Calo MET, where muon energy deposits within the calorimeters are included in its computation, thereby biasing its value. Since trigger efficiency is driven by Calo MET at the L1 level, to recover the MET deposits within the calo towers, one could build a proxy observable that mimics the Run--2 Calo MET. Therefore, a pseudo-MET variable is reconstructed offline from the calorimeter jet (CaloJets) collection as the magnitude of the vectorial transverse-momentum sum of all calorimeter jets in the event,
\begin{equation}
    E_{T}^{\text{pseudo}} = \sqrt{\left\|\sum_{\text{CaloJets}} \overrightarrow{p_{x_j}}\right\|^2 + \left\|\sum_{\text{CaloJets}} \overrightarrow{p_{y_j}} \right\|^2 } .
\end{equation}
The Pseudo MET was validated on Run~2 AOD samples by comparison with the Calo MET, which showed a similar distribution after the analysis pre-selection step. Since the Pseudo MET is constructed directly from the raw CaloJet collection, no offset subtraction, response correction, or residual scale factor is applied to replicate the corrections provided by the CMS Jet Energy Resolution and Corrections (JERC) group. Consequently, this alternative MET variable includes uncorrected jet energy contributions, and its absolute scale does not match that of the Calo MET. This does not constitute a limitation, as the Pseudo MET is used exclusively to identify the trigger plateau and extract the corresponding scale factors, both of which depend on the shape of the turn-on rather than on the absolute energy calibration. The same definition is applied consistently to both data and simulation, ensuring that any residual miscalibration cancels in the data/MC ratio.

The efficiency of the combined (logical-OR) MET trigger was evaluated as a function of the Pseudo MET and the PUppi MET, which drive the online HLT triggers.

In addition to the trigger, requirements on the value of $H_{T}$, MHT, or MHTNoMu are asked as these variable drive the trigger value online (e.g. $H_{T}>500$~GeV and $\text{MHT}>100$~GeV for \texttt{HLT\_PFHT500\_PFMET100\_PFMHT100\_IDTight}, or $\text{MHT}^{\text{NoMu}}>120$~GeV and $H_{T}>60$~GeV for the \texttt{NoMu} path), so as to exclude configurations where the Pseudo MET is populated by mismeasured or ill-defined jet activity. Those three variables are computed offline using the jet collection with the recommended Run--3 CMS jet corrections~\cite{CMSJetID13p6TeV}: the jet ID\footnote{A set of quality requirements on the energy fractions and constituent multiplicities of a jet, designed to reject jets originating from detector noise while keeping a nearly uniform efficiency for genuine jets.} and the jet veto map\footnote{A map of $(\eta,\phi)$ regions where the jet response is anomalous, due to dead, noisy or miscalibrated calorimeter channels. Events containing a jet pointing to such a region are discarded to avoid a mismeasurement of the jet energy and of the MET.}.

Signal trigger efficiency is defined as the ratio of events that pass both the analysis pre-selection and the logical OR of the MET triggers to those that pass the analysis pre-selection alone. The results are presented in \cref{fig:triggerEffsig}. The trigger efficiency plateaus at 100\% for Pseudo MET near 250~GeV and at 30\% for PUppi MET near 450~GeV. The lower plateau for PUppi MET is primarily due to events with low Pseudo MET values failing to pass the trigger, while those with higher Pseudo MET values do pass.

The trigger's response to pair-produced HSCPs depends on their electric charge. This study presents a strategy for selecting HSCP gluinos using MET triggers to address the imbalance among the three production scenarios: charged-charged, neutral-charged, and neutral-neutral. Strategy (2) provides an effective compromise for signal acceptance, given that the uncalibrated Pseudo MET variable requires additional caution. Events are selected using a PUppi MET threshold greater than 150~GeV after passing one of the four MET triggers, with additional checks on the $H_T$, MHT, and MHTNoMu variables. The uncertainty in trigger efficiency is managed using scale factors, defined as the ratio of trigger efficiencies in data and MC simulations from orthogonal datasets that produce similar MET and signal signatures. The upward and downward variations of these scale factors will subsequently be applied as systematic uncertainties in the trigger selection, as discussed in \textbf{section XXXX}.

\section{Systematic Uncertainties}\label{sec:systematics}

\subsection{$F_\text{pixel}$ systematic uncertainty}
\label{sec:fi_syst}

The value of $F_\text{pixel}$ is sensitive to the pixel hit reconstruction, in particular to the Lorentz angle corrections and the pixel charge templates used during reconstruction. To evaluate the systematic uncertainty associated with the pixel calibration, a dedicated simulation sample is produced with the same generator-level signal (gluino, $m=1800$~GeV, $\sqrt{s}=13.6$ TeV) but simulated with an updated Global Tag\footnote{A Global Tag is a versioned label that identifies a coherent set of conditions and calibration data, including alignment constants, electric-field maps, and detector-response parameters, which are applied consistently during the reconstruction and simulation of a specific data-taking period.}. The updated tag contains new conditions describing the detector at the end of 2024 (i.e., after significant irradiation): one- and two-dimensional pixel charge templates (for the charge-reweighting irradiation model), Lorentz-angle corrections for data and simulation, and generic pixel-error parameters. The full simulation chain with 100k events is rerun with the new tag, and the results are processed using the standard analysis code.

\cref{fig:fi_syst} shows the $F_\text{pixel}$ distributions for the nominal and systematic samples, normalised to unity, both without any selection and after the standard track preselection described in Section~\cref{sec:selection}. The ratio of the number of tracks satisfying $F_\text{pixel}>0.9$ in the systematic variation to the nominal is $1.037$ before any selection and $1.016$ after preselection.

The difference observed in the signal region after preselection, $1.6\%$, is taken as a one-sided systematic uncertainty on the signal efficiency and is symmetrised to $\pm 1.6\%$. This uncertainty is applied to all signal mass points and is treated as correlated across signal categories. The pre-selection ratio of $3.7\%$ is larger because the preselection requirements on track quality and isolation preferentially retain tracks with well-measured pixel hits; the tighter sample is therefore less sensitive to the template calibration. The $1.6\%$ post-preselection value is therefore the conservative and appropriate estimate for the analysis.
