\section{Analysis strategy}

This analysis focuses on the search for heavy stable charged particles (HSCPs), specifically R-hadrons produced via gluino pair production. The experimental signature is characterised by a high-momentum, highly ionising track accompanied by missing transverse energy, which originates from ISR jets or from charge flipping during nuclear interactions within the detector. Unlike the previous analysis based on the Muon dataset~\cite{HSCP_Run2}, this search utilises the MET-triggered dataset. R-hadrons are frequently not reconstructed as muons, either due to insufficient velocity or because they become neutral before reaching the muon system. The use of MET trigger paths therefore recovers a significant portion of the signal that would be missed by a muon-based selection. Multiple MET HLT paths are combined, and the tracker acceptance is extended from $|\eta| < 1$ to $|\eta| < 2.4$, relative to the previous analysis.

A pre-selection step requires events to pass MET triggers, in addition to track-level criteria, the full list of which is described in \cref{sec:HSCPpresel}. The candidate mass is reconstructed using the track momentum $p$ and the harmonic mean $I_h$ of its $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x$ deposits in the strip tracker. Heavy particles are expected to manifest as an excess at large reconstructed mass, while Standard Model tracks predominantly populate lower mass values.

The background is estimated directly from data. The phase space is divided according to transverse momentum and the pixel-based discriminant $F_\text{pixel}$, which is uniformly distributed for Standard Model tracks, peaks at unity for signal, and is uncorrelated with $p_T$. Both high $p_T$ and high $F_\text{pixel}$ correspond to the signal region. The region characterised by high $p_T$ and low $F_\text{pixel}$, where signal contamination is negligible, serves as the control region. Background normalisation in the signal region is determined using an ABCD method, while the mass shape is predicted by convolving, for each pseudo-rapidity slice, the $1/p$ and $I_h$ templates extracted from the control region. The method is validated in an intermediate value of $F_\text{pixel}$, which is kept separate from the blinded signal region. Systematic uncertainties are propagated to the predicted mass spectrum. The comparison between observed and predicted spectra in the signal region is then used to derive exclusion limits on the HSCP production cross section as a function of mass.


\section{Datasets}

The data were collected in 2024 at a centre-of-mass energy of $\sqrt{s}=13.6$~TeV and are divided into seven eras (C through I). The analysis considers events that pass a MET trigger and are stored in the MET dataset. The total integrated luminosity is 109\,fb$^{-1}$.

The signal samples (\cref{tab:signal_samples}) comprise pair-produced gluinos that hadronise into R-hadrons, generated with \textsc{MadGraph} at $\sqrt{s}=13.6$\,TeV for ten mass hypotheses ranging from 1.1 to 2.6\,TeV. The theoretical production cross section, computed at NNLO+NNLL~\cite{10.21468/SciPostPhysCore.7.4.072, Fiaschi_2023}, decreases as the gluino mass increases. This mass range surpasses the exclusion limits established by previous searches ($m_{\tilde{g}}^\textrm{lim}=2.08$~TeV)~\cite{HSCP_Run2}.

The main background primarily consists of high-$p_T$ muons produced in addition to MET. The dominant processes are top-quark pair production in the dileptonic channel, $t\bar{t}\to 2\ell 2\nu$, and $W$ boson production in association with jets in the muonic decay channel, $W(\to\mu\nu)+\text{jets}$. Additionally, QCD multijet production is modelled using twelve slices of muon-enriched samples spanning $p_T$ from 15~GeV to above 1~TeV. These MC samples, listed in \cref{tab:mc_samples_2024}, are primarily utilised to calibrate the HSCP pre-selection stage. These samples are not incorporated into the background prediction method.

All signal and background MC samples are reweighted to match the 2024 luminosity, from which the corresponding weight is given in the tables and calculated as follows:
\begin{equation}
    w = \frac{\sigma \times \mathcal{L}}{\rm Total\,\,number\,\,of\,\,events} \, .
\end{equation}

\section{Analysis tools}

\subsection{The d\textit{E}/d\textit{x} pixel discriminator \texorpdfstring{$F_{\text{pixel}}$}{Fpixel}}

In this analysis, the pixel information is exploited via $F_{\text{pixel}}$, which quantifies the compatibility of a track with a MIP deposit. The pixel cluster shape and the charge distribution of MIPs are modelled from \texttt{PixelAV} simulations~\cite{1495807}, which reproduce charge deposition, transport, and collection processes in pixel sensors. 
These simulations are periodically tuned to data using large-$\eta$ tracks to provide angle-dependent models that yield different Vavilov function $V(Q)$ templates to model the charge distribution of MIPs.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

For a given pixel cluster carrying a charge $Q_{\text{cluster}}$, we define the tail probability 
\begin{equation}
p_Q = \int_{Q_{\text{cluster}}}^{+\infty} V(Q)\,\mathrm{d}Q,
\end{equation}
which quantifies the probability of depositing a charge greater than $Q_{\text{cluster}}$ for a MIP (see~\cref{fig:clustQ}). According to the Probability Integral Transform theorem~\cite{PITtheorem}, if $Q_{\text{cluster}}$ follows a Vavilov distribution $V(Q)$, then $p_Q$ is uniformly distributed on the interval [0,1]. In other words, assuming the particle is a MIP and the model $V(Q)$ is accurate, $p_Q$ is uniformly distributed by construction.

To complete the description of the four layers of the pixel subdetector, a joint coefficient is built using the different tail probabilities $p_{Q_i}$ associated with the charge deposits $Q_{\text{cluster}_i}$ in each pixel layer:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_n = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_{Q_i},
\end{equation}

The quantity $\alpha_n$ represents the joint probability of simultaneously observing charges at least as large in all layers: if a single layer is strongly ionising, $\alpha_n$ decreases; and if all layers are, $\alpha_n$ becomes significantly smaller.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.8\textwidth]{Images/Ch4/vavilov_charge.pdf}
    \caption{Example of charge deposits in pixel sensors at $\eta=2.40$, with the distribution (in black) fitted using a Vavilov function (in red). The tail distribution $p_Q$ is shown in filled grey.}
    \label{fig:clustQ}
\end{figure}

A difficulty arises because $\alpha_n$ is not uniformly distributed, even for a MIP. Its distribution depends strongly on $n$, which prevents a meaningful comparison between, for instance, the $n=3$ and $n=4$ cases. A different variable must therefore be constructed to obtain a behaviour subject to universal selection.

To do so, the variable $-\ln(\alpha_n)$ is taken as it follows a Gamma distribution with parameters $(n,1)$, if and only if each $p_i$ is uniformly distributed on $[0,1]$. The corresponding probability density function is then
\begin{equation}
\Gamma(n,1) = \frac{x^{\,n-1} e^{-x}}{(n-1)!}, \qquad x = -\ln(\alpha_n).
\end{equation}

The associated cumulative distribution function (CDF) $\beta_n$ can be expressed analytically as
\begin{equation}
\beta_n = \alpha_n \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} \frac{\left[-\ln(\alpha_n)\right]^m}{m!}.
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Since $\beta_n$ is the exact CDF of $\alpha_n$ under the MIP hypothesis, the Probability Integral Transform theorem guarantees that $\beta_n$ is uniformly distributed on [0,1] for MIPs, and the $n$-dependence that affected the raw coefficient $\alpha_n$ is fully absorbed into its CDF. This restores the uniformity required for a universal selection.

We therefore define:
\begin{equation}
    F_{\text{pixel}} = 1 - \beta_n \, ,
\end{equation}
which is also uniformly distributed on $[0,1]$ for MIPs, regardless of the number of pixel layers considered.

However, for strongly ionising particles, such as gluinos, the charge deposited in each layer exceeds that expected for a MIP. This leads to small $p_{Q_i}$ values, hence to a small $\alpha_n$ and a $\beta_n$ close to zero, so that $F_{\text{pixel}}$ approaches unity.

The variable $F_{\text{pixel}}$ therefore constitutes an effective discriminator between Standard Model and BSM processes.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

In this analysis, hits from the first layer of the pixel detector were excluded from the computation of $F_{\text{pixel}}$ due to premature ageing caused by high radiation levels in this layer of the Phase--1 detector. \cref{fig:FpixelInSignalData} shows the $F_{\text{pixel}}$ distribution for data collected in 2024 (left), in a region free from signal interference (all analysis pre-selection criteria, and $p_T < 55$~GeV). The $F_{\text{pixel}}$ distribution for a simulated HSCP gluino at $m_{\tilde{g}}=2000$~GeV after the analysis pre-selection criteria (right) shows that most of the signal is concentrated around unity.

Assuming the data distribution is perfectly flat when it is not introduces systematic uncertainties. These uncertainties are estimated by varying the aforementioned probability models and will be discussed in~\cref{subsec:systSignal}.

\subsection{The d\textit{E}/d\textit{x} strip estimator \texorpdfstring{$I_h$}{Ih}}

The search for HSCPs relies on tracking anomalous d$E$/d$x$ deposits within detector materials. The average energy deposit per track across the silicon tracker layers is quantified using the $I_h$ estimator, presented in \cref{sec:ihpres}. Owing to their greater mass, HSCPs generate higher energy deposits than MIPs produced by SM processes. MIPs exhibit nearly constant ionisation, resulting in low $I_h$ values for SM tracks, whereas HSCPs deposit substantially more charge per unit length and occupy the high-$I_h$ region. Therefore, $I_h$ enables effective discrimination between SM processes and HSCPs. This approach is complementary to the pixel-based estimator $F_{\text{pixel}}$, which deal with the charge distribution in the pixel layers, whereas $I_h$ aggregates ionisation measurements across the strip tracker.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\cref{fig:IhSignalData} demonstrates this separation. The upper panel displays the normalised $I_h$ distribution after applying analysis pre-selection criteria for the signal (right) and with an additional restrictive cut of $F_{\text{pixel}}<0.9$ to the data (left) to mitigate signal contamination. The data distribution exhibits a sharp peak at low ionisation, approximately $3.2$~MeV/cm, consistent with the MIP expectation, and decreases rapidly at higher values. In contrast, a gluino with $m_{\tilde{g}}=2000$~GeV gives rise to higher $\langle\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x\rangle$ values. The lower panel shows the corresponding cumulative distribution functions: the data CDF rises to unity almost immediately after the MIP level, while the gluino CDF increases gradually, highlighting the strong separation between the two populations across most of the $I_h$ range.

\subsection{Stability of d\textit{E}/d\textit{x} variables over the year}

The stability of the two ionisation observables used in this analysis is monitored throughout the 2024 year by computing, for each run, the mean values of $I_h$ and $F_\text{pixel}$ for tracks without selections and for those that pass the analysis pre-selection. The evolution of $\langle I_h \rangle$ as a function of run number is presented in \cref{fig:dedx_stability} (top left), along with the distribution of the per-run means (top right). Following the application of the charge calibration described in \cref{app:calibration}, the strip-based estimator remains stable over the year. The per-run means are centred around $\langle I_h \rangle \approx 3.37~(3.32)$~MeV/cm, with a spread at the percent level for tracks that do (don't) pass the analysis pre-selection step.

A similar monitoring procedure is applied to the pixel-based discriminant (bottom row). The per-run mean of $F_\text{pixel}$ is centred around 0.65 (0.61) for tracks that do (don't) pass the analysis pre-selection step, with a relative dispersion of a few percent. Because $F_\text{pixel}$ is constructed from the pixel charge templates, the slight decrease observed over the year reflects the progressive degradation of the pixel sensors due to irradiation, which reduces the collected charge. However, this drift remains modest compared to the width of the $F_\text{pixel}$ distribution, allowing the variable to be used consistently throughout the 2024 dataset.

These checks confirm that both ionisation variables can be used consistently over the full 2024 dataset without any time-dependent correction beyond the charge calibration already applied.

\section{Selecting signal-like candidates}
\label{sec:HSCPpresel}

\subsection{Trigger event selection}
\label{sec:trigger}

The Run--3 analysis targets primarily hadron-like HSCPs, for which the $dE/dx$-based single-muon trigger strategy adopted in Run--2 is not optimal. R-hadrons are pair-produced with a charged and a neutral component in roughly half of the events, and the resulting momentum imbalance, together with initial-state radiations (ISRs), gives rise to missing transverse energy. A dedicated MET trigger strategy was therefore developed, combining four HLT paths:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{HLT\_PFMET120\_PFMHT120\_IDTight},
    \item \texttt{HLT\_PFMETNoMu120\_PFMHTNoMu120\_IDTight\_PFHT60},
    \item \texttt{HLT\_PFHT500\_PFMET100\_PFMHT100\_IDTight},
    \item \texttt{HLT\_MET105\_IsoTrk50}.
\end{itemize}
The naming convention of each HLT path directly encodes the offline-like cuts applied at that stage: the algorithm prefix (\texttt{PFMET}, \texttt{PFMHT}, \texttt{PFHT} and \texttt{IsoTrk}) identifies the reconstructed quantity being thresholded, while the number gives the corresponding $p_T$ (or $H_T$) cut in GeV. \texttt{IDTight} signifies that tight jet identification is applied to the jets entering the MHT sum, \texttt{NoMu} indicates that muon contributions are subtracted from the MET/MHT computation, and \texttt{IsoTrk50} requires an isolated track with $p_T>50$~GeV. 

At the L1 trigger level, the seed lists are a logical OR of several \texttt{L1\_ETMHF} thresholds (MET computing using the central and forward calorimeters). Since \texttt{ETMHF} is a calorimeter-based quantity, the initial L1 decision is essentially always driven by the Calo MET, with the PF MET/MHT only becoming the discriminating variable at the HLT stage.


\subsubsection{HSCP MET topologies}

PUppi MET (Run--3 recommended MET estimator, similar to PF MET but with cautious PU considerations) is only produced when the R-hadron pair consists of one charged and one neutral state, since a charged-charged or neutral-neutral pair leaves no net PF imbalance. On the other hand, Calo MET still arise from an ISR jet or photon recoiling against the pair. Five kinematic topologies, depending on the nature of the pair-produced HSCP, were identified on this basis and are illustrated in \cref{fig:METtopology}, ranging from configurations with negligible MET to those dominated by either PUppi or Calo MET, thereby motivating the use of several complementary MET observables rather than a single variable.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\cref{fig:pairtypestages} follows the HSCP pair-type distribution through the analysis chain for a representative signal point ($m_{\tilde{g}}=2000$~GeV), comparing the generated composition with that obtained after the OR of the four MET triggers.

The reduction from the generated composition (black dots, summing to unity) to the post-trigger composition (red squares) is essentially uniform across the three pair-type categories, and simply reflects the overall OR-trigger efficiency already measured in \cref{fig:pftypeprop} (Right plot, last bin content): summing the red squares over the three categories recovers the same $\sim$30\% efficiency obtained for the $m_{\tilde{g}}=2000$~GeV point, confirming that the OR trigger is not preferentially selecting a given pair type at this stage, but rather applies an approximately flat acceptance across all three configurations.

Among triggered events, the fractions instead change significantly depending on the number of HSCP candidates matched with a track: neutral--neutral events populate almost exclusively the 0-matched category, since the neutral R-hadron leaves no track, while charged-charged events dominate the 2-matched category and charged--neutral events mainly populate the 1-matched category.

This correspondence can be quantified further. At generator level, essentially all signal events contain two generated HSCPs, distributed among the charged-charged, charged-neutral, and neutral-neutral pair types with respective fractions of about 20\%, 50\%, and 30\% (black dots in~\cref{fig:pairtypestages}). After requiring a track match between the generated HSCPs and the event reconstructed tracks, the observed 0-, 1-, and 2-matched-track fractions are instead 41\%, 46\%, and 14\%, respectively. While the 1-matched-track fraction closely follows the generated charged-neutral fraction, the 2-matched-track fraction falls below the generated charged-charged fraction (14\% vs 20\%), and the 0-matched-track fraction correspondingly exceeds the generated neutral-neutral fraction (41\% vs 30\%). This shift towards lower matched-track multiplicities indicates that HSCPs are predominantly lost through charge flipping of a charged R-hadron to a neutral state.

\subsubsection{MET Trigger strategy}

The selected MET triggers are determined by the Calo MET and PUppi MET at the L1 level, and the efficiency of selecting offline events using these triggers can be assessed as a function of these two variables. This trigger-efficiency procedure must be carefully performed on both simulation and data. Near the turn-on region, the efficiency depends on the thresholded observable, and this dependence is governed by the MET resolution, which is not perfectly modelled in simulation. As a result, data and simulation diverge in this region, and the signal efficiency cannot be considered reliable across the whole Calo MET or PUppi MET range. Beyond the turn-on, in the \textit{trigger plateau}, the efficiency saturates and becomes flat. In this regime, the ratio of the trigger efficiency in data to that in simulation, expressed as a function of one of the MET variables and known as the \textit{scale factor}, is close to unity. The trigger response should therefore be studied on the plateau rather than integrated over the full turn-on, since the signal acceptance used in the final result is taken from simulation and its trigger contribution is reliable only if the simulated trigger accurately reproduces the one observed in data.

This requirement is especially critical for the HSCP analysis, where, if no excess is observed, limits on the theoretical model are computed using a shape analysis. A flat plateau efficiency multiplies the mass spectrum by a constant, affecting only its overall normalisation and leaving both the signal shape and the data-driven background prediction unchanged. However, without scale-factor corrections and a selection applied to the plateau, the signal's accuracy near the turn-on point is uncertain, leading to uncertainty in counting signal events and, consequently, to unreliable exclusion limits. 

The adopted strategy follows directly from these considerations. The turn-on curves are first measured in parallel in data and simulation as functions of the offline PUppi MET and Calo MET, and the corresponding data-to-simulation scale factors are extracted. The offline thresholds are then set sufficiently high to place the analysis on the plateau, where these scale factors are flat and well controlled.


\subsubsection{MET trigger efficiency}

As the HSCP analysis moved from an \textsc{AOD} to a \textsc{MiniAOD} framework for analysing Run--3 data, some variables have changed. This is the case for the Calo MET, where muon energy deposits within the calorimeters are included in its computation, thereby biasing its value. Since trigger efficiency is driven by Calo MET at the L1 level, to recover the MET deposits within the calo towers, one could build a proxy observable that mimics the AOD Calo MET. Therefore, a pseudo-MET variable is reconstructed offline from the calorimeter jet (CaloJets) collection as the magnitude of the vectorial transverse-momentum sum of all calorimeter jets in the event,
\begin{equation}
   \mathrm{Pseudo~MET} = \sqrt{\left\|\sum_{\text{CaloJets}} \overrightarrow{p_{x_j}}\right\|^2 + \left\|\sum_{\text{CaloJets}} \overrightarrow{p_{y_j}} \right\|^2 } .
\end{equation}
This variable was validated on Run--2 AOD samples by comparison with the Calo MET, which showed a similar distribution after the analysis pre-selection step. Since the Pseudo MET is constructed directly from the raw CaloJet collection, no offset subtraction, response correction, or residual scale factor is applied to replicate the corrections provided by the CMS Jet Energy Resolution and Corrections (JERC) group. Consequently, this alternative MET variable includes uncorrected jet energy contributions, and its absolute scale does not match that of the Calo MET. This is not considered a limitation because the Pseudo MET is used solely to identify the trigger plateau and to extract the corresponding scale factors. Both processes depend on the shape of the turn-on rather than on the absolute energy calibration. The same definition is applied consistently to both data and simulation, ensuring that any residual miscalibration cancels in the data/MC ratio.

\subsubsection{Trigger strategy adopted}

The trigger's response to pair-produced HSCPs depends on their electric charge. This study presents a strategy for selecting HSCP gluinos using MET triggers to address the imbalance among the three production scenarios: charged-charged, neutral-charged, and neutral-neutral. Strategy (2) provides an effective compromise for signal acceptance, given that the uncalibrated Pseudo MET variable requires additional caution. Events are selected using a PUppi MET threshold greater than 150~GeV after passing one of the four MET triggers, with additional checks on the $H_T$, MHT, and MHTNoMu variables. The uncertainty in trigger efficiency is managed using scale factors, defined as the ratio of trigger efficiencies in data and MC simulations from orthogonal datasets that produce similar MET and signal signatures. The upward and downward variations of these scale factors will subsequently be applied as systematic uncertainties in the trigger selection, as discussed in \cref{sec:systematics}.


\subsection{HSCP candidates selection}
\label{sec:preselection}

Before background estimation, a set of offline requirements is imposed on events and tracks to select HSCP candidates. The primary sources of such tracks in data are $W(\to \mu\nu)$+jets, $t\bar{t}\to 2\ell 2\nu$ events, and QCD multijet $\mu$-enriched production, where a high $p_T$ muon can imitate the signature of a slow, highly ionising particle. The pre-selection aims to suppress these backgrounds while maintaining high signal efficiency. Final selection occurs in the $\left(p_T, F_\textrm{pixel}\right)$ plane, as defined in the ABCD method at the background estimation step, with region D serving as the signal region.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

At the event level, candidates must activate at least one of the MET trigger paths described in \cref{sec:trigger} and pass the standard MET noise filters, which remove events where missing transverse momentum arises from detector effects such as noisy calorimeter cells. An offline requirement of PUppi MET > 150~GeV is subsequently applied to ensure robust signal selection while maintaining scale factors close to unity, thereby preserving reliable simulation modelling.

Candidate tracks must satisfy $p_T > 50$~GeV and $|\eta| < 2.4$. The $p_T$ requirement is a mandatory selection to retain tracks with d$E$/d$x$ clusters in the \textsc{MiniAOD} datasets. This threshold minimally affects the signal, as $p/m = \beta\gamma$ implies that particles with masses of several hundred GeV possess momenta well above this limit, while the background spectrum decreases sharply. The pseudorapidity range matches the full tracker acceptance. Unlike the Run-2 analysis, which relied on a limited search to $|\eta| < 1.0$ (homogeneous silicon tracker and mostly muon DTs).

\vspace{0.5 cm}

A first group of criteria targets the quality of the track reconstruction and of the ionisation measurement:
\begin{itemize}
  \item at least two valid pixel hits are required, so that the $F_\textrm{pixel}$ discriminant is built from a minimum of two independent cluster probabilities;
  \item the fraction of valid hits, i.e.\ the fraction of hits compatible with the propagated trajectory among the crossed modules, must exceed 0.8, rejecting misreconstructed tracks;
  \item at least ten $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x$ measurements must enter the estimators, the first barrel pixel layer being excluded as it is the most exposed to radiation effects. For signal tracks, the number of measurements peaks around fifteen, so this requirement mainly removes short tracks;
  \item the track must carry the \textit{HighPurity} flag (see \cref{sec:final_track_fit}) and satisfy $\chi^{2}/N_{\mathrm{dof}} < 5$, the latter being largely redundant with the former but removing a residual population with anomalous fit quality.
\end{itemize}

Compatibility with the primary vertex is enforced through $|d_{z}| < 0.1$~cm and $|d_{xy}| < 0.02$~cm, restricting the search to promptly produced particles.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Two isolation criteria are applied to reject non-prompt leptons produced within jets, which are the primary sources of QCD and $t\overline{t}$ background contributions. The relative isolation, calculated from particle-flow candidates surrounding the track, must satisfy $I_\textrm{rel}^\textrm{PF} < 0.02$. Additionally, the tracker-based isolation $I_\textrm{trk}$, defined for all tracks within a cone of $\Delta R < 0.3$, must remain below 15~GeV (both isolations are defined in \cref{sec:muons}). As shown in the cutflow of table~\ref{tab:preselection}, the particle-flow isolation requirement is among the most effective offline discriminants in the data. This criterion reduces the selected sample by nearly an order of magnitude, while the associated signal loss remains at the percent level.

Due to the relativistic rise of the Bethe-Bloch curve, electrons in this region can produce ionisation signatures similar to HSCPs. However, electrons deposit nearly all their energy in the electromagnetic calorimeter. Therefore, imposing the requirement that the calorimetric energy be less than the track momentum ($E/p < 0.3$) effectively removes this background without affecting the signal. Tracks with poorly measured momentum, which may result from incorrectly assigned hits or hit sharing between nearby particles, are excluded by requiring $\sigma_{p_T}/p_T^{2} < 0.0008$~GeV$^{-1}$ and $\sigma_{p_T}/p_T < 1$. The latter criterion specifically addresses the high-momentum region, where the relative resolution deteriorates.

Two additional requirements are imposed directly on the ionisation estimators. Broken clusters, which occur when a long pixel cluster is split into multiple clusters in the readout chips or due to irradiation effects, bias the charge estimate toward lower values. Applying a cut at $F_\textrm{pixel} > 0.3$ removes these affected tracks, while the signal distribution peaks near unity. A minimum ionisation requirement of $I_h > C_\mathrm{mass}$ is also imposed, where $C_\mathrm{mass}$ is a parameter used in the subsequent mass reconstruction; its value sits near the MIP level ($\approx3$~MeV/cm).

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\cref{tab:preselection} presents the successive selection requirements and their cumulative efficiencies for the 2024 MET dataset and a pair-produced gluino sample with $m_{\tilde{g}} = 2000$~GeV. \cref{fig:cutflow} displays the corresponding cutflow in terms of the absolute number of events. The most significant reduction in signal after the trigger stage occurs at the $p_T>50$~GeV requirement, which is the first criterion applied directly to the candidate track. Events showing no tracks, particularly where both R-hadrons are neutral in the tracker, are excluded at this stage. The subsequent two isolation requirements further remove a related subset of events. Because none of the earlier criteria prevents a jet from being identified as an HSCP candidate, jets may be misidentified in events where the R-hadrons are neutral or not reconstructed; the isolation cuts then eliminate these jets. The pre-selection reduces the dataset by nearly four orders of magnitude while retaining $11.6\%$ of the signal. In comparison, the Run~2 analysis achieved a final efficiency of $4.8\%$ for the same mass point. The improvement by more than a factor of two is attributed to the revised trigger strategy, which no longer relies on reconstructing the HSCP as a muon, and to the extended pseudorapidity coverage enabled by this approach.

\section{Mass reconstruction strategy}




\section{Background estimation method}

The background estimation relied on the mass reconstruction of HSCP candidates. It allows prediction of the event yield in the signal region as well as the background mass spectrum. 

\subsection{Method description}

Background estimation is performed using the ABCD method, in which the phase space is partitioned according to the $F_\textrm{pixel}$ variable and transverse momentum $p_T$. Control (CR) and signal regions (SR) are established by applying selection criteria to these variables, which are assumed to be uncorrelated. \cref{fig:scheme_ABCDmass} illustrates the defined regions. The SR, denoted as region $D$, includes HSCP candidates with both high $p_T$ and high $F_\textrm{pixel}$ values. Regions $A$, $B$, and $C$ correspond to candidates with low $p_T$ and low $F_\textrm{pixel}$ values, to candidates with low $p_T$ and high $F_\textrm{pixel}$ values, and to candidates with high $p_T$ and low $F_\textrm{pixel}$ values, respectively.

Multiple $B$ and $D$ regions can be defined for application of the background prediction method. In addition to the signal regions, a validation region (VR), determined by region $D'$ with high $p_T$ and $0.8<F_\textrm{pixel}\leq0.9$, is utilised to assess the validity of the mass prediction.

This method estimates the normalisation, defined as the number of events, in the signal region $D$ by utilising the event counts from control regions $A$, $B$, and $C$ as follows:
\begin{equation}
	{\rm yield}_D= {\rm yield}_B\times {\rm yield}_C/{\rm yield}_A. 
\label{eq:normalisation}
\end{equation}

Furthermore, the method predicts the shape of the mass distribution in the signal region $D$ using information from the $\langle\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x\rangle$ estimator $I_h$ and the inverse momentum $1/p$ distributions (to include high $p$ values) of the CR $C$.

An example of these templates is presented in \cref{fig:ih_oP_fit}. Fits are applied to the tails of these templates to minimise the influence of statistical fluctuations. The $I_h$ tail distribution is fitted with a Gaussian function, and the latter is used when the bin content is below 100 tracks and with an $I_h$ value of at least 10\% greater than the maximum. The $1/p$ initial tail distribution is fitted using an Error function for bins below 90\% of the maximum and is used when $1/p$ is less than 20\% of the maximum. For the latter, the fit exhibits a higher $\chi^2/N_\textrm{dof}$, primarily due to fluctuations near the distribution's maximum.

  \label{fig:ih_oP_fit}
\end{figure}

Because the $\eta$ distribution in the CR $C$ does not accurately represent the $\eta$ distribution in region $D$ (as shown in \cref{fig:eta_reweighting} (Left)), a rescaling procedure is implemented to align the $\eta$ distribution with that of the SR. The distribution in CR $C$ is modified as follows:
\begin{equation}
  \eta_C \rightarrow \eta_C \times \frac{\eta_B}{\eta_A} \, .
\end{equation}
These weights are subsequently applied to the $1/p$ distribution in CR $C$ to achieve consistency with the distribution observed in the signal region. \cref{fig:eta_reweighting} (Right) demonstrates the agreement between the reweighted distribution in CR $C$ and that in the VR.

		 \label{fig:eta_reweighting}
\end{figure}

Due to the correlation between $p$, $p_T$, and $\eta$ (and to a lesser extent between $I_h$ and $\eta$), the mass distribution is reconstructed in bins of $\eta$. To facilitate this, templates in the CR are constructed in two dimensions: ($1/p$, $\eta$) and ($\eta$, $I_h$). The $\eta$ distribution is divided into 24 bins ($\Delta_{\eta} = 0.1$), $I_h$ into 100 bins ($\Delta_{I_h} = 0.05~\mathrm{MeV/cm}$), and $1/p$ into 500 bins ($\Delta_{1/p} = 0.4$~GeV$^{-1}$).

The mass distribution is filled using a triple loop: the outer loop iterates over $\eta$ bins, the middle loop over $1/p$ bins, and the inner loop over $I_h$ bins. Within this structure, the indices ($i$, $j$, $k$) correspond to the ($\eta$, $1/p$, $I_h$) bin, where the relevant computations are performed:

\begin{itemize}
  \item the mass value $M_{i,j,k}$ as
  \begin{equation}
    M_{i,j,k} = p_{j,i} \times \sqrt{\frac{I_{h_{i,k}}-C_\mathrm{mass}}{K_\mathrm{mass}} }
  \end{equation}
  where $p_{j,i}$ is the momentum value taken as the inverse of the $1/p$ value in the $(j,i)$ bin of the $(1/p,\,\eta)$ template, and $I_{h_{i,k}}$ is the $\langle\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x\rangle$ value of the $(i,k)$ bin of the $(\eta,\,I_h)$ template;
  
  \item and the associated weight $w_{i,j,k}$
  \begin{equation}
    w_{i,j,k} = \left(\frac{n_{ji}}{\sum_m n_{mi}}\right) \cdot h_{ik}
  \end{equation}
  where $n_{ji}$ is the number of observed events in the $(j,i)$ bin of the $(1/p,\,\eta)$ distribution and $h_{ik}$ is the number of observed events in the $(i,k)$ bin of the $(\eta,\,I_h)$ distribution.
\end{itemize}

The mass distribution is then constructed and rescaled to the expected global normalisation given by equation (\ref{eq:normalisation}). Statistical uncertainty associated with the prediction is estimated using 200 pseudo-experiments. For each pseudo-experiment, new $I_h$ and $1/p$ templates are generated by randomly sampling each bin from a Poisson distribution with a mean equal to the yield of the corresponding original template bin. This randomisation is also applied to the yield in the CR used for global normalisation. The mean of the 200 pseudo-mass predictions serves as the central value for the mass prediction, while the RMS defines the statistical uncertainty envelope.


\subsection{Preliminary remarks on the mass reconstruction method}
\label{sec:abcd_remarks}

This method has been employed in several previous iterations of the HSCP analysis. However, multiple modifications have been implemented since the most recent CMS public results~\cite{HSCP_Run2}. The key changes are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A new mapping for the phase space, $F_\textrm{pixel}$, is employed in place of $G_\textrm{strip}$. The $G_\textrm{strip}$ estimator relies on d$E$/d$x$ deposits within strip sensors to determine whether a HSCP candidate is compatible with a track depositing at the MIP level. $G_\textrm{strip}$ increases exponentially towards 1 for events that are incompatible with the MIP. However, this estimator correlates with $p_T$: higher $p_T$ values yield greater d$E$/d$x$ deposits, introducing bias in mass-spectrum predictions.
  \item The fits of the $I_h$ and $1/p$ tail templates have been refined to accommodate the expanded eta acceptance. This adjustment is particularly significant for the $1/p$ distribution, where, in the endcap region ($|\eta|>1$), the shape differs at low $1/p$, leading to inaccurate mass calculations with previous fitting strategies.
  \item Eta reweighting is now conducted using all three alternative regions ($A$, $B$, and $C$) of the ABCD method, rather than relying solely on regions $B$ and $C$ as in the previous version of the analysis.
\end{itemize}

\section{Systematics}
\label{sec:systematics}

This strategy considers two primary types of systematic uncertainties for the final interpretation. These uncertainties may arise from variable calibration, methodological biases, or binning choices. Systematic uncertainties are incorporated into both the background prediction and the signal modelling.

\subsection{Systematics related to the background prediction}
\label{subsec:systBkgPred}

The background prediction is based on several methodological choices, including the binning of input distributions, the modelling of fit tail templates, and the assumption that the $1/p$ and $I_h$ distributions are independent of $F_\textrm{pixel}$. Each choice introduces a systematic uncertainty in the predicted mass spectrum. These uncertainties are quantified by repeating the full prediction procedure with modified configurations and comparing the resulting mass spectra to the nominal spectrum. For each source, both upward and downward variations are defined, and the bin-by-bin ratios between the varied and nominal spectra are used to determine the corresponding uncertainties. The considered sources are the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The binning of the $\eta$, $I_h$, and $10^{4}/p$ distributions used in the convolution employs nominal bin widths of $\Delta_{\eta} = 0.1$, $\Delta_{I_h} = 0.05~\mathrm{MeV/cm}$, and $\Delta_{1/p} = 0.4$~GeV$^{-1}$, respectively. For each variable, upward (downward) variation is implemented by doubling (halving) the nominal bin width, while all other aspects of the procedure remain unchanged.
    \item The fits used to model the tails of the $I_h$ and $1/p$ templates are varied by shifting the fitted parameters upward and downward by their respective uncertainties. The prediction is then recalculated using these modified templates.
    \item The method assumes that the $I_h$ and $1/p$ templates are independent of the $F_\textrm{pixel}$ variable. Violation of this assumption would invalidate the prediction of the shape in the SR, which is fundamental to the method. The residual correlation between $1/p$ and $F_\textrm{pixel}$ is addressed through the rescaling in $\eta$ shown in \cref{fig:eta_reweighting}. The correlation between $I_h$ and $F_\textrm{pixel}$ is evaluated by taking the ratio of the $I_h$ template in the CR to that in the VR (see \cref{fig:ih_correlation}). This ratio is fitted with a linear function, and the resulting bias is corrected in the $I_h$ template in the CR prior to mass spectrum construction. The systematic uncertainty is estimated by omitting this correction and comparing the resulting difference with the nominal mass spectrum distribution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Systematics related to the signal}
\label{subsec:systSignal}

Uncertainties in the simulation affect the signal predictions. These uncertainties are estimated using the same methodology as for the background. Each source is varied upward and downward, the complete analysis chain is rerun, and the bin-by-bin ratios between the varied and nominal mass spectra are used to quantify the corresponding uncertainties. The total systematic uncertainty is calculated as the quadratic sum of the individual sources. The considered sources are the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The uncertainty associated with the luminosity measurement is estimated to be 1.4\% for 2024~\cite{CMS-DP-2026-003}.
    \item The Pile-Up profile in the 2024 simulation is compared to the observed 2024 Pile-Up distribution. A shift of $\pm1\sigma$ is applied, and corresponding event weights are assigned in the simulation.
    \item Trigger scale factors related to the PUppi MET shown in \cref{fig:triggerSF} (left) are varied within their respective statistical uncertainties.
    \item The $K_\textrm{mass}$ and $C_\textrm{mass}$ calibration factors used in mass reconstruction are varied based on the observed differences between data and simulation. The upward and downward variations are defined as $K_\textrm{mass}^\textrm{MC} \pm |K_\textrm{mass}^\textrm{data} - K_\textrm{mass}^\textrm{MC}|$, and in the same way for $C_\textrm{mass}$.
    \item Jet energy uncertainties, provided by the CMS Jet MET working group, are propagated upward and downward in the computation of $H_T$, MHT, and MHTNoMu for the trigger requirement, as well as for the PUppi MET.
    \item The value of $F_\textrm{pixel}$ is sensitive to pixel hit reconstruction, particularly to angle corrections coming from high-$p$ tracks, and the charge templates applied during reconstruction. To evaluate the impact of pixel calibration, a dedicated sample of 100,000 gluino events ($m_{\tilde{g}} = 1800$~GeV) was simulated using an updated Global Tag\footnote{A Global Tag serves as a versioned identifier that defines the coherent set of calibration, alignment, and detector-condition records utilised by CMSSW during dataset processing.} that reflects pixel conditions at the end of 2024, following significant irradiation, and processed with the standard analysis chain. \cref{fig:fi_syst} presents the $F_\textrm{pixel}$ distributions for the nominal and systematic samples, after the analysis pre-selection step. The ratio of yields in the region $F_\textrm{pixel} > 0.9$ between the two samples is $1.016$ after pre-selection. The difference of $1.6\%$ is symmetrised, and the corresponding variations are implemented by selecting HSCP candidates in the Signal region such that $F_\textrm{pixel} \cdot (1 \pm 0.016) > 0.9$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Statistical interpretation and expected limits}
\label{sec:limits}

The background prediction and the signal mass spectra derived in the previous sections are now combined into a statistical model from which upper limits on the gluino pair-production cross section are extracted. As the signal region remains blinded at this stage of the analysis, the observed data entering the model are replaced by the background expectation, and all results presented below are expected limits. They serve two purposes: quantifying the sensitivity of the search prior to unblinding and motivating the choice of the acceptance configuration and the extraction method for the final interpretation.

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{CL$_\text{s}$}{CLs} method}
\label{sec:limits:cls}

Upper limits are derived with the modified frequentist CL$_\text{s}$ method~\cite{CLSpaper},
the standard prescription for searches at the LHC. Two hypotheses are confronted: the background-only hypothesis $b$, under which the data are entirely described by known processes, and the signal-plus-background hypothesis $s+b$, under which a gluino contribution of strength $\mu$ is added on top of the background. The comparison between the two relies on the profile likelihood ratio test statistic
\begin{equation}
    q_\mu = -2 \ln \frac{\mathcal{L}\left(\text{data} \,\middle|\, \mu s + b,\, \hat{\hat{\theta}}_\mu\right)}{\mathcal{L}\left(\text{data} \,\middle|\, \hat{\mu} s + b,\, \hat{\theta}\right)},
    \qquad 0 \leq \hat{\mu} \leq \mu,
    \label{eq:limits:qmu}
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ denotes the ensemble of nuisance parameters encoding the systematic uncertainties, $\hat{\mu}$ and $\hat{\theta}$ maximise the likelihood globally, and $\hat{\hat{\theta}}_\mu$ maximises it for a fixed signal strength $\mu$. The CL$_\text{s}$ quantity is defined as the ratio of the $p$-values of the two hypotheses, $\textrm{CL$_\text{s}$} = \text{CL}_{s+b}/\text{CL}_b$, a construction that prevents the exclusion of signal hypotheses to which the analysis has no actual sensitivity. A cross section is excluded at $95\%$ confidence level when $\textrm{CL$_\text{s}$} < 0.05$.

All limits in this section are computed in the asymptotic approximation~\cite{AsymptoticPaper}, in which the distributions of $q_\mu$ under both hypotheses take analytic forms and the median expected limit, together with its one and two standard deviation bands, is obtained from a single Asimov dataset. This bypasses the need to generate pseudo-experiments and reduces computation to a few minutes per mass hypothesis, compared with several hours of parallel toy generation for the full CL$_\text{s}$ construction. The extraction is performed with the \textsc{Combine} package developed by the CMS collaboration~\cite{CombinePaper}.

\subsection{Construction of the statistical models}
\label{sec:limits:models}

Two statistical treatments of the mass spectrum are compared, built from identical inputs: a counting experiment in a mass window, hereafter referred to as the cut-and-count method, and a binned maximum-likelihood fit of the full spectrum, referred to as the shape analysis.

In both cases a mass window is defined for each signal hypothesis as $[\mu_m - \sigma_m,\, \mu_m + 2\sigma_m]$, where $\mu_m$ and $\sigma_m$ are the mean and standard deviation of the reconstructed mass distribution of the signal of mass $m$; the lower edge is in addition not allowed to fall below $300$~GeV, so as to stay clear of the region where the background is largest. The asymmetry of the window with respect to $\mu_m$ reflects the steeply falling background: extending the window downwards admits background at a much higher rate than it gains signal, whereas the upper side is nearly background free.

In the cut-and-count method, the signal and background yields are the integrals of the corresponding spectra within the window, and the statistical model reduces to a single-bin Poisson counting experiment. Systematic uncertainties are treated as log-normal nuisance parameters, with factors obtained by integrating the systematically varied templates over the same window and normalising to the nominal yield. Two-sided variations are symmetrised by retaining the larger of the two deviations, while one-sided variations translate into a single multiplicative factor.

In the shape analysis, the full mass spectrum is included in the likelihood. The spectra are cast into a common variable binning spanning $0$ to $4000$~GeV, with a constant $20$~GeV width up to about $400$~GeV that progressively widens to several hundred GeV at high mass, following the decrease of the background statistics so that the predicted spectrum remains populated over the whole range. Each systematic uncertainty is described by a pair of alternative templates, from which \textsc{Combine} interpolates continuously; one-sided uncertainties use the same template for both directions. The background normalisation is deliberately left free in the fit through an unconstrained rate parameter, one per channel: the limit thus exploits the predicted shape of the background, while its normalisation is determined in situ from the sidebands of the mass spectrum. Finally, the statistical uncertainty of the templates is accounted for bin by bin with the Barlow--Beeston-lite approach implemented in the \texttt{autoMCStats} directive.

The systematic uncertainties entering the models are summarised in \cref{tab:limits:systs}. The uncertainties affecting the signal are the level of pileup, the trigger scale factors, the calibration constants $K_\textrm{mass}$ and $C_\textrm{mass}$ of the mass reconstruction, the $F_\textrm{pixel}$ calibration through the up and down procedure described in \cref{subsec:systSignal}
the jet energy scale, and the integrated luminosity, assigned a $1.4\%$ normalisation uncertainty. The uncertainties affecting the background prediction probe the stability of the method itself: the binning of the $\eta$, $I_h$ and momentum templates is halved and doubled with respect to the nominal choice, the fits of the $I_h$ and $1/p$ templates are varied within their uncertainties, and a one-sided variation replaces the nominal $I_h$ template with its corrected counterpart. In the cut-and-count model the same sources are propagated as log-normal factors.

\subsection{Acceptance configurations}
\label{sec:limits:categories}

The extension of the track acceptance from $|\eta|<1$, as retained in earlier searches, to $|\eta|<2.4$ raises the question of how the forward region should enter the statistical model. Three configurations are considered. In the first, only the central region $|\eta|<1$ is used. In the second, the full acceptance $|\eta|<2.4$ is treated as a single inclusive channel. In the third, the central and forward ($1<|\eta|<2.4$) regions enter the fit as two separate channels: the signal templates, the background prediction and all its systematic variations are produced independently in each region, and the two channels are combined in a single likelihood. All nuisance parameters are correlated between the two channels, since both regions are built from the same dataset, the same calibration and the same prediction machinery; the only exceptions are the background rate parameters, which float independently in each channel. The framework nevertheless allows any individual source to be decorrelated between the categories, should the treatment of a given uncertainty require it.

For each configuration and each extraction method, a datacard and, for the shape analysis, the associated template file are produced for the ten gluino mass hypotheses ranging from $1100$ to $2600$~GeV. The resulting limits on the signal strength are converted into cross section limits using the NNLO+NNLL gluino pair-production cross sections at $\sqrt{s}=13.6$~TeV.

The six configurations are compared directly in \cref{fig:limits:compare}, together with the limit published by the previous CMS search~\cite{HSCP_Run2}. Three observations emerge from this comparison.

First, the shape analysis performs at least as well as the cut-and-count method in every configuration. The gain is modest in the central region, where the counting experiment already operates in a nearly background-free window, but it reaches about $70$~GeV in excluded mass for the inclusive acceptance, where the larger background leaves more information to recover from the event distribution within the window. Since the shape analysis contains the counting experiment as a limiting case, it is retained as the baseline extraction method.

Second, the treatment of the forward region substantially affects sensitivity. Merging the two regions into a single inclusive channel degrades the expected excluded mass by more than $200$~GeV with respect to the central region alone: the background level rises steeply with $|\eta|$, and in an inclusive channel this background dilutes the nearly pure central region. Splitting the acceptance into two channels removes this dilution entirely, as the likelihood then weights each region according to its own purity. The resulting limit coincides with that of the central region alone at the mass points considered, showing that for gluino signals, which are produced centrally, the sensitivity is carried almost entirely by the $|\eta|<1$ channel. The split configuration is nonetheless preferred over the purely central one: it preserves the full acceptance at no cost in sensitivity, retains sensitivity to signal models with broader pseudorapidity spectra, and keeps the forward region available as a consistency check once the data are unblinded.

Third, the comparison with the previous CMS result places the expected reach of this analysis at $2.42$~TeV, to be compared with the $2.08$~TeV excluded by the Run~2 search at a comparable integrated luminosity. This comparison calls for some care: the earlier result is an observed limit obtained with the full CL$_\text{s}$ construction and compared to the $13$~TeV cross sections, whereas the present figures are expected limits in the asymptotic approximation, which is known to be somewhat aggressive in the low-count regime, compared to the slightly larger $13.6$~TeV cross sections. These caveats accounted for, the improvement remains considerable, and reflects the higher signal efficiency of the MET-based trigger strategy developed for this analysis.

The picture is consistent across the four mass points. The two leading parameters are the free background normalisations of the central and forward channels, which is the expected behaviour of a model in which the background rate is determined by the fit itself rather than constrained a priori. They are followed by the d$E$/d$x$ calibration constants $K_\textrm{mass}$ and $C_\textrm{mass}$ of the signal mass reconstruction and by the uncertainties on the background modelling, in particular the variations of the $1/p$ fit and of the $I_h$ template binning. The remaining sources, among which the pileup, the trigger scale factors, the luminosity and the $\eta$ and momentum binnings, contribute only marginally. No parameter exhibits a significant pull or an anomalously strong constraint, as expected from a fit to the Asimov dataset. The ordering varies only mildly from one mass point to the next, indicating that the sensitivity is not driven by any single systematic effect but limited by the background normalisation, and therefore ultimately by the statistical precision of the prediction in the high-mass sidebands.
