\section{Motivations for the Run--3 analysis}

The theoretical motivation and phenomenology for searching for HSCPs have been established in \cref{ch:chapitre3}. This section aims to describe how these theoretical predictions are translated into measurable quantities within the CMS detector and to describe why the combination of sub-detectors makes this search feasible.

HSCP searches differ from most analyses conducted within CMS in that they do not focus on particle decays. There is no reconstruction of resonance or final-state object cascades. Instead, the signature remains conceptually straightforward as it is inherent in the candidate track itself. The methodology relies on the Bethe-Bloch formula for energy loss: particles with low $\beta\gamma$ values are more likely to ionise, and since HSCPs are highly massive, they are produced with low $\beta\gamma$ across nearly the entire accessible momentum range. Consequently, a heavy candidate produces a track characterised by both high momentum and anomalously high ionisation, and the combination of these measurements yields a reconstructed mass well beyond the Standard Model spectrum.

The straightforward nature of the HSCP signature is reflected in the analytical tools employed. The analysis utilises a limited set of direct track-level selection criteria, foregoing multivariate or machine-learning classifiers. Background estimation is performed directly from data using the ABCD method. This intentional simplicity enhances transparency, reduces dependence on simulation, and facilitates validation of each step in the background prediction process.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

This search is motivated by the limitations identified in the Run--2 analysis predecessor~\cite{HSCP_Run2}. The previous analysis relied on muon triggers, making it insensitive to a significant fraction of R-hadrons that flip charge within the detector and thus do not produce a muon signature, even though they leave a fully reconstructed ionising track in the tracker. Additionally, the detector acceptance was confined to the central region, restricting sensitivity to the highest masses, where the expected signal yield is minimal. The current analysis addresses these limitations by: triggering on missing transverse energy (MET), thereby recovering the neutral-escape topology missed by muon-based selections; extending the detector acceptance to the full pseudo-rapidity range to ensure no signal track is excluded for geometrical reasons; and utilising the larger Run--3 dataset, collected at a higher centre-of-mass energy, to enhance sensitivity to higher masses. Collectively, these improvements result in a search that is both more comprehensive and methodologically streamlined than its predecessor.


\section{Analysis strategy}

The analysis targets specifically R-hadrons produced via gluino pair production. The experimental signature consists of a high-momentum, highly ionising track accompanied by MET. This missing energy may originate from intrinsic signal properties, mis-reconstruction of these particles, or detector effects. Unlike the Run--2 analysis, which relied on the muon system, the current search employs MET triggers to recover a substantial fraction of the signal that was missed by the muon-based selection. Tracker acceptance is extended to its full range compared to the previous search.

Events are selected based on passing MET triggers and satisfying track-level quality criteria. For each candidate, mass is reconstructed by combining track momentum with ionisation energy loss measured in the tracker. An authentic signal manifests as an excess at large reconstructed mass, while Standard Model tracks are concentrated at low mass.

Background estimation is performed directly from data. The relevant phase space is partitioned using candidate $p_T$ and a dedicated discriminating variable that separates signal-like from background-like tracks. This approach defines a blinded signal region, a signal-free control region, and an intermediate validation region. The expected background mass distribution in the signal region is inferred from the control region. The method is validated in the intermediate region, and systematic uncertainties are propagated to the predicted spectrum. Comparison of observed and predicted mass spectra yields exclusion limits on the HSCP production cross section as a function of mass.

Within this analysis, my contributions included studying MET trigger efficiency and the associated trigger strategy, developing the data-driven background estimation method, and deriving the exclusion limits. The event selection closely follows the Run--2 analysis, while simulated samples were managed by other members of the international team.


\section{Datasets}

The data analysed were collected in 2024 at a centre-of-mass energy of $\sqrt{s}=13.6$~TeV and are divided into seven eras (C through I), as recommended by the CMS Physics Performance and Dataset Project (PPD) for proton-proton analysis, out of the ten recorded eras (A through J). The analysis considers events that pass a MET trigger and are stored in the MET dataset. The total integrated luminosity is 110\,fb$^{-1}$.

The signal samples (\cref{tab:signal_samples}) comprise pair-produced gluinos that hadronise into R-hadrons, generated with \textsc{MadGraph} at $\sqrt{s}=13.6$\,TeV for ten mass hypotheses ranging from 1.1 to 2.6\,TeV. The theoretical production cross section, computed at NNLO+NNLL \cite{10.21468/SciPostPhysCore.7.4.072, Fiaschi_2023}, decreases as the gluino mass increases. This mass range surpasses the exclusion limits established by the previous search of the CMS collaboration ($m_{\tilde{g}}^\textrm{lim}=2.08$~TeV)~\cite{HSCP_Run2}.

The main background, after the analysis pre-selection, primarily consists of high-$p_T$ muons produced in addition to MET. The dominant processes are $W$ boson production in association with jets in the muonic decay channel, $W(\to\mu\nu)+\text{jets}$; top-quark pair production in the semi- and di-leptonic channel, $t\bar{t}\to \ell \nu qq$ and $t\bar{t}\to 2\ell 2\nu$. Additionally, QCD multijet production is modelled using twelve slices of muon-enriched samples spanning $\hat{p}_T$\footnote{$\hat{p}_T$ is the transverse momentum of the outgoing partons in the hard scattering at generator level. QCD samples are binned in $\hat{p}_T$ because the cross section falls steeply with $\hat{p}_T$} from 15~GeV to above 1~TeV. These MC samples, listed in \cref{tab:mc_samples_2024}, are primarily utilised to guide the HSCP pre-selection stage. These samples are not incorporated into the background prediction method.

All signal and background MC samples are reweighted to match the 2024 luminosity, from which the corresponding weight is given in the tables and calculated as follows:
\begin{equation}
    w = \frac{\sigma \times \mathcal{L}}{\rm Total\,\,number\,\,of\,\,events} \, .
\end{equation}

\section{d\textit{E}/d\textit{x} discriminators}

\subsection{The d\textit{E}/d\textit{x} pixel discriminator \texorpdfstring{$F_{\text{pixel}}$}{Fpixel}}

In this analysis, the pixel information is exploited via $F_{\text{pixel}}$, which quantifies the compatibility of a track with a MIP deposit. The pixel cluster shape and the charge distribution of MIPs are modelled from \texttt{PixelAV} simulations~\cite{1495807}, which reproduce charge deposition, transport, and collection processes in pixel sensors. 
These simulations are periodically tuned to data using large-$\eta$ tracks to provide angle-dependent models that yield different Vavilov function $V(Q)$ templates to model the charge distribution of MIPs.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

For a given pixel cluster carrying a charge $Q_{\text{cluster}}$, we define the tail probability 
\begin{equation}
p_Q = \int_{Q_{\text{cluster}}}^{+\infty} V(Q)\,\mathrm{d}Q,
\end{equation}
which quantifies the probability of depositing a charge greater than $Q_{\text{cluster}}$ for a MIP (see~\cref{fig:clustQ}). According to the Probability Integral Transform theorem~\cite{PITtheorem}, if $Q_{\text{cluster}}$ follows a Vavilov distribution $V(Q)$, then $p_Q$ is uniformly distributed on the interval [0,1]. In other words, assuming the particle is a MIP and the model $V(Q)$ is accurate, $p_Q$ is uniformly distributed by construction.

To complete the description of the four (three) layers of the barrel (endcap) pixel subdetector, a joint coefficient is built using the different tail probabilities $p_{Q_i}$ associated with the charge deposits $Q_{\text{cluster}_i}$ in each pixel layer $i$:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_n = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_{Q_{\text{cluster}_i}},
\end{equation}

The quantity $\alpha_n$ represents the joint probability of simultaneously observing charges at least as important in all layers: if a single layer is strongly ionising, $\alpha_n$ decreases; and if all layers are, $\alpha_n$ becomes significantly smaller.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.8\textwidth]{Images/Ch4/vavilov_charge.pdf}
    \caption{Example of charge deposits in pixel sensors at $\eta=2.40$, with the distribution (in black) fitted using a Vavilov function (in red). The tail distribution $p_Q$ is shown in filled grey.}
    \label{fig:clustQ}
\end{figure}

A difficulty arises because $\alpha_n$ is not uniformly distributed, even for a MIP. Its distribution depends strongly on $n$, which prevents a meaningful comparison between, for instance, the $n=3$ and $n=4$ cases. A different variable must therefore be constructed to obtain a behaviour subject to universal selection.

To do so, the variable $-\ln(\alpha_n)$ is taken as it follows a Gamma distribution with parameters $(n,1)$, if and only if each $p_{Q_{\text{cluster}_i}}$ is uniformly distributed on $[0,1]$. The corresponding probability density function is then
\begin{equation}
\Gamma(n,1) = \frac{x^{\,n-1} e^{-x}}{(n-1)!}, \qquad x = -\ln(\alpha_n).
\end{equation}

The associated cumulative distribution function (CDF) $\beta_n$ can be expressed analytically as
\begin{equation}
\beta_n = \alpha_n \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} \frac{\left[-\ln(\alpha_n)\right]^m}{m!}.
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Since $\beta_n$ is the exact CDF of $\alpha_n$ under the MIP hypothesis, the Probability Integral Transform theorem guarantees that $\beta_n$ is uniformly distributed on [0,1] for MIPs, and the $n$-dependence that affected the raw coefficient $\alpha_n$ is fully absorbed into its CDF. This restores the uniformity required for a universal selection.

We therefore define:
\begin{equation}
    F_{\text{pixel}} = 1 - \beta_n \, ,
\end{equation}
which is also uniformly distributed on $[0,1]$ for MIPs, regardless of the number of pixel layers considered.

However, for strongly ionising particles, such as gluinos, the charge deposited in each layer exceeds that expected for a MIP. This leads to small $p_{Q_i}$ values, hence to a small $\alpha_n$ and a $\beta_n$ close to zero, so that $F_{\text{pixel}}$ approaches unity.

The variable $F_{\text{pixel}}$ therefore constitutes an effective discriminator between Standard Model and BSM processes.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

In this analysis, hits from the first layer of the pixel barrel detector were excluded from the computation of $F_{\text{pixel}}$ due to premature ageing caused by high radiation levels in this layer of the Phase--1 detector. \cref{fig:FpixelInSignalData} shows the $F_{\text{pixel}}$ distribution for data collected in 2024 (left), in a region free from signal contamination (all analysis pre-selection criteria, listed in \cref{tab:preselection}, and $p_T < 55$~GeV). The $F_{\text{pixel}}$ distribution for a simulated HSCP gluino at $m_{\tilde{g}}=2000$~GeV after the analysis pre-selection criteria (right) shows that most of the signal is concentrated around unity.

Assuming the data distribution is perfectly flat when it is not introduces systematic uncertainties. These uncertainties are estimated by varying the aforementioned probability models and will be discussed in~\cref{subsec:systSignal}.

\subsection{The d\textit{E}/d\textit{x} strip estimator \texorpdfstring{$I_h$}{Ih}}

The search for HSCPs relies on tracking anomalous d$E$/d$x$ deposits within detector materials. The average energy deposit per track across the silicon tracker layers is quantified using the $I_h$ estimator, presented in \cref{sec:ihpres}. Owing to their greater mass, HSCPs generate higher energy deposits than MIPs produced by SM processes. MIPs exhibit nearly constant ionisation, resulting in low $I_h$ values for SM tracks, whereas HSCPs deposit substantially more charge per unit length and occupy the high-$I_h$ region. Therefore, $I_h$ enables effective discrimination between SM processes and HSCPs. This approach is complementary to the pixel-based estimator $F_{\text{pixel}}$, which deal with the charge distribution in the pixel layers, whereas $I_h$ aggregates ionisation measurements across the strip tracker.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\cref{fig:IhSignalData} demonstrates this separation. The upper panel displays the normalised $I_h$ distribution after applying analysis pre-selection criteria (see \cref{tab:preselection}), with an additional restrictive cut of $F_{\text{pixel}}<0.9$ applied to the data (left) to mitigate signal contamination, and for the signal (right). The data distribution exhibits a sharp peak at low ionisation, approximately $3.2$~MeV/cm, consistent with the MIP expectation, and decreases rapidly at higher values. In contrast, a gluino with $m_{\tilde{g}}=2000$~GeV gives rise to higher $\langle\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x\rangle$ values. The lower panel shows the corresponding cumulative distribution functions: the data CDF rises to unity almost immediately after the MIP level, while the gluino CDF increases gradually, highlighting the strong separation between the two populations across most of the $I_h$ range.

\subsection{Stability of d\textit{E}/d\textit{x} variables over the year}

The stability of the two ionisation observables used in this analysis is monitored throughout 2024 by computing, for each run, the mean values of $I_h$ and $F_\text{pixel}$ for tracks that pass the analysis pre-selection. The evolution of $\langle I_h \rangle$ as a function of run number is presented in \cref{fig:dedx_stability} (top left), along with the distribution of the per-run means (top right). Following the application of the charge calibration described in \cref{app:calibration}, the strip-based estimator remains stable over the year. The distribution of the mean is centred around $\langle I_h \rangle \approx 3.37$~MeV/cm, with a spread at the per cent level for tracks that pass the analysis pre-selection step.

A similar monitoring procedure is applied to the pixel-based discriminant (bottom row). The distribution of the mean of $F_\text{pixel}$ is centred around 0.65 for tracks that pass the analysis pre-selection step, with a relative dispersion of a few percent. Because $F_\text{pixel}$ is constructed from the pixel charge templates, the slight decrease observed over the year reflects the progressive degradation of the pixel sensors due to irradiation, which reduces the collected charge. However, this drift remains modest compared to the width of the $F_\text{pixel}$ distribution, allowing the variable to be used consistently throughout the 2024 dataset.

These checks confirm that both ionisation variables can be used consistently over the full 2024 dataset without any time-dependent correction beyond the charge calibration already applied.

\section{Selecting signal-like candidates}
\label{sec:HSCPpresel}

\subsection{Trigger event selection}
\label{sec:trigger}

The Run--3 analysis targets primarily hadron-like HSCPs, for which the \dedx-based single-muon trigger strategy adopted in Run--2 is not optimal. R-hadrons travel slowly within the detector and may reach the muon chambers too late to be reconstructed in the event. The $\beta=v/c$ distributions for different signal mass points illustrate this behaviour: although these particles can be produced with high momentum, their substantial mass reduces their velocity, resulting in distribution cores located at $\beta\approx XXXX$ (see \cref{fig:betaHSCP} left). Although HSCPs move more slowly than muons, when a generated gluino is matched with a reconstructed track from the event, over 99\% are tagged as PF muons, while less than 1\% are tagged as PF charged hadrons (these are the only two PF candidates observed). 

To evaluate the performance of a muon-based trigger, such as \texttt{HLT\_Mu50} (which requires $p_T^\mu>50$~GeV at the HLT trigger level), the fraction of generated gluino events passing this trigger relative to the total number of events can be examined as a function of $\beta$. This relationship is shown in \cref{fig:betaHSCP} (right), where the turn-on point is reached at $\beta\approx XXXX$. By comparing these distributions, it is evident that using a muon trigger significantly reduces the signal selection efficiency, thereby diminishing the sensitivity of this analysis.

This observation should be compared with the pair composition of R-hadrons at the generator level. The fractions of neutral-neutral, neutral-charged, and charged-charged R-hadrons are presented in \cref{fig:HSCPfractionPair}. 

At the generator level, nearly all signal events contain two generated HSCPs, distributed among the neutral-neutral, charged-neutral, and charged-charged pair types with respective fractions of approximately 30\%, 50\%, and 20\% (black dots in \cref{fig:HSCPfractionPair}). After requiring a track match between the generated HSCPs and the event-reconstructed tracks, the observed 0-, 1-, and 2-matched-track fractions are 41\%, 46\%, and 14\%, respectively, for each pair-produced category. The 1-matched-track fraction closely follows the generated charged-neutral fraction, while the 2-matched-track fraction is lower than the generated charged-charged fraction (14\% compared to 20\%). Conversely, the 0-matched-track fraction exceeds the generated neutral-neutral fraction (41\% compared to 30\%). This shift toward lower matched-track multiplicities suggests that HSCPs are predominantly lost due to charge flipping of a charged R-hadron to a neutral state. Given this effect, combined with the slow-moving nature of these particles, selecting events based solely on track information, such as muon identification using \texttt{HLT\_Mu50}, would significantly reduce the signal selection efficiency.

Thus, the signal selection will rely on the momentum imbalance induced by pair production or initial-state radiations (ISRs), which both generate missing transverse energy. Consequently, a dedicated MET trigger strategy was developed, combining four HLT paths:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{HLT\_PFMET120\_PFMHT120\_IDTight},
    \item \texttt{HLT\_PFMETNoMu120\_PFMHTNoMu120\_IDTight\_PFHT60},
    \item \texttt{HLT\_PFHT500\_PFMET100\_PFMHT100\_IDTight},
    \item \texttt{HLT\_MET105\_IsoTrk50}.
\end{itemize}

The naming convention of each HLT path directly encodes the online-like cuts applied at that stage: the algorithm prefix (\texttt{PFMET}, \texttt{PFMHT}, \texttt{PFHT} --- in \cref{subsubsec:METdef} --- defined and \texttt{IsoTrk}) identifies the reconstructed quantity being thresholded, while the number gives the corresponding $p_T$ (or $H_T$) cut in GeV. The MET prefix refers to the L1 Calo MET variable. \texttt{IDTight} signifies that tight jet identification is applied to the jets entering the MHT and MET sum, \texttt{NoMu} indicates that muon contributions are subtracted from the PF MET/MHT computation, and \texttt{IsoTrk50} requires an isolated track with $p_T>50$~GeV. 

At the L1 trigger level, the seed lists consist of a logical OR of several \texttt{L1\_ETMHF} thresholds that compute MET using both the central and forward calorimeters. Because \texttt{ETMHF} is derived from calorimeter data, the initial L1 decision is predominantly determined by the Calo MET, while the PF MET/MHT becomes the primary discriminating variable only at the HLT stage. Additionally, an HLT filter on Calo MET is applied at the HLT level for the first three triggers.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\cref{fig:HSCPfractionPairTrigger} (left) shows the individual MET trigger efficiency, the logical OR combination of these triggers, and the muon trigger. It is apparent that MET triggers select events more efficiently than muon triggers.

\cref{fig:HSCPfractionPairTrigger} presents the HSCP-generated pair-type distribution (black dots, identical to \cref{fig:HSCPfractionPair}) following the application of MET triggers (red squares) and, for comparison, the \texttt{HLT\_Mu50} triggers (dark yellow crosses) for the $m_{\tilde{g}}=2000$~GeV point. The reduction from the generated composition to the post MET trigger composition remains essentially uniform across all three pair-type categories: summing the red squares over the three categories recovers the same $\sim$30\% efficiency obtained for the $m_{\tilde{g}}=2000$~GeV point of \cref{fig:HSCPfractionPairTrigger} (left). The efficiency is lower than that obtained after applying the muon trigger, which retains $\approx XXXX\%$ of the signal, because only events containing a charged HSCP and an associated track are selected.

Consequently, MET triggers are designated for this analysis, as they demonstrate the highest efficiency for the relevant R-hadron topologies.

\subsubsection{HSCP MET topologies}

The MET signature arising from the distinct properties of R-hadrons deserves further investigation, as PF MET and Calo MET measurements vary with the specific production topology.

PUppi MET, the Run 3 recommended MET estimator, which is similar to PF MET but incorporates more robust Pile-Up mitigation, will be used in place of PF MET. PUppi MET is only produced when the R-hadron pair consists of one charged and one neutral state, since a charged-charged or neutral-neutral pair leaves no net $p_T$ imbalance. In contrast, an ISR gluon, which would hadronise into a jet, or a photon recoiling against the pair production, produces ECAL or HCAL deposits. Since charged or neutral HSCPs leave minimal calorimeter information, this results in an imbalance in the calorimeter towers and consequently leads to non-zero Calo MET. Five kinematic topologies, depending on the nature of the pair-produced HSCP, were identified on this basis and are illustrated in \cref{fig:METtopology}, ranging from configurations with negligible MET to those dominated by either PUppi or Calo MET, thereby motivating the use of several complementary MET observables rather than a single variable.

\subsubsection{MET Trigger strategy}

The trigger decision relies on online quantities, specifically Calo MET at the L1 level and both Calo MET and PF MET at the HLT level, which are reconstructed with reduced precision. In contrast, the analysis selection employs their offline counterparts, namely Calo MET and PUppi MET. The finite-resolution difference between these quantities causes efficiency to increase gradually rather than exhibit a step function at the nominal threshold. The width and position of this efficiency rise are determined by the online resolution and the calibration of the online objects, neither of which is precisely reproduced in simulation. Consequently, even a small offset between data and simulation can result in a discrepancy in efficiency where the curve is steep, making it unreliable to predict the number of signal events accepted by the trigger from simulation within the Calo MET or PUppi MET range. Beyond the turn-on, in the \textit{trigger plateau}, the efficiency saturates and becomes flat. In this regime, the ratio of the trigger efficiency in data to that in simulation, expressed as a function of one of the MET variables and known as the \textit{scale factor} (SF), is constant. Regions where the SFs are large and approximately constant are selected to characterise the trigger response. In contrast, small or non-constant SFs suggest a strong dependence of the data-to-simulation correction on the MET variables, rendering the correction of signal selection efficiency from simulation unreliable.

This requirement is especially critical for the HSCP analysis, where, if no excess is observed, limits on the theoretical model are computed using a shape analysis. A flat plateau efficiency multiplies the mass spectrum by a constant, affecting only its overall normalisation and leaving both the signal shape and the data-driven background prediction unchanged. However, without scale-factor corrections and a selection applied to the plateau, the signal's accuracy near the turn-on point is uncertain, leading to uncertainty in counting signal events and, consequently, to unreliable exclusion limits.

The adopted strategy follows directly from these considerations. The turn-on curves are first measured in parallel in data and simulation as functions of the offline PUppi MET and Calo MET, and the corresponding data-to-simulation scale factors are extracted. The offline thresholds are then set sufficiently high to place the analysis close to the trigger plateau, where these scale factors are constant and well controlled.


\subsubsection{MET trigger efficiency}

With the transition of the HSCP analysis from the AOD to the \textsc{MiniAOD} framework for Run-3 data, several variables have been modified. Notably, in \textsc{MiniAOD}, the offline calculation of Calo MET now includes muon energy deposits in the calorimeters, introducing a bias in the value. Since trigger efficiency is determined online at the L1 and HLT levels using Calo MET, which differs from its offline value in \textsc{MiniAOD}, it is not possible to evaluate trigger efficiency solely based on Calo MET. To recover the MET deposits within the calo towers, one could then build a proxy observable that mimics the AOD Calo MET. Therefore, a pseudo-MET variable is reconstructed offline from the calorimeter jet (CaloJets) collection as the magnitude of the vectorial transverse-momentum sum of all calorimeter jets in the event,
\begin{equation}
   \mathrm{Pseudo~MET} = \sqrt{\left\|\sum_{\text{CaloJets}} \overrightarrow{p_{x_j}}\right\|^2 + \left\|\sum_{\text{CaloJets}} \overrightarrow{p_{y_j}} \right\|^2 } .
\end{equation}
The comparison between AOD Calo MET and \textsc{MiniAOD} Pseudo MET was validated on the same Run--3 MC dataset, which showed a similar distribution after the analysis pre-selection step. Since Run--3 corrections for the CaloJet collection have not yet been established by the CMS Jet Energy Resolution and Corrections (JERC) group, the Pseudo MET value remains uncorrected. This discrepancy is not addressed in the trigger efficiency analysis because the Pseudo MET is used solely to identify the trigger plateau and to extract the corresponding scale factors, which depend on the shape of the turn-on curve rather than on absolute energy calibration. Nevertheless, applying an offline selection to this uncalibrated variable presents significant challenges.

\subsubsection{Trigger strategy adopted}

The trigger's response to pair-produced HSCPs depends on their electric charge. This study presents a strategy for selecting HSCP gluinos using MET triggers to address the imbalance among the three production scenarios: charged-charged, neutral-charged, and neutral-neutral. Strategy (2) provides an effective compromise for signal acceptance, given that the uncalibrated Pseudo MET variable requires additional caution. Events are selected using a PUppi MET threshold greater than 150~GeV after passing one of the four MET triggers, with additional checks on the $H_T$, MHT, and MHT$^\mathrm{NoMu}$ variables. The uncertainty in trigger efficiency is managed using scale factors, defined as the ratio of trigger efficiencies in data and MC simulations from orthogonal datasets that produce similar MET and signal signatures. The upward and downward variations of these scale factors will subsequently be applied as systematic uncertainties in the trigger selection, as discussed in \cref{sec:systematics}.


\subsection{HSCP candidates selection}
\label{sec:preselection}

Before background estimation, a set of offline requirements is imposed on events and tracks to select HSCP candidates. The primary sources of such tracks in data are $W(\to \mu\nu)$+jets, $t\bar{t}\to 2\ell 2\nu$ events, and QCD multijet $\mu$-enriched production, where a high $p_T$ muon can imitate the signature of a slow, highly ionising particle. The pre-selection aims to suppress these backgrounds while maintaining high signal efficiency. Final selection occurs in the $\left(p_T, F_\textrm{pixel}\right)$ plane, as defined in the ABCD method at the background estimation step, with region D serving as the signal region.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

At the event level, candidates must activate at least one of the MET trigger paths described in \cref{sec:trigger} and pass the standard MET noise filters, which remove events where missing transverse momentum arises from detector effects such as noisy calorimeter cells. An offline requirement of PUppi MET > 150~GeV is subsequently applied to ensure robust signal selection while maintaining scale factors close to unity, thereby preserving reliable simulation modelling.

Candidate tracks must satisfy $p_T > 50$~GeV and $|\eta| < 2.4$. The $p_T$ requirement is a mandatory selection to retain tracks with d$E$/d$x$ clusters in the \textsc{MiniAOD} datasets. This threshold minimally affects the signal, as $p/m = \beta\gamma$ implies that particles with masses of several hundred GeV possess momenta well above this limit, while the background spectrum decreases sharply. The pseudorapidity range matches the full tracker acceptance. Unlike the Run-2 analysis, which relied on a limited search to $|\eta| < 1.0$ (homogeneous silicon tracker and mostly muon DTs).

\vspace{0.5 cm}

A first group of criteria targets the quality of the track reconstruction and of the ionisation measurement:
\begin{itemize}
  \item at least two valid pixel hits are required, so that the $F_\textrm{pixel}$ discriminant is built from a minimum of two independent cluster probabilities;
  \item the fraction of valid hits, i.e.\ the fraction of hits compatible with the propagated trajectory among the crossed modules, must exceed 0.8, rejecting misreconstructed tracks;
  \item at least ten $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x$ measurements must enter the estimators, the first barrel pixel layer being excluded as it is the most exposed to radiation effects. For signal tracks, the number of measurements peaks around fifteen, so this requirement mainly removes short tracks;
  \item the track must carry the \textit{HighPurity} flag (see \cref{sec:final_track_fit}) and satisfy $\chi^{2}/N_{\mathrm{dof}} < 5$, the latter being largely redundant with the former but removing a residual population with anomalous fit quality.
\end{itemize}

Compatibility with the primary vertex is enforced through $|d_{z}| < 0.1$~cm and $|d_{xy}| < 0.02$~cm, restricting the search to promptly produced particles.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Two isolation criteria are applied to reject non-prompt leptons produced within jets, which are the primary sources of QCD and $t\overline{t}$ background contributions. The relative isolation, calculated from particle-flow candidates surrounding the track, must satisfy $I_\textrm{rel}^\textrm{PF} < 0.02$. Additionally, the tracker-based isolation $I_\textrm{trk}$, defined for all tracks within a cone of $\Delta R < 0.3$, must remain below 15~GeV (both isolations are defined in \cref{sec:muons}). As shown in the cutflow of table~\ref{tab:preselection}, the particle-flow isolation requirement is among the most effective offline discriminants in the data. This criterion reduces the selected sample by nearly an order of magnitude, while the associated signal loss remains at the percent level.

Due to the relativistic rise of the Bethe-Bloch curve, electrons in this region can produce ionisation signatures similar to HSCPs. However, electrons deposit nearly all their energy in the electromagnetic calorimeter. Therefore, imposing the requirement that the calorimetric energy be less than the track momentum ($E/p < 0.3$) effectively removes this background without affecting the signal. Tracks with poorly measured momentum, which may result from incorrectly assigned hits or hit sharing between nearby particles, are excluded by requiring $\sigma_{p_T}/p_T^{2} < 0.0008$~GeV$^{-1}$ and $\sigma_{p_T}/p_T < 1$. The latter criterion specifically addresses the high-momentum region, where the relative resolution deteriorates.

Two additional requirements are imposed directly on the ionisation estimators. Broken clusters, which occur when a long pixel cluster is split into multiple clusters in the readout chips or due to irradiation effects, bias the charge estimate toward lower values. Applying a cut at $F_\textrm{pixel} > 0.3$ removes these affected tracks, while the signal distribution peaks near unity. A minimum ionisation requirement of $I_h > C_\mathrm{mass}$ is also imposed, where $C_\mathrm{mass}$ is a parameter used in the subsequent mass reconstruction; its value sits near the MIP level ($\approx3$~MeV/cm).

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\cref{tab:preselection} presents the successive selection requirements and their cumulative efficiencies for the 2024 MET dataset and a pair-produced gluino sample with $m_{\tilde{g}} = 2000$~GeV. \cref{fig:cutflow} displays the corresponding cutflow in terms of the absolute number of events. The most significant reduction in signal after the trigger stage occurs at the $p_T>50$~GeV requirement, which is the first criterion applied directly to the candidate track. Events showing no tracks, particularly where both R-hadrons are neutral in the tracker, are excluded at this stage. The subsequent two isolation requirements further remove a related subset of events. Because none of the earlier criteria prevents a jet from being identified as an HSCP candidate, jets may be misidentified in events where the R-hadrons are neutral or not reconstructed; the isolation cuts then eliminate these jets. The pre-selection reduces the dataset by nearly four orders of magnitude while retaining $11.6\%$ of the signal. In comparison, the Run~2 analysis achieved a final efficiency of $4.8\%$ for the same mass point. The improvement by more than a factor of two is attributed to the revised trigger strategy, which no longer relies on reconstructing the HSCP as a muon, and to the extended pseudorapidity coverage enabled by this approach.

\section{Mass reconstruction strategy}




\section{Background estimation method}

The background estimation relied on the mass reconstruction of HSCP candidates. It allows prediction of the event yield in the signal region as well as the background mass spectrum. 

\subsection{Method description}

Background estimation is performed using the ABCD method, in which the phase space is partitioned according to the $F_\textrm{pixel}$ variable and transverse momentum $p_T$. Control (CR) and signal regions (SR) are established by applying selection criteria to these variables, which are assumed to be uncorrelated. \cref{fig:scheme_ABCDmass} illustrates the defined regions. The SR, denoted as region $D$, includes HSCP candidates with both high $p_T$ and high $F_\textrm{pixel}$ values. Regions $A$, $B$, and $C$ correspond to candidates with low $p_T$ and low $F_\textrm{pixel}$ values, to candidates with low $p_T$ and high $F_\textrm{pixel}$ values, and to candidates with high $p_T$ and low $F_\textrm{pixel}$ values, respectively.

Multiple $B$ and $D$ regions can be defined for application of the background prediction method. In addition to the signal regions, a validation region (VR), determined by region $D'$ with high $p_T$ and $0.8<F_\textrm{pixel}\leq0.9$, is utilised to assess the validity of the mass prediction.

Templates used to derive the predicted mass spectrum are obtained in a control region (CR). The signal region (SR) is defined as the region with high $F_\textrm{pixel}$ and $p_T$ values.}
  \label{fig:scheme_ABCDmass}

Distributions of background (represented by the MC) and signal events in the $F_\textrm{pixel}$ versus $p_T$ plane are presented in \cref{fig:2D_mc_fpix_pt}. A threshold of $F_\textrm{pixel} = 0.9$ is selected to achieve approximately 90\% signal efficiency while maintaining background rejection near 10\%. The Y-profile of the plane indicates that the average $p_T$ of the background remains constant at approximately 120~GeV, regardless of the $F_\textrm{pixel}$ value.

This method estimates the normalisation, defined as the number of events, in the signal region $D$ by utilising the event counts from control regions $A$, $B$, and $C$ as follows:
\begin{equation}
	{\rm yield}_D= {\rm yield}_B\times {\rm yield}_C/{\rm yield}_A. 
\label{eq:normalisation}
\end{equation}

Furthermore, the method predicts the shape of the mass distribution in the signal region $D$ using information from the $\langle\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x\rangle$ estimator $I_h$ and the inverse momentum $1/p$ distributions (to include high $p$ values) of the CR $C$.

An example of these templates is presented in \cref{fig:ih_oP_fit}. Fits are applied to the tails of these templates to minimise the influence of statistical fluctuations. The $I_h$ tail distribution is fitted with a Gaussian function, and the latter is used when the bin content is below 100 tracks and with an $I_h$ value of at least 10\% greater than the maximum. The $1/p$ initial tail distribution is fitted using an Error function for bins below 90\% of the maximum and is used when $1/p$ is less than 20\% of the maximum. For the latter, the fit exhibits a higher $\chi^2/N_\textrm{dof}$, primarily due to fluctuations near the distribution's maximum.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth,trim={0.2cm 0 1.5cm 0},clip]{Images/Ch4/Ih_newfit_bis.pdf}
  \includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth,trim={0.2cm 0 1.5cm 0},clip]{Images/Ch4/oP_newfit_bis.pdf}
  \caption{Templates of the $I_h$ (Left) and $1/p$ (Right) distributions extracted in the control region $C$ used for the mass prediction. The red lines represent the fits performed on the tail of these distributions, used to minimise the effect of statistical fluctuations.}
  \label{fig:ih_oP_fit}
\end{figure}

Because the $\eta$ distribution in the CR $C$ does not accurately represent the $\eta$ distribution in region $D$ (as shown in \cref{fig:eta_reweighting} (Left)), a rescaling procedure is implemented to align the $\eta$ distribution with that of the SR. The distribution in CR $C$ is modified as follows:
\begin{equation}
  \eta_C \rightarrow \eta_C \times \frac{\eta_B}{\eta_A} \, .
\end{equation}
These weights are subsequently applied to the $1/p$ distribution in CR $C$ to achieve consistency with the distribution observed in the signal region. \cref{fig:eta_reweighting} (Right) demonstrates the agreement between the reweighted distribution in CR $C$ and that in the VR.

		 \label{fig:eta_reweighting}
\end{figure}

Due to the correlation between $p$, $p_T$, and $\eta$ (and to a lesser extent between $I_h$ and $\eta$), the mass distribution is reconstructed in bins of $\eta$. To facilitate this, templates in the CR are constructed in two dimensions: ($1/p$, $\eta$) and ($\eta$, $I_h$). The $\eta$ distribution is divided into 24 bins ($\Delta_{\eta} = 0.1$), $I_h$ into 100 bins ($\Delta_{I_h} = 0.05~\mathrm{MeV/cm}$), and $1/p$ into 500 bins ($\Delta_{1/p} = 0.4$~GeV$^{-1}$).

The mass distribution is filled using a triple loop: the outer loop iterates over $\eta$ bins, the middle loop over $1/p$ bins, and the inner loop over $I_h$ bins. Within this structure, the indices ($i$, $j$, $k$) correspond to the ($\eta$, $1/p$, $I_h$) bin, where the relevant computations are performed:

\begin{itemize}
  \item the mass value $M_{i,j,k}$ as
  \begin{equation}
    M_{i,j,k} = p_{j,i} \times \sqrt{\frac{I_{h_{i,k}}-C_\mathrm{mass}}{K_\mathrm{mass}} }
  \end{equation}
  where $p_{j,i}$ is the momentum value taken as the inverse of the $1/p$ value in the $(j,i)$ bin of the $(1/p,\,\eta)$ template, and $I_{h_{i,k}}$ is the $\langle\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x\rangle$ value of the $(i,k)$ bin of the $(\eta,\,I_h)$ template;
  
  \item and the associated weight $w_{i,j,k}$
  \begin{equation}
    w_{i,j,k} = \left(\frac{n_{ji}}{\sum_m n_{mi}}\right) \cdot h_{ik}
  \end{equation}
  where $n_{ji}$ is the number of observed events in the $(j,i)$ bin of the $(1/p,\,\eta)$ distribution and $h_{ik}$ is the number of observed events in the $(i,k)$ bin of the $(\eta,\,I_h)$ distribution.
\end{itemize}

The mass distribution is then constructed and rescaled to the expected global normalisation given by equation (\ref{eq:normalisation}). Statistical uncertainty associated with the prediction is estimated using 200 pseudo-experiments. For each pseudo-experiment, new $I_h$ and $1/p$ templates are generated by randomly sampling each bin from a Poisson distribution with a mean equal to the yield of the corresponding original template bin. This randomisation is also applied to the yield in the CR used for global normalisation. The mean of the 200 pseudo-mass predictions serves as the central value for the mass prediction, while the RMS defines the statistical uncertainty envelope.


\subsection{Preliminary remarks on the mass reconstruction method}
\label{sec:abcd_remarks}

This method has been employed in several previous iterations of the HSCP analysis. However, multiple modifications have been implemented since the most recent CMS public results~\cite{HSCP_Run2}. The key changes are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A new mapping for the phase space, $F_\textrm{pixel}$, is employed in place of $G_\textrm{strip}$. The $G_\textrm{strip}$ estimator relies on d$E$/d$x$ deposits within strip sensors to determine whether a HSCP candidate is compatible with a track depositing at the MIP level. $G_\textrm{strip}$ increases exponentially towards 1 for events that are incompatible with the MIP. However, this estimator correlates with $p_T$: higher $p_T$ values yield greater d$E$/d$x$ deposits, introducing bias in mass-spectrum predictions.
  \item The fits of the $I_h$ and $1/p$ tail templates have been refined to accommodate the expanded eta acceptance. This adjustment is particularly significant for the $1/p$ distribution, where, in the endcap region ($|\eta|>1$), the shape differs at low $1/p$, leading to inaccurate mass calculations with previous fitting strategies.
  \item Eta reweighting is now conducted using all three alternative regions ($A$, $B$, and $C$) of the ABCD method, rather than relying solely on regions $B$ and $C$ as in the previous version of the analysis.
\end{itemize}

\section{Systematics}
\label{sec:systematics}

This strategy considers two primary types of systematic uncertainties for the final interpretation. These uncertainties may arise from variable calibration, methodological biases, or binning choices. Systematic uncertainties are incorporated into both the background prediction and the signal modelling.

\subsection{Systematics related to the background prediction}
\label{subsec:systBkgPred}

The background prediction is based on several methodological choices, including the binning of input distributions, the modelling of fit tail templates, and the assumption that the $1/p$ and $I_h$ distributions are independent of $F_\textrm{pixel}$. Each choice introduces a systematic uncertainty in the predicted mass spectrum. These uncertainties are quantified by repeating the full prediction procedure with modified configurations and comparing the resulting mass spectra to the nominal spectrum. For each source, both upward and downward variations are defined, and the bin-by-bin ratios between the varied and nominal spectra are used to determine the corresponding uncertainties. The considered sources are the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The binning of the $\eta$, $I_h$, and $10^{4}/p$ distributions used in the convolution employs nominal bin widths of $\Delta_{\eta} = 0.1$, $\Delta_{I_h} = 0.05~\mathrm{MeV/cm}$, and $\Delta_{1/p} = 0.4$~GeV$^{-1}$, respectively. For each variable, upward (downward) variation is implemented by doubling (halving) the nominal bin width, while all other aspects of the procedure remain unchanged.
    \item The fits used to model the tails of the $I_h$ and $1/p$ templates are varied by shifting the fitted parameters upward and downward by their respective uncertainties. The prediction is then recalculated using these modified templates.
    \item The method assumes that the $I_h$ and $1/p$ templates are independent of the $F_\textrm{pixel}$ variable. Violation of this assumption would invalidate the prediction of the shape in the SR, which is fundamental to the method. The residual correlation between $1/p$ and $F_\textrm{pixel}$ is addressed through the rescaling in $\eta$ shown in \cref{fig:eta_reweighting}. The correlation between $I_h$ and $F_\textrm{pixel}$ is evaluated by taking the ratio of the $I_h$ template in the CR to that in the VR (see \cref{fig:ih_correlation}). This ratio is fitted with a linear function, and the resulting bias is corrected in the $I_h$ template in the CR prior to mass spectrum construction. The systematic uncertainty is estimated by omitting this correction and comparing the resulting difference with the nominal mass spectrum distribution.
\end{itemize}

\end{figure}

The total systematic uncertainty is calculated as the quadratic sum of the individual sources. In each category, the binning variation of the $1/p$ templates constitutes the dominant contribution, increasing the total uncertainty to approximately $100\%$ in the highest-mass bins in the endcap region.

\subsection{Systematics related to the signal}
\label{subsec:systSignal}

Uncertainties in the simulation affect the signal predictions. These uncertainties are estimated using the same methodology as for the background. Each source is varied upward and downward, the complete analysis chain is rerun, and the bin-by-bin ratios between the varied and nominal mass spectra are used to quantify the corresponding uncertainties. The total systematic uncertainty is calculated as the quadratic sum of the individual sources. The considered sources are the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The uncertainty associated with the luminosity measurement is estimated to be 1.4\% for 2024~\cite{CMS-DP-2026-003}.
    \item The Pile-Up profile in the 2024 simulation is compared to the observed 2024 Pile-Up distribution. A shift of $\pm1\sigma$ is applied, and corresponding event weights are assigned in the simulation.
    \item Trigger scale factors related to the PUppi MET shown in \cref{fig:triggerSF} (left) are varied within their respective statistical uncertainties.
    \item The $K_\textrm{mass}$ and $C_\textrm{mass}$ calibration factors used in mass reconstruction are varied based on the observed differences between data and simulation. The upward and downward variations are defined as $K_\textrm{mass}^\textrm{MC} \pm |K_\textrm{mass}^\textrm{data} - K_\textrm{mass}^\textrm{MC}|$, and in the same way for $C_\textrm{mass}$.
    \item Jet energy uncertainties, provided by the CMS Jet MET working group, are propagated upward and downward in the computation of $H_T$, MHT, and MHT$^\mathrm{NoMu}$ for the trigger requirement, as well as for the PUppi MET.
    \item The value of $F_\textrm{pixel}$ is sensitive to pixel hit reconstruction, particularly to angle corrections coming from high-$p$ tracks, and the charge templates applied during reconstruction. To evaluate the impact of pixel calibration, a dedicated sample of 100,000 gluino events ($m_{\tilde{g}} = 1800$~GeV) was simulated using an updated Global Tag\footnote{A Global Tag serves as a versioned identifier that defines the coherent set of calibration, alignment, and detector-condition records utilised by CMSSW during dataset processing.} that reflects pixel conditions at the end of 2024, following significant irradiation, and processed with the standard analysis chain. \cref{fig:fi_syst} presents the $F_\textrm{pixel}$ distributions for the nominal and systematic samples, after the analysis pre-selection step. The ratio of yields in the region $F_\textrm{pixel} > 0.9$ between the two samples is $1.016$ after pre-selection. The difference of $1.6\%$ is symmetrised, and the corresponding variations are implemented by selecting HSCP candidates in the Signal region such that $F_\textrm{pixel} \cdot (1 \pm 0.016) > 0.9$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Statistical interpretation and expected limits}
\label{sec:limits}

The background prediction derived in the previous sections is combined with signal mass distributions to construct a statistical model from which upper limits on the gluino pair-production cross section are extracted. Because the signal region remains blinded at this stage of the analysis, the observed data entering the model are replaced by the background expectation, and all results presented below are expected limits. These expected limits serve two purposes: they quantify the sensitivity of the search prior to unblinding and inform the selection of the acceptance configuration and extraction method for the final interpretation.

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{CL$_\text{s}$}{CLs} method}
\label{sec:limits:cls}

Upper limits are determined using the modified frequentist CL$_\text{s}$ method~\cite{CLSpaper}. This approach represents the standard procedure for searches at the LHC. Two hypotheses are considered: the background-only hypothesis $b$, in which the data are fully described by known processes, and the signal-plus-background hypothesis $s+b$, in which a gluino contribution of strength $\mu$ is included in addition to the background. The comparison between these hypotheses is based on the profile likelihood ratio test statistic, defined as follows:
\begin{equation}
    q_\mu = -2 \ln \frac{\mathcal{L}\left(\text{data} \,\middle|\, \mu s + b,\, \hat{\hat{\theta}}_\mu\right)}{\mathcal{L}\left(\text{data} \,\middle|\, \hat{\mu} s + b,\, \hat{\theta}\right)},
    \qquad 0 \leq \hat{\mu} \leq \mu\,,
    \label{eq:limits:qmu}
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ represents the set of nuisance parameters that encode systematic uncertainties. The parameters $\hat{\mu}$ and $\hat{\theta}$ globally maximise the likelihood, while $\hat{\hat{\theta}}_\mu$ maximises the likelihood for a fixed signal strength $\mu$. The distributions of $q_\mu$ under the two hypotheses determine the corresponding $p$-values: $\text{CL}_{s+b}$ is the probability, under the signal-plus-background hypothesis, of obtaining a value of $q_\mu$ at least as large as the observed value. Similarly, $\text{CL}_b$ is the probability computed under the background-only hypothesis. The quantity CL$_\text{s}$ is defined as the ratio of the $p$-values for the two hypotheses, $\textrm{CL$_\text{s}$} = \text{CL}_{s+b}/\text{CL}_b$. This construction prevents the exclusion of signal hypotheses that the analysis lacks sensitivity to. The $95\%$ confidence level upper limit on the cross section corresponds to the value of $\mu$ for which $\textrm{CL$_\text{s}$} = 0.05$.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

All limits in this section are computed using the asymptotic approximation~\cite{AsymptoticPaper}, where the distributions of $q_\mu$ under both hypotheses assume analytic forms. The median expected limit, along with its $\pm1\sigma$ and $\pm2\sigma$ bands, is derived from a single Asimov dataset\footnote{The Asimov dataset is an artificial construct in which each observable is assigned its expected value under the background-only hypothesis. This approach eliminates statistical fluctuations, enabling a single evaluation of the test statistic to reproduce the median expected result.}. This approach eliminates the need for pseudo-experiment generation and reduces computation time to a few minutes per mass hypothesis, in contrast to several hours required for parallel toy generation in the full CL$_\text{s}$ construction. Limit extraction is performed using the \textsc{Combine} package developed by the CMS collaboration~\cite{CombinePaper}.

\subsection{Construction of the statistical models}
\label{sec:limits:models}

This study compares two statistical approaches to analysing the mass spectrum, both constructed from identical inputs: a counting experiment within a defined mass window, referred to as the \textit{cut-and-count method}, and a binned maximum-likelihood fit of the entire spectrum, known as \textit{shape analysis}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

In the cut-and-count method, a mass window is defined for each signal hypothesis as $[\mu_m - \sigma_m,\, \mu_m + 2\sigma_m]$, where $\mu_m$ and $\sigma_m$ represent the mean and standard deviation of the reconstructed mass distribution for a signal of mass $m$. The lower edge of the window is constrained to remain above $300$~GeV to avoid the region with the highest background. The asymmetry of the window relative to $\mu_m$ accounts for the steeply falling background: extending the window downward increases the background rate significantly more than the signal, while the upper side remains nearly free of background. Signal and background yields are calculated as the integrals of their respective spectra within the defined window. The statistical model is thereby reduced to a single-bin Poisson counting experiment. Systematic uncertainties are included as log-normal nuisance parameters, with scaling factors determined by integrating the systematically varied templates over the same window and normalising to the nominal yield. For two-sided variations, the larger deviation is retained to symmetrise the uncertainty, whereas one-sided variations are represented by a single multiplicative factor.

The shape analysis incorporates the full mass spectrum into the likelihood. Spectra are rebinned to the same adaptive binning as for the background estimation method. Each systematic uncertainty is represented by a pair of alternative templates, which \textsc{Combine} interpolates continuously; for one-sided uncertainties, the same template is used for both directions. Background normalisation is left unconstrained in the fit via a free rate parameter for each channel. Consequently, the limit uses the predicted background shape, while its normalisation is determined in situ from the mass spectrum. Statistical uncertainties of the templates are incorporated on a bin-by-bin basis using the Barlow-Beeston lite method~\cite{Cranmer:1456844}.

\subsection{Acceptance configurations}
\label{sec:limits:categories}

The extension of track acceptance from $|\eta|<1$ to $|\eta|<2.4$ necessitates consideration of the endcap region within the statistical model. Three eta regimes are evaluated: the first utilises only the central region ($|\eta|<1$), the second treats the full acceptance ($|\eta|<2.4$) as a single inclusive channel, and the third configuration incorporates the central and endcap ($1\leq|\eta|<2.4$) regions as two distinct channels. Signal templates, background predictions, and all systematic variations are generated independently for each regime, and the channels are combined in a unified likelihood. All nuisance parameters are correlated between the channels, reflecting their derivation from the same dataset, calibration, and prediction framework. The background rate parameters are the only exceptions, allowed to vary independently in each channel.

For each configuration and extraction method, a datacard is generated, and for the shape analysis, the corresponding template file is produced for the gluino mass hypotheses spanning $1100$ to $2600$~GeV. The derived limits on signal strength are subsequently converted into cross section limits using NNLO+NNLL gluino pair-production cross sections at $\sqrt{s}=13.6$~TeV~\cite{10.21468/SciPostPhysCore.7.4.072, Fiaschi_2023}.

The six configurations are compared directly in \cref{fig:limits:compare}, together with the observed limit computed using the full CL$_\textrm{s}$ in the previous HSCP search~\cite{HSCP_Run2}. Three observations emerge from this comparison.

Shape analysis performs at least as well as the cut-and-count method in all configurations. However, the magnitude of improvement varies significantly: in the central region (black) and the split configuration (green), both methods yield nearly identical results, while shape analysis demonstrates a clear advantage for the inclusive tracker acceptance (red). This behaviour is attributed to differences in the background composition of the mass windows. For the $2000$~GeV mass hypothesis, the central window contains $12.70$ expected signal events and only $0.03$ background events, resulting in a background-free regime in which the limit is determined solely by the signal yield, and the event distribution within the window provides no additional information. In contrast, the endcap region contains $4.60$ signal events and $10.04$ background events. When both regions are combined into a single inclusive channel, the endcap background contaminates the otherwise pure central sample, reducing the performance of the counting experiment. Shape analysis partially mitigates this loss, as the background is concentrated at low mass while the signal is distributed in the upper part of the window.

These results clarify why the split configuration consistently outperforms the inclusive configuration, independent of the extraction method. In the inclusive channel, increased endcap background reduces the purity of the central region. Dividing the acceptance into two distinct channels mitigates this effect, as the likelihood function assigns weights to each region based on its purity. The central channel, which is nearly background-free, determines the exclusion limit, while the endcap channel, with a signal-to-background ratio below one half, contributes minimally. Consequently, the exclusion limit aligns with that of the central channel alone at the studied mass points, demonstrating that sensitivity to centrally produced gluino signals is dominated by the $|\eta|<1$ region.

A comparison with the previous HSCP result shows that the expected reach of this analysis is $2.42$~TeV, while the Run--2 search excluded up to $2.08$~TeV at a similar integrated luminosity. However, this comparison requires caution: the earlier result is an observed limit derived using the full CL$_\text{s}$ method and $13$~TeV cross sections, whereas the current values are expected limits based on the asymptotic approximation, which may be optimistic in low-count scenarios, and are compared to the slightly higher $13.6$~TeV cross sections. Nevertheless, the improvement is significant and is primarily due to the increased signal efficiency of the MET trigger strategy implemented in this analysis.

\subsection{Impacts of the nuisance parameters}
\label{sec:limits:impacts}

Each source of uncertainty is evaluated by quantifying the impact of the nuisance parameters on the Asimov dataset using the splitting configuration (barrel+endcap) in the shape analysis. The impact of a nuisance parameter is defined as the change in the fitted signal strength when the parameter is varied by one standard deviation of its post-fit uncertainty around its best-fit value, while all other parameters are profiled. \cref{fig:limits:impacts} presents the ranked impacts of the four highest-mass hypotheses that determine the exclusion reach.

For each parameter, the left panel presents the pull and post-fit constraint, while the right panel displays the impact on the signal strength for a $\pm1\sigma$ variation.}
    \label{fig:limits:impacts}

The highest-ranked uncertainties originate from two primary sources. The most significant impacts are linked to the trigger scale factors applied to the expected signal yield. These factors rank first at the $2000$~GeV point and remain among the top three parameters across all masses. Variables related to the background estimation method, particularly the binning of the $\eta$ and $1/p$ templates, become increasingly important as the mass hypothesis increases. Adjustments to binning, whether coarser or finer, alter the predicted spectrum in the sparsely populated high-mass region where the signal windows are located. Pile-Up reweighting, luminosity, and $F_\textrm{pixel}$ calibration have moderate effects, while the d$E$/d$x$ calibration constants $K_\textrm{mass}$ and $C_\textrm{mass}$, template fit variations, $I_h$ binning and jet energy variations have minimal impact.

The free background normalisations of the two channels are ranked lowest (\texttt{rateAll\_Eta1} and \texttt{rateAll\_Eta1\_2p4}), which may initially seem unexpected given that these are the only parameters left unconstrained in the fit. This outcome is explained by the definition of impact: each parameter is varied by its post-fit uncertainty, and for a free parameter, this uncertainty is determined entirely by the data. Because the mass spectrum contains many events at low mass, the fit constrains the overall normalisation with precision that exceeds any systematic effect. Consequently, shifting the normalisation within such a narrow range has minimal effect on the background expected under the signal. However, an uncertainty that alters the spectrum shape, such as binning variations, changes the predicted background in the signal window even if the normalisation is perfectly known. This effect directly translates into a shift in the signal strength. Therefore, shape-altering uncertainties dominate the ranking, whereas the normalisation itself does not.

The fitted signal strength $\hat{r}$ shown in each figure represents the ratio of the fitted cross section to its nominal theoretical value. Because the Asimov dataset is generated with a nominal-strength injected signal, the fit yields $\hat{r}=1$ by design. The key information is contained in the uncertainty, which indicates the precision with which a nominal cross section signal could be measured. This uncertainty increases from $^{+0.28}_{-0.24}$ at $2000$~GeV to $^{+1.5}_{-0.7}$ at $2600$~GeV, reflecting the rapid decline in signal yield. When the uncertainty approaches unity, a nominal-strength signal can no longer be distinguished from the background-only hypothesis, consistent with an exclusion reach just above $2.4$~TeV. No parameter shows a significant pull, as expected for a fit to the Asimov dataset, and the ranking changes only gradually with increasing mass.
