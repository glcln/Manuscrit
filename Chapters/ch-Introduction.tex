\chaptermark{Introduction}
\markboth{INTRODUCTION}{INTRODUCTION}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}


Throughout the twentieth century, particle physicists developed a unified framework describing matter and its interactions at the most fundamental level: the Standard Model of particle physics. Grounded in quantum field theory and local gauge invariance, this model has undergone decades of rigorous experimental testing and has consistently demonstrated exceptional predictive accuracy. The discovery of the Higgs boson by the ATLAS and CMS collaborations in 2012 marked the completion of the Standard Model's particle content. However, the Standard Model does not address several fundamental issues, such as the absence of a dark matter candidate, the lack of an explanation for the matter-antimatter asymmetry in the Universe, and the omission of gravity. These limitations indicate that the Standard Model likely represents only the low-energy manifestation of a more comprehensive theory, thereby motivating extensive searches for physics beyond the Standard Model at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC). This thesis contributes to this endeavour by presenting a search for hypothetical heavy, long-lived (or stable), and highly ionising charged particles (grouped under the name HSCPs) in proton-proton collision data collected by the Compact Muon Solenoid (CMS) experiment.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\cref{ch:chapitre1} introduces the experimental environment relevant to this research. It details the CERN accelerator complex and the LHC, which provided proton-proton collisions at a centre-of-mass energy of $\sqrt{s} = 13.6$~TeV for its third data-taking period (Run--3), and then describes the CMS detector and its sub-detectors that identify and characterise particles produced in each collision. The chapter focuses on the technical and software constraints involved in reconstructing the outcomes of such collisions.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The HSCP search strategy relies on precise measurement of the energy loss of charged particles due to ionisation (\dedx) in the CMS silicon tracker. \cref{ch:chapitre2} focuses on reconstructing this deposited charge. Following a review of the mechanisms ruling energy loss in matter and the operation of silicon strip sensors, the chapter probes saturation effects in the strip readout electronics that introduce bias into \dedx{} measurements for highly ionising deposits expected from massive exotic particles. The limitations of a first-order saturation correction are analysed, and a new saturation correction based on the strip cluster shape is developed and validated using both simulation and 2018 collision data. The impact of this correction is evaluated through \dedx{} performance studies with 2024–2025 CMS data and by comparing the reconstructed mass of simulated heavy particles, demonstrating significant improvement over the previous method.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\cref{ch:chapitre3} presents the theoretical background and experimental context motivating the HSCP research. It summarises the formulation of the Standard Model and its limitations, then introduces supersymmetry as a main extension and the theoretical basis for the benchmark signals considered in this thesis. The chapter discusses long-lived particles, defined as those with lifetimes sufficient to traverse macroscopic distances before decaying, with particular emphasis on HSCPs that pass through the entire detector. HSCPs are categorised as either bound states of gluinos or top squarks (R-hadrons), which interact primarily via the strong force, or as lepton-like particles such as the tau slepton. Due to their large mass and charge, HSCPs are produced at low velocities and appear as high-momentum, anomalously ionising tracks, a signature rarely targeted by other analyses, as most long-lived particle searches focus on neutral states. The chapter concludes with a review of the current experimental status of HSCP searches at CMS and ATLAS.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\cref{ch:chapitre4} details the search for HSCPs using Run--3 data, which forms the core of this thesis. The study is done in the 2024 dataset, corresponding to an integrated luminosity of 110~fb$^{-1}$, and focuses on the signatures of R-hadrons produced from gluino pair production. A trigger strategy based on missing transverse energy is used to recover R-hadrons that undergo charge flipping within the detector and evade muon-based selections, as well as heavy particles arriving outside the online timing window of the muon chambers. Moreover, the acceptance has been extended to cover the full pseudo-rapidity range of the detector. The analysis comprises defining the trigger strategy, selecting signal-like candidates, reconstructing candidate masses using combined momentum and \dedx{} measurements, developing and validating a data-driven background estimation method, deriving systematic uncertainties, and interpreting expected results by calculating exclusion limits. As the signal region remains blinded, expected upper limits on the gluino pair-production cross section are reported, extending sensitivity beyond a gluino mass of 2.4~TeV and surpassing the previous CMS exclusion of 2.08~TeV. These results establish a foundation for the analysis of the complete Run--3 dataset.
