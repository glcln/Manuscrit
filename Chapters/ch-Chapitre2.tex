\section{Interaction of charged particles with matter}
\label{sec:interactionWithMatter}

\indent A charged particle traversing a material loses energy via ionisation or Coulomb scattering. The Bethe-Bloch formula describes the energy loss due to ionisation in a material by a particle with a mass $M$ much greater than that of an electron $m_e$ \cite{PDG}:

\begin{equation} 
    \left\langle - \frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}x} \right\rangle = Kz^2 \frac{Z}{A} \frac{1}{\beta^2} \left[ \frac{1}{2} \ln \left( \frac{2m_ec^2\beta^2\gamma^2W_{\mathrm{max}}}{I^2} \right) - \beta^2 - \frac{\delta(\beta\gamma)}{2} \right]
    \label{eq:BetheBloch_formula}
\end{equation}

\noindent with

\begin{equation}
    W_{\max} = \frac{2m_e c^2 \beta^2 \gamma^2}{1 + 2\gamma m_e/M + (m_e/M)^2}
    \label{eq:Wmax}
\end{equation}

\noindent and

\begin{equation}
    \delta(\beta\gamma) = \begin{cases} 2(\ln 10)x - \overline{C} & \text{if } x \geq x_1; \\ 2(\ln 10)x - \overline{C} + a(x_1 - x)^k & \text{if } x_0 \leq x < x_1; \\ 0 & \text{if } x < x_0 \textrm{ (non conductors)}; \\ \delta_0 10^{2(x - x_0)} & \text{if } x < x_0 \text{ (conductors)} \end{cases}
    \label{eq:deltabetagamma}
\end{equation}
where the term $\beta\gamma= p/(Mc)$ represents the kinematics of the particle with mass $M$ and momentum $p$. The term $\beta$ is equal to $v/c$ (where $v$ is the velocity of the particle), $A$ and $Z$ correspond respectively to the mass number and atomic number of the material traversed, $z$ is the algebraic charge of the particle and $K\simeq0.307075 \,\, \textrm{MeV.mol}^{-1}.\textrm{cm}^2$. $W_{\mathrm{max}}$ [MeV] is the maximum energy transferred in a single collision described in \cref{eq:Wmax}, $I$ [eV] is the average excitation energy of the material, and $\delta(\beta\gamma)$ is the density correction for the ultra-relativistic regime.

As for the latter, \cref{eq:deltabetagamma} gives an empirical expression depending on the $\beta\gamma$ regime with $x=\log_{10}(\beta\gamma)$; $x_0$, $x_1$, $\overline{C}$, $a$, $k$ and $\delta_0$ are constants related to the material, one can find numerical values of them in \cite{DeltaBetaGamma}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The average stopping power $\langle \dedx \rangle $ is usually depicted in MeV.$\mathrm{g}^{-1}.\mathrm{cm}^2$. By multiplying this value by the material density, the average ionisation loss is therefore expressed in [MeV/cm]. The mean energy deposit per unit of length value depends on $p/M$, so that for slow moving particle and/or heavy ones, \dedx\, decreases as $-\ln(p/M)$ before reaching a minimum at around $3-4$ MeV/cm for $\beta\gamma\sim3-4$, before gradually increasing due to the relativistic-regime term induced by $\delta(\beta\gamma)$. \cref{fig:BetheBloch} shows the energy deposition per unit distance travelled for different particles passing through silicon according to \cref{eq:BetheBloch_formula}.


\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Images/Ch2/bethebloch_multiparticle_Si.pdf}
    \caption{Average ionisation loss of muons, pions, kaons and protons in the silicon derived from \cref{eq:BetheBloch_formula}. Numerical values of the parameters were taken in \cite{DeltaBetaGamma}.}
    \label{fig:BetheBloch}
\end{figure}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Most of the particles produced by proton-proton collisions at the CMS detector are low-energy particles, which release some of their energy at the minimum of the curve. The silicon tracker has been optimised to detect such particles, labelled as \textit{Minimum Ionising Particles} (MIPs).

Note that for high relativistic particles at $\beta\gamma\sim1000$, the average deposit is 2.5 times higher than the MIP. Thus, by measuring the \dedx\,information, one could identify the $\beta\gamma$ regime of the particle. This information will later be used to distinguish HSCP deposits from those produced by SM processes.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

One additional point is that the dominant energy-loss mechanism depends on the particle type. For muons with energies below about 100~GeV, ionisation is the primary energy-loss process. Consequently, muons can travel considerable distances even in dense materials such as iron. For instance, a 10~GeV muon loses approximately 13~MeV/cm in iron, corresponding to a range of several metres. 

At higher energies, radiative processes such as Bremsstrahlung, pair production, and photonuclear interactions become non-negligible and progressively dominate the energy loss. 

As a result, muons produced in particle accelerators are highly penetrating and typically traverse the entire detector, leaving an imprint of ionisation. This distinctive behaviour can be exploited for muon identification, since other charged particles generally undergo additional interactions besides ionisation energy loss~\cite{Thomson_2013}.


\vspace{0.5 cm}

While the Bethe–Bloch formula provides an accurate description of the average energy loss over a given path length, it becomes inadequate when considering thin absorbers such as the strip or pixel silicon sensors of the CMS tracker, where the energy deposit is measured locally, layer by layer.

In this regime, the number of collisions experienced by the particle is relatively small, and the deposited energy is no longer a well-defined quantity. Still, it must instead be described by a probability distribution. Rare but large energy transfers dominate this distribution to $\delta$-rays\footnote{Secondary electrons with enough energy to escape a significant distance away from the primary radiation beam and produce further ionisation.}, which distort it away from a symmetric Gaussian shape and produce a characteristic asymmetric tail toward large energy losses.

In 1944, Lev Landau addressed this problem analytically. Starting from the differential cross section for individual collisions, he showed that in the limit of a sufficiently thin absorber the energy-loss fluctuations follow a universal functional form, now known as the \textit{Landau distribution} \cite{Landau}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

As the tail goes to infinity, the Landau distribution does not have a finite mean and standard deviation. Instead, it is commonly described by the \textit{Most Probable Value} (MPV), defined as the position of the maximum of the Landau distribution. The MPV depends on the thickness of the material crossed by the particle and increases approximately linearly with thickness, as a thicker layer results in more ionising collisions. 

The Landau Probability Density Function (PDF) is a function of the particle mass and momentum, the crossed material and the thickness of the latter. \cref{fig:landau} shows this PDF for a pion with a momentum of 10 GeV/$c$ passing through two silicon sensors of different thicknesses.

Note that particles can't actually lose more energy than their total budget; thus, the infinite tail of the Landau distribution is purely conceptual.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Images/Ch2/landau_total_keV.pdf}
    \caption{Landau PDF for a 10 GeV pion passing through a 320~$\mu$m and 500~$\mu$m silicon sensor.}
    \label{fig:landau}
\end{figure}

Another important point is the dependence of the MPV on the relativistic regime, $\beta\gamma$, and on the material thickness. The slower the particle is, the higher the MPV is, and, in the relativistic regime, the MPV converges asymptotically to a value that depends solely on the thickness of the material. An example of the evolution of the MPV for two different silicon sensor thicknesses is shown in \cref{fig:MPV}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The Landau distribution provides information on the total energy deposited in a finite silicon layer (here for 320 and 500 $\mu\mathrm{m}$). At the same time, the MPV curve gives the energy loss per unit length (MeV/cm). Multiplying the MPV at the MIP value ($\sim$2.6-2.7 MeV/cm on silicon) by the layer thickness naturally yields a deposit in the keV range, consistent with the MPV observed in the Landau PDF of \cref{fig:landau}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Images/Ch2/MPV.pdf}
    \caption{Most Probable Value of the Landau PDF as a function of the relativistic regime $\beta\gamma$ for two silicon sensors of different thickness.}
    \label{fig:MPV}
\end{figure}


\vspace{0.5 cm}

Finally, one could also present a more general description of energy-loss fluctuations, provided by the \textit{Vavilov distribution}, which extends the Landau model to absorbers of finite thickness. In the limit of very thin materials, it reduces to the Landau distribution, while for thicker absorbers it gradually approaches a Gaussian shape. This behaviour reflects the Central Limit Theorem: as the number of independent collisions increases, the sum of many small energy transfers tends toward a normal distribution \cite{VavilovFunctionPixels}.

This function is used, for instance, to simulate charge deposits in pixel sensors. Particles at high pseudorapidity traverse a larger amount of material than those produced in the central region; thus, the Landau distribution is no longer representative of their energy distribution. Incorporating this effect into the simulation enables a more accurate tuning of the detector response, bringing it into closer agreement with the behaviour observed in data.



\section{Physics of the silicon tracker modules}

A typical semiconductor detector consists of a sensitive layer and read-out electronics that process the analogue signals. The following section will cover the underlying physics governing semiconductor sensors and will present the typical read-out electronics chain used for data acquisition of silicon strip sensors.

\subsection{Semiconductor sensors}

\subsubsection{Basic concept of semiconductor physics}

The Pauli exclusion principle states that two identical fermions can not occupy the same quantum state simultaneously. The Fermi-Dirac distribution therefore describes the distribution of energy levels in fermionic systems:

\begin{equation}
    f(E)=\frac{1}{1+\exp\left( \displaystyle \frac{E-E_F}{k_BT} \right)}
    \label{eq:fermidirac}
\end{equation}
where $k_B$ denotes the Boltzmann constant, $T$ the temperature of the system, and $E_F$ the Fermi level.

The distribution gives the probability density of finding a fermion at a given energy, while the Fermi level corresponds to the energy at which this probability equals 1/2. At $T$ = 0 K, all energy states up to $E_F$ are filled, and as the temperature rises, electrons begin to populate higher-energy excited states. 

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Unlike isolated atoms, where electrons occupy discrete orbitals with well-defined binding energies, electrons in crystalline solids arrange themselves into continuous energy bands. The band structure of various materials is illustrated schematically in \cref{fig:EnergyBand}. The valence band is the highest band fully occupied at $T$~=~0~K, while the partially filled band above it is known as the conduction band. Between these two bands lies a forbidden region with no allowed energy states, referred to as the band gap. Electrons residing in the conduction band are those that contribute to the electrical conductivity of the material. 

Based on this band picture, materials can be classified into three broad categories: conductors, semiconductors, and insulators. Conductors already possess electrons in the conduction band at $T=0$~K, whereas semiconductors and insulators do not, making them behave similarly at absolute zero. At elevated temperatures, however, thermally excited electrons in semiconductors can acquire enough energy to bridge the band gap, rendering the material conductive. These thermally generated carriers are known as intrinsic \textit{charge carriers}, and a semiconductor free of any impurities is accordingly called an intrinsic semiconductor.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \input{Images/Ch2/EnergyBand.tex}
    \caption{Sketch of the energy band structure for three different materials at $T=0$ K: insulators, semiconductors and conductors. The orange (blue) regions represent the states that contain (no) electrons.}
    \label{fig:EnergyBand}
\end{figure}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Band theory is based on chemical bonds between atoms, which arise from interactions between electron shells. The valence shell, in particular, determines an element's electrical conductivity. A filled valence shell makes an element chemically inert, gaseous, and poorly conductive. A nearly full shell, as in F, Cl, and Br, forms non-conductive solids called insulators. Elements with a single valence electron, such as Li, Na, and Cu, are good conductors because the electron moves freely across the lattice. Atoms like C, Si, and Ge, which have four valence electrons, form covalent bonds by sharing electrons with neighbours. These elements, known as semiconductors, bind their electrons less tightly than insulators, enabling conduction under certain conditions.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Electrical conductivity in a crystal lattice is driven by the movement of charge carriers, namely electrons and holes. When an electron vacates its position, it leaves behind an empty site in the lattice, referred to as a hole. This hole is quickly occupied by a neighbouring electron, which in turn creates a new vacancy elsewhere. Through this successive displacement mechanism, holes effectively travel across the lattice, giving rise to hole conductivity. The velocity at which electrons and holes move varies with both the material and the applied electric field. In an intrinsic semiconductor, electrons and holes are present in equal numbers, meaning that neither carrier type is dominant.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The conduction properties of semiconductors can be tailored by deliberately introducing impurities into the crystal lattice, a process known as doping, which is schematically illustrated for silicon in \cref{fig:DopedSemiConductor}.

To enhance electron conductivity, atoms carrying an extra valence electron are incorporated into the lattice. These impurity atoms are called donors, and the resulting material is designated as n-type, or n-doped, semiconductor. In this case, Phosphorus, which possesses five valence electrons, is a typical donor atom. P-type semiconductors, on the other hand, are obtained by introducing acceptor atoms into the intrinsic semiconductor; these atoms have one fewer electron in their outer shell, making hole conductivity the dominant transport mechanism. Boron is commonly used as an acceptor in p-doped silicon.

\end{figure}


\subsubsection{p--n junctions}

Semiconductor sensors are built around a p--n junction, which consists of a p-type and an n-type semiconductor brought into contact. The corresponding energy state diagram for doped semiconductors and a p--n junction is depicted in \cref{fig:PNEnergy}.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \input{Images/Ch2/PNEnergy.tex}
    \caption{Energy diagram of doped semiconductors and a p--n junction. $E_A$ and $E_D$ are the energy levels of acceptor and donor atoms, respectively. The orange (blue) regions represent the states that contain (no) electrons.}
    \label{fig:PNEnergy}
\end{figure}


Doping shifts the Fermi energy level due to the increased concentration of charge carriers in the semiconductor. At the interface between the p-type and n-type regions, electrons and holes begin to recombine, leaving a positively charged region on the n-doped side and a negatively charged region on the p-doped side. The resulting built-in electric field counteracts further diffusion of charge carriers, giving rise to a carrier-free region known as the \textit{depletion zone}.

This zone can be extended by attaching electrodes to both sides of the junction to establish electrical contacts, and then applying an external voltage across the p--n junction. When a positive voltage is applied to the n-side, the induced electric field draws charge carriers away from the junction. Beyond a certain voltage threshold, the field extends across the entire thickness of the sensor, at which point the sensor is said to be fully depleted. This threshold, known as the \textit{full depletion voltage}, depends on both the doping concentration and the sensor thickness.


\subsection{Silicon strip sensors}

The CMS detector uses the tracker's silicon p--n junctions for particle detection. Among the various natural semiconductors, the collaboration opted for silicon-based sensors for various reasons. It is the most common semiconductor (thus cheaper), with natural radiation tolerance and an efficient band gap, and a low electron-hole pair creation energy threshold.

A silicon strip sensor module comprises several distinct elements, each playing a specific role in the detection chain. \Cref{fig:SiliconStripSensor} provides a schematic overview of such a module, the key components of which are described in the following.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Images/Ch2/SiliconStripSensorSketch.png}
    \caption{Sketch of a silicon strip module with its different subparts \cite{SiliconLayout}.}
    \label{fig:SiliconStripSensor}
\end{figure}

\textbf{General structure}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The sensor consists of a phosphorus-doped n-type silicon bulk forming the active detection volume. Multiple $\mathrm{p^+}$ strips are implanted on the front face, creating a p--n junction that, under reverse bias, depletes the bulk and enables charge collection.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\textbf{Signal generation}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

As a MIP traverses the depleted silicon bulk, it generates approximately 70 electron-hole pairs per micrometre of sensor thickness, each requiring 3.62 eV of energy \cite{ElectronHolePairsEnergy}. This is significantly lower than in gaseous detectors (34.6\,eV for an $\mathrm{H_2}$-based detector). For a 300\,$\mu$m thick sensor, this corresponds to $\sim 2\times10^4$ electron-hole pairs per MIP.

Under the applied electric field, holes drift toward the $\mathrm{p^+}$ strips while electrons drift toward the $\mathrm{n^+}$ backplane, with all carriers being collected within $\mathcal{O}(10\,\mathrm{ns})$. However, this signal is several orders of magnitude smaller than the \textit{leakage current}, arising from the $\sim 10^8$ electron-hole pairs continuously generated by thermal excitation within the bulk. This parasitic current constitutes a significant source of noise and is further enhanced by radiation-induced lattice defects. To mitigate this effect, the sensors are cooled to suppress thermal generation and improve the signal-to-noise ratio for MIP charge collection \cite{ElectronHoleInSilicon}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\textbf{Front side readout}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Each strip is read out via an AC-coupled pad, which blocks the DC leakage current while transmitting the signal to the read-out chip through a bond wire. A polyresistor connects the DC pad to the bias ring, allowing strip biasing while preserving AC isolation.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\textbf{Edge structures}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Two surrounding $\mathrm{p^+}$ rings protect the active volume. The bias ring is connected to ground and biases the strips, while the guard ring grades the electric field at the sensor periphery, preventing the depletion region from reaching the physical edges and avoiding breakdown. This part of the device sits on a $\mathrm{SiO_2/Si_3N_4}$ insulating layer deposited on the front surface to protect and electrically insulate the surface structures.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\textbf{Backside}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

A heavily doped $\mathrm{n^+}$ implant on the rear face ensures an ohmic contact with the aluminium backplane electrode, through which the positive high voltage is applied to deplete the sensor bulk fully.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\textbf{Geometric parameters}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The centre-to-centre distance between adjacent strips defines the strip pitch $P$, while their implant width is denoted $W$; these are the key geometric parameters of the sensor summarised in \cref{tab:tracker_modules}. Since the silicon strip tracker spans a large tracking volume, both parameters vary with the module's position within the tracker to keep the $W/P$ ratio close to 0.25. This value has been chosen to minimise the total strip capacitance while maintaining a sufficiently large electric field peak at the $\mathrm{p^+}$ strip edges.



\subsection{Read-out pipeline}

Once a charged particle has generated electron-hole pairs in the bulk material, the drift of these electrons induces a current that is then read out. The resulting current is transmitted via a pitch adapter\footnote{The pitch adapter bridges the geometrical mismatch between the strip pitch and the read-out electronics}, to the Front-End Hybrid (FEH), where the APV25 (a 25 $\mu$m thick CMOS Analogue Pipeline Voltage) front-end chips are located \cite{ReadoutStrip}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Each APV25 chip reads out 128 strips. Given that strip modules comprise either 512 or 768 strips, between four and six APV25 chips are required per sensor. Within each chip, the charge signal first passes through a preamplifier, which converts it into a voltage step. A unity-gain inverter is then applied to normalise the signal polarity, ensuring a positive voltage step regardless of whether the signal originates from electrons or holes. The signal subsequently enters a CR-RC shaper\footnote{A CR-RC shaper is an analogue filtering stage that differentiates the input signal to remove the DC baseline (CR high-pass filter) and then integrates it to limit the bandwidth and optimise the signal-to-noise ratio (RC low-pass filter).}, which produces a 40\,MHz voltage pulse. Two operating modes are available: the \textit{peak mode}, which samples the pulse at its maximum, and the \textit{deconvolution mode}, which computes a weighted sum over three consecutive samples separated by 25\,ns each. Whilst peak mode suffers from a pulse length of approximately 200\,ns (longer than the 25\,ns LHC bunch-crossing interval), deconvolution mode effectively confines the signal to a single bunch crossing and is therefore the standard mode during physics data-taking.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The analogue outputs of pairs of APV25 chips are time-multiplexed and transmitted via analogue optical links to the Front-End Drivers (FEDs), located outside the CMS detector in the experimental cavern. At the FED level, the signal from each strip is digitised using a 10-bit analogue-to-digital converter (ADC), yielding a \textit{raw-digit}. The FEDs then operate in zero-suppressed mode, performing two successive noise subtractions on a chip-by-chip basis. The first subtraction removes the \textit{pedestal}, defined as the average baseline activity of a given strip in the absence of any particle signal. The second subtraction addresses the Common Mode Noise (CMN), an electronic baseline fluctuation common to all 128 strips of a given APV25 chip, estimated as the median over those strips for each event. Both the pedestal and noise levels are evaluated during dedicated calibration runs, performed several times per year outside of standard physics data-taking periods.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Following these subtractions, strips that return negative ADC values are set to zero, since the FEDs handle only positive values. A zero-suppression threshold is then applied: individual strips with a signal-to-noise ratio $S/N$ above 5 are included, as are groups of adjacent strips provided each neighbouring strip exceeds $S/N > 2$. The 10--bit ADC range is subsequently truncated to 8 bits according to the following scheme:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Strips with a digitised signal below 254 ADC counts are left unchanged;
    \item Those with a digitised signal between 254 and 1023 ADC counts are set to 254;
    \item Strips at 1024 ADC counts are assigned a value of 255.
\end{itemize}
Strips with zero ADC counts are suppressed entirely, thereby reducing the overall data rate.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

In addition to signal digitisation, the Front-End Controllers (FEC) distribute global timing, trigger, and command information from the Timing, Trigger and Control (TTC) system to the front-end electronics via the Communication and Control Unit (CCU). The FEC also handles the configuration of the front-end electronics and monitors key operational parameters, including sensor temperature, leakage current, and bias voltage, through the Detector Control Unit (DCU).

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Finally, one can convert the charge collected in the ADC to MeV by knowing the electron-hole energy-pair creation and the effective gain of the read-out chain (APV25 + FED), the latter being determined experimentally during calibration runs:

\begin{equation}
    Q[\mathrm{ADC \, count}]= 3.62 \cdot10^{-6} \times 265 \times Q[\mathrm{MeV}]
    \label{eq:ADCfactorToMeV}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Properties of charge deposits}

As described in \cref{subsubsection:Hitreconstruction}, collected charges in adjacent strips are grouped into clusters. The width of the cluster, i.e., the number of strips, is affected by two physical processes: the \textit{charge sharing} and the \textit{cross-talk}.

\subsubsection{Charge sharing}

As depicted in \cref{fig:SiliconStripSensor}, a MIP crossing the n-bulk creates electron-hole pairs that can be captured by several strips depending on the incident angle of the particle. According to the Lorentz force, the movement of the charge carriers is also affected by the internal electric field between the electrodes and the external magnetic field. The latter causes a drift of about 5° in the barrel sensors, resulting in a position shift of the order of 10 to 20 $\mu$m \cite{ChargeCharingDrift}.

One could also mention charge diffusion induced by collisions between charge carriers, thereby causing a small drift along the path.

These two phenomena, combined, have a small effect relative to the pitch size (the smaller value being 80 $\mu$m) but are accounted for in the simulation of charge spreading.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

To illustrate that, \cref{fig:clusterCenteredShifted} shows two \textit{raw-digit} clusters of charges collected in a silicon strip sensor. These clusters originate from a Monte-Carlo simulation of back-to-back muons generated with a $p_T$ ranging from 50 to 200 GeV/$c$ with a pseudorapidity range $|\eta|<2.1$ without any electronic saturation, noise or pile-up contamination.

The charge collected on each strip is expressed in ADC counts, while the strip index identifies its position within the cluster. The left cluster originates from a muon that crosses a TIB module at an angle of 0.16 rad ($\sim9$°) to the module's normal. The effect of charge sharing is not very important, but the effect of cross-talk is clearly visible (developed below). On the other hand, the right cluster corresponds to a muon with an incident angle of 1.23 rad ($\sim 71$°). This tilted approach leads to higher charge collection in strips 436 and 437 than in the adjacent strips.

\subsubsection{Cross-talk}
\label{subsubsection:crosstalk}

In the CMS silicon strip sensor, each strip is capacitively coupled to both the backplane and its immediate neighbours via inter-strip capacitance \cite{CapacitiveCoupling}. It is inherent to the sensor geometry itself: the inter-strip capacitance arises from the proximity of adjacent $\mathrm{p^+}$ implants, while the backplane capacitance couples each strip to the aluminium backplane through the bulk, as illustrated in \cref{fig:SiliconStripSensor}.

This capacitive network has a direct consequence on the charge measurement: when a particle deposits charge on a given strip, a fraction of that charge is redistributed to the adjacent strips through capacitive coupling. This effect is referred to as \textit{cross-talk}, and must be carefully accounted for in the cluster charge reconstruction.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The magnitude of the charge sharing is parametrised as follows. For a cluster of total charge $Q$, the strip with the largest charge fraction receives $x_0 \times Q$, its two immediate neighbours each receive $x_1 \times Q$, and the next-to-nearest neighbours receive $x_2 \times Q$. For a common 5-strip width cluster, this leads to the following equation: $\displaystyle x_0 + 2\cdot x_1 + 2 \cdot x_2 = 1$. If a MIP particle crosses the sensor with an incident angle close to zero with respect to the normal, this leads to pyramid-shaped clusters, as the left cluster of \cref{fig:clusterCenteredShifted}.

One should note that these parameters are not fixed; they depend on the APV25 chip's read-out mode, as the degree of charge sharing is sensitive to the signal sampling time. The values of $x_0$, $x_1$ and $x_2$ have been extracted from data in deconvolution mode, using muon tracks traversing the full tracker with trajectories close to normal incidence to the sensor plane, and were evaluated both in 2010 and 2018 to monitor potential variations over time \cite{Xtalk2018CMS}. Values of those three parameters measured in the tracker barrel for different years are presented in \cref{fig:Xtalk}. Due to ageing, $x_1$ and $x_2$ are smaller than at the beginning of the last decade; thus, clusters tend to shrink.

\subsection{Radiation effects}

The performance of silicon tracking detectors inevitably degrades under the intense radiation environment of the LHC. Energy loss of traversing particles occurs through two distinct mechanisms: Ionising Energy Loss (IEL), arising from collisions with atomic electrons, and Non-Ionising Energy Loss (NIEL), in which nuclear interactions displace lattice atoms. Both processes induce radiation damage, which can be classified as bulk or surface damage.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Bulk damage, primarily driven by NIEL, arises when recoiling atoms displace neighbouring lattice sites, forming defect clusters. These introduce new energy levels within the silicon bandgap, leading to increased leakage current, charge carrier trapping, and local electric field distortions, all of which degrade charge collection efficiency and ultimately the sensor resolution.

Surface damage is instead dominated by ionising radiation, which generates positive charges trapped at the Si–Si$\mathrm{O}_2$ interface. This accumulated charge can induce inter-strip short circuits, increasing both the bulk current and cross-talk between read-out channels, thereby degrading the detector's noise performance.

Beyond permanent damage, ionising particles can also flip the state of memory cells in the front-end electronics, an effect known as Single Event Upset (SEU). Unlike bulk or surface damage, SEUs are non-permanent and can be recovered by reconfiguring the affected circuits.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

To cope with the cumulative effects of permanent radiation damage, detectors can be recalibrated and operated at higher bias voltages and lower temperatures. However, once the signal-to-noise ratio becomes too degraded, front-end modules must be replaced. A typical operational lifetime for the inner layers of a silicon tracker at the LHC is of the order of 5 to 10 years.



\section{Study of saturated strip clusters}

In the next, the energy deposit per unit of length of a charged particle crossing a material will be denoted as \dedx.

\subsection{The harmonic ionisation mean \texorpdfstring{$I_h$}{Ih}}

One way to assess the average ionisation of a track along several silicon strip modules is to use a harmonic mean function. Therefore, $I_h$ is defined to be the harmonic mean of the ionisation of a charged particle track that has deposited $N$ charge clusters $c_j = (\dedx)_j$ along several silicon strip layers:

\begin{equation}
    I_h = \left( \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N c_j^{-2} \right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
    \label{eq:ih}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

$I_h$ is, by construction, very robust against upward fluctuations of the $c_j$, which have a non-negligible chance to happen (as they are distributed on a Landau with a long tail). On the other hand, $I_h$ is very sensitive to downward fluctuations, which are unlikely to occur randomly (if the $c_j$ are indeed following a Landau distribution), but may occur due to detector effects such as noise, baseline voltage drop, etc.

\subsection{Preliminary studies}
\label{subsubsection:satstripclusters}

A preliminary work on saturated strip clusters has been carried out in a 2018 Monte Carlo simulation of two back-to-back muons generated with a $p_T$ range from 50 to 200 GeV/$c$ and a pseudorapidity range $|\eta|<3$. The original simulated charge deposit on silicon sensors is saved without saturation or zero suppression (raw-digit format). This sample provides a good number of pure tracks, leaving charge deposits at the MIP level within the tracker, while also saving crucial simulated information for the rest. For the rest of the study, this muon simulation will be labelled as the \textit{bTbM sample}.

Considering this, saturated clusters are saved in addition to the reconstructed ones (with saturation filters and zero-suppression process applied), as shown in \cref{fig:ClusterExample}.

Continuing on this line, a first step was to determine the proportion of saturated clusters across different Monte Carlo samples relative to a dataset. Because muons are of interest in the search for HSCPs, a comparison of various muon-related processes was conducted to reduce bias in subsequent analysis.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

In addition to the bTbM sample, three distinct 2018 samples are considered. The first is a Monte Carlo simulation of W bosons produced in association with jets, modelling standard-model muons that deposit energy at the MIP level. This contrasts with a high-energy deposit scenario studied in a 2018 HSCP simulation of gluinos with $m_{\widetilde{g}}=2000$~GeV/$c^2$, in which the HSCP is matched to a muon. The third sample is an Ultra-Legacy\footnote{The Ultra-Legacy (UL) dataset is the full reprocessing of the CMS Run 2 data with improved calibrations, alignment corrections, and reprocessed MC simulations, providing the final and definitive version of the Run 2 dataset for physics analyses.} 2018 Era D dataset of muon data, providing a reference from actual detector data.

In each case, the muon track must be a \textit{HighPurity} track, with at least eight \dedx\ deposits, to ensure good reconstruction.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

As depicted in \cref{tab:WjetHSCPsat}, most of the saturated clusters are those with a single saturated strip. With, on top of that, more than 99\% of them being at the 254--saturation level (with a charge between 254 and 1023 ADC count).

As expected, heavier particles, like gluinos, deposit more energy and thus lead to more saturation, with 44 \% of \dedx\,clusters being saturated. On the other hand, for standard model muons, the saturation affects 8.5 to 10\% of the total number of clusters of a track. The spatial distribution of those clusters is not uniform within the tracker. Most of them are situated at the TOB--TEC junction ($|\eta|\sim 1.8-2.2$), where the strip sensors are oriented differently: horizontally on the TOB and vertically on the TEC. Thus, a MIP crossing a vertical sensor in the central region deposes a higher \dedx\,due to the higher pathlength\footnote{The pathlength $l$ is defined as the length of the particle trajectory within the silicon sensor, which depends on both the sensor thickness $d$ and the track incident angle $\theta$ with respect to the sensor normal: $l=d/\cos(\theta)$.}.

These preliminary studies show that the fraction of saturated clusters is negligible in standard-model processes but significant in beyond-standard-model searches. For such hypothetical particles, the deposited charge often differs from the reconstructed charge, leading to a bias in the average \dedx\,measurement using the $I_h$ estimator.

Building on this, since this thesis focuses on the HSCP search using the \dedx\, approach, it is essential to address the observed bias. To do so, a saturation correction is required. The first step involves developing an algorithm for single-strip saturated clusters, which are the most common, with a correction for two consecutive saturated strips to be addressed later.

\subsection{Shape classification}
\label{subsection:shapeclassification}

This part relates to clusters with a single saturated strip. As access to the original charge deposit will be needed without any saturation, this part is based on the bTbM sample.

Single saturated strip clusters are grouped into five different labels depending on their shape, as shown in \cref{fig:clustershape}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Center} clusters: Originating from particles with a near-normal approach to the sensor, they are the ones where the effect of the cross-talk is the most noticeable. In the bTbM sample, they represent 69\% of the saturated cluster fraction.
    \item \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters (L and R): Coming from incident inclined particles with respect to the normal of the sensors, their barycentre is shifted toward the strips with more deposit. Each accounts for 14\% of the total population.
    \item \textit{FullLeft} and \textit{FullRight} clusters (FL and FR): These are the abnormal clusters; their shape runs counter to what the charge sharing should provide at the charge collection step. FL and FR clusters are not common; they account for 1.5\% each of the total fraction. Most of them ($\sim$80\%) result from a dead strip adjacent to the maximum, and about 20\% of them because their maximum is located at the beginning or the end of the APV25 read-out.
\end{itemize}

The dependency between each cluster shape and the tracker layer is depicted in \cref{fig:shapeFraction}. As expected, the population of \textit{Center} clusters is higher, especially in the central region where the charged particles crossed the material without a tilted incident angle. Note that in a given tracker layer, the sum of each population does not always match the saturated cluster population, because the shape classification only applies to single saturated strip clusters.

\subsection{Basic saturation correction}

So far, the majority of saturated clusters are the \textit{Center} ones for which the cross-talk is more visible. One could then invert the cross-talk effect, knowing the expected fraction of the total charge that should be deposited in the first adjacent strips of the saturated maximum. As already presented in \cref{subsubsection:crosstalk}, the value of this fraction (the $x_1$ parameter) has already been measured (see \cref{fig:Xtalk}).

Thus, the corrected charge $Q_\mathrm{basic\,corr}$ is computed using the charge collected on the left ($L_\mathrm{max}$) and right ($R_\mathrm{max}$) strips adjacent to the saturated maximum. A sketch of the correction process is shown in \cref{fig:XtalkInversion}. It is assumed that each of these corresponds to 10\% of the total charge:

\begin{equation}
    Q_\mathrm{basic\,corr} = \frac{1}{0.1} \cdot \frac{L_\mathrm{max}+R_\mathrm{max}}{2}
    \label{eq:xtalkinversion}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \input{Images/Ch2/XtalkInversion.tex}
    \caption{Sketch of the cross-talk inversion on a \textit{Center} saturated cluster, assuming that the single saturated strip counts for 78\% of the total charge $Q$ of the cluster.}
    \label{fig:XtalkInversion}
\end{figure}

This saturation correction method is implemented within the HSCP analysis framework. To be corrected, saturated clusters must fulfil the following criteria:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The cluster must contain between 2 and 8 strips.
    \item Only one saturated strip is allowed.
    \item The first adjacent strips next to the saturated maximum must be symmetrical ($|L_{\mathrm{max}}-R_{\mathrm{max}}|<40$ ADC counts) and not too low ($L_{\mathrm{max}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{max}}>25$ ADC counts). 
\end{itemize}

One can assess the quality of reconstruction by looking at the $Q_\mathrm{basic\,corr}/Q_\mathrm{true}$ ratio and the $Q_\mathrm{basic\,corr}-Q_\mathrm{true}$ difference, with $Q_\mathrm{true}$ being the original charge of the cluster. Before applying the saturation correction procedure, for each cluster, strips with a maximum above 254 are set to 254 or 255 ADC counts, depending on the charge, thus simulating a saturated cluster. Both results are depicted in \cref{fig:QcorrOldcorrection}. 

This basic correction introduces bias and yields poor accuracy, with a large spread in the corrected charge (a variation of $\mu/\sigma\sim13\%$ in the ratio plot). This comes from the fixed value of the cross-talk inversion coefficient at $1/0.1$; a different value, depending on the shape and the tracker layer, would be more suitable. Moreover, looking at the difference between the corrected and actual charges, a large population of corrected clusters shows an under-correction of about -50 ADC counts, leading to poor overall correction. 

On top of that, only two-thirds of the saturated cluster population are corrected. With a population composed of 85\% of \textit{Center}, 30\% of LR, and 15\% of FLFR clusters. None of the clusters containing two consecutive saturated strips is corrected. This comes from the restrictive selection on the saturated maximum neighbourhood ($|L_{\mathrm{max}}-R_{\mathrm{max}}|<40$ ADC count, $L_{\mathrm{max}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{max}}>25$ ADC count). A change in these two values leads to either a larger selection with poor correction (as more misshapen clusters were corrected) or the opposite.

\section{Saturation correction based on cluster shape}

This section will focus on clusters that contain a single saturated strip. Therefore, saturated clusters will be designed as follows. A discussion on clusters containing two consecutive saturated strips will then be dedicated. Three methods are presented first before the results are shown.

\subsection{\textit{Center} clusters}

As shown in \cref{subsection:shapeclassification}, Clusters coming from incident normal muons are the most common ones; they are labelled as \textit{Center} clusters. To be included in this category, saturated clusters must meet the following criteria:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cluster with at least three strips.
    \item Saturated maximum not at the beginning, nor at the end of the cluster.
    \item $0.9 \leq L_{\mathrm{max}}/R_{\mathrm{max}} \leq 1.1$ to select only pyramid-shaped clusters. With $L_{\mathrm{max}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{max}}$ being the names of the left and right adjacent strips of the maximum, respectively. The choice of taking $L_{\mathrm{max}}$ as the numerator has the same consequences as taking $R_{\mathrm{max}}$.
\end{itemize}

The effect of cross-talk is most noticeable in these clusters; thus, considering a cross-talk inversion is a good solution to the saturation issue. The value of the saturated strip $Q_\mathrm{max}$ is corrected using:

\begin{equation}
    Q_\mathrm{max} = a_{\mathrm{center}} \cdot \frac{L_{\mathrm{max}} + R_{\mathrm{max}}}{2}
    \label{eq:CenterCorr}
\end{equation}
with $a_{\mathrm{center}}$ taken to be the maximum of $Q_\mathrm{max}/[(L_{\mathrm{max}} + R_{\mathrm{max}})/2]$ histogram templates. These templates are derived from the bTbM sample, since the actual maximum value is known. They are generated for each layer of the TIB and TOB, and for each ring (a wheel can have different sensor geometries instead of rings) of the TID and TEC; examples are shown in \cref{fig:centerhist}.

Because the bTbM sample contains only 600,000 Events, there were insufficient cluster statistics to perform a Gaussian fit near the maximum; thus, the maximum values of those templates were taken.

\subsection{\textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters}

Similar to \textit{Center} clusters, \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters must fulfil the following criteria to be labelled as such:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cluster with at least three strips.
    \item Saturated maximum not at the beginning, nor at the end of the cluster.
    \item $L_{\mathrm{max}} \geq 1.1\cdot R_{\mathrm{max}}$ or $R_{\mathrm{max}} \geq 1.1\cdot L_{\mathrm{max}}$.
\end{itemize}

Contrary to \textit{Center} clusters, the proportion of the charge carried by adjacent strips relative to the total charge is greater, as shown in \cref{fig:adjacentStripsLR}. Therefore, considering a similar cross-talk inversion as in \cref{eq:CenterCorr} would be a bad correction, leading to under- and over-corrected clusters.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Images/Ch2/AdjacentStripCharge_CLR.pdf}
    \caption{Ratio of the charge collected by the highest-charge neighbouring strip to the total cluster charge, for the \textit{Center} (with $N_\mathrm{max}=(L_\mathrm{max}+R_\mathrm{max})/2$), \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters.}
    \label{fig:adjacentStripsLR}
\end{figure}

Thus, a generalised cross-talk inversion is developed using the two different values of the coefficient between the adjacent strip with the highest charge, labelled $N_\mathrm{max}$, and the other adjacent strip, labelled $N_\mathrm{min}$, to correct the saturated maximum $Q_\mathrm{max}$:

\begin{align}
    Q_\mathrm{max} &= a_\mathrm{LR} \cdot N_\mathrm{max} + b_\mathrm{LR} \cdot N_\mathrm{min} \\
    \Longleftrightarrow \quad \frac{Q_\mathrm{max}}{N_\mathrm{max}} &= a_\mathrm{LR} + \frac{b_\mathrm{LR}}{\frac{N_\mathrm{max}}{N_\mathrm{min}}}
    \label{eq:LRcorr}
\end{align}

The idea is to plot the $Q_\mathrm{max}/N_\mathrm{max}$ value as a function of $N_\mathrm{max}/N_\mathrm{min}$. Thus, if \cref{eq:LRcorr} is valid, one could observe the tendency following $\displaystyle f_\mathrm{LR}(x)=a_\mathrm{LR}+\frac{b_\mathrm{LR}}{x}$.

Templates are generated for each barrel layer and endcap ring to avoid any dependence on the sensor thickness; two examples are shown in \cref{fig:LRtemplates}. The 2D histograms match the attended behaviour with a decreasing line along the x--axis. However, there is a large region below that curve showing that not every \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters sit on the $f_\mathrm{LR}(x)$ curve.

To evaluate the population of clusters that are in this range, a preliminary fit is performed according to the $f_\mathrm{LR}(x)$ equation. The number of clusters sitting below 85\% of the fit curve represents 14\% of the total number of \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters, or in other words, \cref{eq:LRcorr} describes 86\% of the population well.

After the preliminary fit, another one is performed on 85\% of the data above. Examples of the preliminary and final fits on a layer of the tracker barrel are shown in \cref{fig:LRfitcorr}.

\subsection{\textit{FullLeft} and \textit{FullRight} clusters}

Finally, the last clusters are labelled as \textit{FullLeft} and \textit{FullRight} clusters if they satisfy the following criteria:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cluster with at least two strips.
    \item Saturated maximum at the beginning or at the end of the cluster.
    \item Adjacent strip of the saturated maximum must fulfilled $N_\mathrm{max} \geq 16$ ADC counts for 320 $\mu$m strip sensors or $N_\mathrm{max} \geq 25$ ADC counts for the 500 $\mu$m ones.
\end{itemize}

Then, similarly to what has been done for the other categories, the charge of the maximum is corrected thanks to the adjacent strip $N_\mathrm{max}$:

\begin{equation}
    Q_\mathrm{max} = a_\mathrm{FL,FR} \cdot N_\mathrm{max}
    \label{eq:FLFRcross}
\end{equation}

The value of $a_\mathrm{FL}$ and $a_\mathrm{FR}$ are extracted after taking the maximum of the $Q_\mathrm{max}/N_\mathrm{max}$ histogram templates. Two of them are shown in \cref{fig:FLFRhists}. As \textit{FullLeft} and \textit{FullRight} clusters represent only a very small fraction of the saturated clusters, the maximum of the histogram is chosen instead of performing a fit due to insufficient statistics. Values of $a_\mathrm{FL}$ and $a_\mathrm{FR}$ are grouped in \cref{tab:FLRValue}.

\subsection{Two strip cases}
\label{subsection:twostrips}

Single saturated strip clusters represent about 90\% of saturated clusters in muon datasets, while the clusters with two consecutive saturated strips stand for 5 to 8\%. Thus, a correction on those two types of clusters leads to an extensive coverage of saturated clusters.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

For such clusters, shape classification is no longer possible. Due to the loss of information from the two saturated strips, it is no longer clear which strip was more saturated than the other.
A first-order correction is therefore applied, selecting only those clusters that satisfy the following criteria:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cluster with at least four strips.
    \item Saturated maximum not at the beginning, nor at the end of the cluster.
    \item The two saturated strips must be side by side.
\end{itemize}

Then a cross-talk inversion is performed using the first adjacent strips adjacent to the two saturated strips, namely $L_\mathrm{max}$ and $R_\mathrm{max}$. Similar to the \cref{fig:XtalkInversion} but with two saturated strips instead of one: 

\begin{equation}
    Q_\mathrm{corr} = a_\mathrm{2strips} \cdot \frac{L_\mathrm{max}+R_\mathrm{max}}{2}
    \label{eq:twostripscorr}
\end{equation}
with $a_\mathrm{2strips}=1/0.0613$ being calibrated on the bTbM sample by looking after the average shape of such clusters and the proportion of charge being on each strip.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

As the bTbM sample does not provide enough clusters with two consecutive saturated strips, the intermediate validation cannot be done. Thus, the performance of this method will be evaluated on data, as with the other single saturated strip clusters.

A final validation is done using the Monte Carlo simulation of W bosons produced with jets and the 2018-era D muon dataset, both of which were already presented in \cref{subsubsection:satstripclusters}. The following selections are used to keep only the good tracks with sufficient \dedx\, and the deposits are retained:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $|\eta|<2.4$.
    \item Track coming from the PV ($|d_z|<0.1$ cm and $|d_{xy}|<0.02$ cm).
    \item \textit{HighPurity} tracks with $\chi^2/\mathrm{NDOF}<5$ and 80\% of valid hits.
    \item At least 10 \dedx\,measurements.
\end{itemize}

The W+jets process does not describe all the muons collected in the muon dataset; thus, some key distributions differ, including pseudorapidity $\eta$ and transverse momentum $p_T$. To match the MC to the data, the ratio between the 2D plot of $p_T$ as a function of $\eta$ in data is done with respect to the same one for MC. Then, the weight $w_i=\left(\eta_i\,,\,p_{T,i}\right)_\mathrm{data/MC}$, which is the bin content of the ratio of the two 2D histograms, is applied on the MC simulation.

Finally, the performance of the correction is evaluated on clusters with a maximum ADC count of 240-250. The maximum strip is artificially set to 240 ADC counts, and then the correction method is applied. With this, the actual charge of the cluster, $Q_\mathrm{true}$, is known, and the corrected charge, $Q_\mathrm{corr}$, is compared with it by taking their ratio. The results of both correction methods are plotted in \cref{fig:1strip2stripcorr_dataWjets}.

Foremost, there is the discrepancy between the data and Monte Carlo, even after rescaling to match the $p_T$ and $\eta$ distributions as closely as possible. The main explanation is the W+jets sample that does not describe all the standard muon processes, moreover for high $p_T$ that may arise from Drell-Yan\footnote{The Drell-Yan process is a hard scattering mechanism in which a quark from one hadron and an antiquark from another annihilate to produce a virtual photon or Z boson, which then decays into a lepton-antilepton pair.} or $t\overline{t}$ processes. Another point worth noting is the width of the two ratios; as the correction was derived from a sample of muons for which the saturated charge was known, it is to be expected that the agreement is not as good as that shown in \cref{fig:1strip2stripcorr_btbM}. Besides this, as already discussed, the basic correction is biased, with most clusters under-corrected, while the shape correction is in better agreement with the cluster's actual charge. Further details of each ratio are listed in \cref{tab:corrResults}

This final table shows the improvement of the method over a basic cross-talk inversion with a fixed factor. The proposed correction is not further biased, corrects a larger population of saturated clusters, and yields a higher population of well-reconstructed saturated charge.

\subsection{Improvements and criticism}
\label{subsection:Improvements}

The saturated cluster correction method developed here shows good results on the charge reconstruction of muon deposits in Monte Carlo and data samples. A cross-talk inversion-based algorithm has been adapted to accommodate different cluster shapes and numbers of consecutive saturated strips. As the population of saturated clusters consists mostly of those with a single saturated strip followed by two consecutive saturated strips, the other saturated clusters are not corrected by this approach. 

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The over-corrected clusters are mostly the \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} ones sitting under the $ f_\mathrm{LR}(x)=a_\mathrm{LR}+b_\mathrm{LR}/x$ fit. Such clusters have high charge sharing between the maximum and the adjacent strips, as shown in \cref{fig:miscorrectedLR}, leading to poor later correction.

One could improve the correction by using a lower threshold for the ratio of $N_\mathrm{max}$ to $N_\mathrm{min}$, which is currently fixed at 10\%. Adjusting this value before performing a new fit and assessing the correction showed that a lower threshold led to more over-corrected clusters. In contrast, a higher one led to a worse \textit{Center} clusters correction (the $a_\mathrm{center}$ factor no longer described every \textit{Center} cluster). This is consistent with the observation that well-corrected clusters exhibit a small saturated-to-total charge ratio.

Another method, which I didn't push, would be to consider the values of the second adjacent strips in the model to distinguish between high charge sharing, leading to higher charge on the second adjacent strips, and lower charge on the lower one. In the same vein, considering the barycentre of charge of a cluster was also explored at some point after the shape classification. The least-bad approach was to reconstruct the saturated maximum using the difference between the strip index of the maximum, $i_\mathrm{max}$, and the barycentre. This method could have been an alternative for the \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters, but it yielded poor selection within the saturated cluster population, with only 80\% of them corrected. On top of not being accurate with 20\% of clusters with $Q_\mathrm{bar.\,corr}/Q_\mathrm{true}>1.05$ and with negative corrected charge due to $i_\mathrm{max}-\mathrm{bar.}$ difference.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Concerning the correction of clusters with two consecutive saturated strips presented in \cref{subsection:twostrips}, the validation was only possible using the data due to the lack of statistics in the MC samples. The $Q_\mathrm{shape\,corr}/Q_\mathrm{true}$ ratio is shown in \cref{fig:2stripcorr}. The cross-talk inversion corrects 77\% of these clusters, with the rest being clusters with a saturated strip at the beginning or the end. The ratio is not close to unity, with a large population of clusters being under- and over-corrected. While this provides a useful first estimate, a more robust method would be desirable.

Regarding the calibration samples, the derivation of the layer-by-layer correction parameters was constrained by the limited size of the bTbM sample (600,000 events). A more in-depth study would benefit from using a W+jets sample, coupled with other processes with a wider muon $p_T$ range, such as Drell-Yan or $t\overline{t}$, to also study saturated deposits outside the MIP point and thus propose parameters more in line with the data.



\section[d\textit{E}/d\textit{x} performance studies with 2024--2025 CMS Data]{d\textit{E}/d\textit{x} performance studies with 2024--2025 \\ CMS Data}

The CMS Tracker Physics Object Group approved the method of the shape correction. Thus, it has been tested on large-scale data for performance plots of the tracker for 2024 and 2025; the CMS Tracker Detector Performance Group approved those plots. At the moment, only the early 2024 performance results are published \cite{DPnote2024dedx}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The procedure consists of selecting all \textit{HighPurity} tracks from the PV that have at least 10 \dedx\,deposits and $p_T$ between 0.5 and 50 GeV/$c$. Then, a 2D histogram of the average ionisation $I_h$ as a function of the track momentum $p$ is filled using the shape-saturation correction.

The maxima of \dedx\,for each bin in $p$ are fitted using a Landau function convoluted with a Gaussian to describe the \dedx\,deposits with thermal noise. Examples of the distributions are shown in \cref{fig:langausFit}. The different \dedx\, maxima come from the different charged particles produced and identified by the PF algorithm, for which their energy deposit, that is decreasing at higher $p$, is described by the Bethe-Bloch formula.

Then, by knowing the particle mass $M$ and its momentum $p$, one can derive a simpler description of the \dedx\,deposit as a function of $\beta\gamma=(p/Mc)$:

{\small
\begin{equation}
    \left\langle \frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}x} \right\rangle = p_1 \left( \frac{\sqrt{(\beta\gamma)^4+4(\beta\gamma)^2}-(\beta\gamma)^2}{2} \right)^\frac{p_2}{2}\cdot \ln\left( 1 + [ p_3\cdot\beta\gamma ]^{p_4} \right) - p_5 = f(\beta\gamma)
    \label{eq:fitAtlas}
\end{equation}
}
with $p_{1,...,5}$ the five parameters extracted after the fit. This fit is performed on each \dedx\,maximum at low $p$ on the proton line and at higher $p$ on the pion line. Finally, the fit is extrapolated to the other particles.

\section{Impact on the search for HSCP}

A natural way to assess the impact of the shape correction beyond tracker performance plots is to look at the reconstructed mass of the 2018 HSCP signal sample. This variable is not the true physical mass. Instead, it is a mass derived from the ionisation curve, assuming the particles deposit energy according to the Bethe-Bloch formula. HSCPs are predicted to be slow and highly ionising. Therefore, any improvement in the saturation correction should translate into a better estimate of the reconstructed mass.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The procedure mirrors that described in the 2024 data performance study. For each of the three correction scenarios from the 2018 HSCP sample presented in \cref{subsubsection:satstripclusters}, a 2D histogram is filled. This histogram shows the average ionisation $I_h$ as a function of track momentum $p$. Before filling these histograms, the Run--2 HSCP pre-selections detailed in Section XXXX are applied, except the HLT muon trigger. The results are shown in \cref{fig:Ih_vs_p_HSCP}. 

The same behaviour observed in the data is also seen in the high-$p$ regime, due to the gluino's high mass (here $m_{\widetilde{g}}=2000$ GeV/$c^2$). As saturated clusters represent almost 45 \% of the total \dedx\, clusters in this sample (see \cref{tab:WjetHSCPsat}), without any saturation correction, the curve is capping to lower $I_h$ values compared to the one computed from the two correction methods. The vertical band at low $p$ corresponds to some tracks that don't pass the HLT muon trigger.
