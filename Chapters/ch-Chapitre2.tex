\section{Interaction of charged particles with matter}

\indent A charged particle passing through a material loses energy through ionisation or Coulomb scattering. Energy loss through ionisation in a material is described by the Bethe-Bloch formula  in the case of a particle with a mass $M$ much greater than that of an electron $m_e$ \cite{PDG}:

\begin{equation} 
    \left\langle - \frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}x} \right\rangle = Kz^2 \frac{Z}{A} \frac{1}{\beta^2} \left[ \frac{1}{2} \ln \left( \frac{2m_ec^2\beta^2\gamma^2W_{\mathrm{max}}}{I^2} \right) - \beta^2 - \frac{\delta(\beta\gamma)}{2} \right]
    \label{eq:BetheBloch_formula}
\end{equation}

\noindent with

\begin{equation}
    W_{\max} = \frac{2m_e c^2 \beta^2 \gamma^2}{1 + 2\gamma m_e/M + (m_e/M)^2}
    \label{eq:Wmax}
\end{equation}

\noindent and

\begin{equation}
    \delta(\beta\gamma) = \begin{cases} 2(\ln 10)x - \overline{C} & \text{if } x \geq x_1; \\ 2(\ln 10)x - \overline{C} + a(x_1 - x)^k & \text{if } x_0 \leq x < x_1; \\ 0 & \text{if } x < x_0 \textrm{ (non conductors)}; \\ \delta_0 10^{2(x - x_0)} & \text{if } x < x_0 \text{ (conductors)} \end{cases}
    \label{eq:deltabetagamma}
\end{equation}

where the term $\beta\gamma= p/Mc$ represents the kinematics of the particle with mass $M$ and momentum $p$. The term $\beta$ is equal to $v/c$ (where $v$ is the velocity of the particle), $A$ and $Z$ correspond respectively to the mass number and atomic number of the material traversed, $z$ is the algebraic charge of the particle and $K\simeq0.307075 \,\, \textrm{MeV.mol}^{-1}.\textrm{cm}^2$. $W_{\mathrm{max}}$ [MeV] is the maximum energy transferred in a single collision described in \cref{eq:Wmax}, $I$ [eV] is the average excitation energy of the material, and $\delta(\beta\gamma)$ is the density correction for the ultra-relativistic regime.

As for the latter, \cref{eq:deltabetagamma} gives an empirical expression depending on the $\beta\gamma$ regime with $x=\log_{10}(\beta\gamma)$; $x_0$, $x_1$, $\overline{C}$, $a$, $k$ and $\delta_0$ are constants related to the material, one can find numerical values of them in \cite{DeltaBetaGamma}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The average stopping power $\langle \dedx \rangle $ is usually depicted in MeV.$\mathrm{g}^{-1}.\mathrm{cm}^2$. By multiplying this value by the material density, the average ionisation loss is therefore expressed in [MeV/cm]. The mean energy deposit per unit of length value depends on $p/M$, so that for slow moving particle and/or heavy ones, \dedx\, decreases as $-\ln(p/M)$ before reaching a minimum at around $3-4$ MeV/cm for $\beta\gamma\sim3-4$, before gradually increasing due to the relativistic-regime term induced by $\delta(\beta\gamma)$. \cref{fig:BetheBloch} shows the energy deposition per unit distance travelled for different particles passing through silicon according to \cref{eq:BetheBloch_formula}.


\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Images/Ch2/bethebloch_multiparticle_Si.pdf}
    \caption{Average ionisation loss of muons, pions, kaons and protons in the silicon derived from \cref{eq:BetheBloch_formula}. Numerical values of the parameters were taken in \cite{DeltaBetaGamma}.}
    \label{fig:BetheBloch}
\end{figure}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Most of the particles produced by proton-proton collisions at CMS are low-energy particles, which release some of their energy at the minimum of the curve. The silicon tracker has been optimised to detect such particles, labelled as \textit{Minimum Ionising Particles} (MIPs).

Note that for high relativistic particles at $\beta\gamma\sim1000$, the average deposit is 2.5 times higher than the MIP. Thus, by measuring the \dedx\, information, one could identify the $\beta\gamma$ regime of the particle. This information will later be used to discriminate between HSCPs' deposits and SM processes.


\vspace{0.5 cm}

While the Bethe–Bloch formula provides an accurate description of the average energy loss over a given path length, it becomes inadequate when considering thin absorbers such as the strip or pixel silicon sensors of the CMS tracker, where the energy deposit is measured locally, layer by layer.

In this regime, the number of collisions experienced by the particle is relatively small, and the deposited energy is no longer a well-defined quantity but must instead be described by a probability distribution. This distribution is dominated by rare but large energy transfers to $\delta$-rays\footnote{Secondary electrons with enough energy to escape a significant distance away from the primary radiation beam and produce further ionisation.}, which distort it away from a symmetric Gaussian shape and produce a characteristic asymmetric tail toward large energy losses.

In 1944, Landau addressed this problem analytically. Starting from the differential cross section for individual collisions, he showed that in the limit of a sufficiently thin absorber the energy-loss fluctuations follow a universal functional form, now known as the \textit{Landau distribution} \cite{Landau}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

As the tail goes to infinity, the Landau distribution does not have a finite mean and standard deviation. Instead, it is commonly described by the \textit{Most Probable Value} (MPV), defined as the position of the maximum of the Landau distribution. The MPV depends on the thickness of the material crossed by the particle and increases approximately linearly with it, as a thicker layer results in a larger number of ionising collisions. 

The Landau Probability Density Function (PDF) is a function of the particle mass and momentum, the crossed material and the thickness of the latter. An example is shown in \cref{fig:landau} for a pion with a 10 GeV/$c$ momentum going through two different silicon sensors with different thickness.

Note that particles can't actually lose more energy than their total budget; thus, the infinite tail of the Landau distribution is purely conceptual.

\newpage

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering

    \caption{Landau PDF for a 10 GeV pion passing through a 320~$\mu$m and 500~$\mu$m silicon sensor.}
    \label{fig:landau}
\end{figure}

Another important point is the dependency between the MPV, the relativistic regime $\beta\gamma$, and the material thickness. The slower the particle is, the higher the MPV is, and, in the relativistic regime, the MPV converges asymptotically to a value that depends solely on the thickness of the material. An example of the evolution of the MPV in two different thicknesses of silicon sensors is depicted on \cref{fig:MPV}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The Landau distribution provides information on the total energy deposited in a finite silicon layer (here for 320 and 500 $\mu\mathrm{m}$), while the MPV curve gives the energy loss per unit length (MeV/cm). Multiplying the MPV at the MIP value ($\sim$2.6-2.7 MeV/cm on Silicon) by the layer thickness naturally yields a deposit in the keV range, consistent with the MPV observed in the Landau PDF of \cref{fig:landau}.

    \caption{Most Probable Value of the Landau PDF as a function of the relativistic regime $\beta\gamma$ for two silicon sensors of different thickness.}
    \label{fig:MPV}
\end{figure}


\vspace{0.5 cm}

Finally, one could also present a more general description of energy-loss fluctuations, provided by the \textit{Vavilov distribution}, which extends the Landau model to absorbers of finite thickness. In the limit of very thin materials, it reduces to the Landau distribution, while for thicker absorbers it gradually approaches a Gaussian shape. This behaviour reflects the Central Limit Theorem: as the number of independent collisions increases, the sum of many small energy transfers tends toward a normal distribution \cite{VavilovFunctionPixels}.

This function is, for instance, used in the simulation of charge deposits in pixel sensors. Particles at high pseudo-rapidity traverse a larger amount of material than those produced in the central region; thus, the Landau distribution is no longer representative of their energy distribution. Incorporating this effect into the simulation enables a more accurate tuning of the detector response, bringing it into closer agreement with the behaviour observed in data.



\section{Physics of the silicon tracker modules}

A typical semiconductor detector is composed of a sensitive layer along with read-out electronics responsible for processing the analogue signals. The following section will cover the underlying physics governing semiconductor sensors and will present the typical read-out electronics chain used for data acquisition of silicon strip sensors.

\subsection{Semiconductor sensors}

\subsubsection{Basic concept of semiconductor physics}

The Pauli exclusion principle states that two identical fermions can not occupy the same quantum state simultaneously. The distribution of energy levels in fermionic systems is therefore described by the Fermi-Dirac distribution, as expressed in \cref{eq:fermidirac}.

\begin{equation}
    f(E)=\frac{1}{1+\exp\left( \displaystyle \frac{E-E_F}{k_BT} \right)}
    \label{eq:fermidirac}
\end{equation}

where $k_B$ denotes the Boltzmann constant, $T$ the temperature of the system, and $E_F$ the Fermi level.

The distribution gives the probability density of finding a fermion at a given energy, while the Fermi level corresponds to the energy at which this probability equals 1/2. At $T$ = 0 K, all energy states up to $E_F$ are filled, and as the temperature rises, electrons begin to populate higher-energy excited states. 

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Unlike isolated atoms, where electrons occupy discrete orbitals with well-defined binding energies, electrons in crystalline solids arrange themselves into continuous energy bands. The band structure of various materials is illustrated schematically in \cref{fig:EnergyBand}. The valence band is the highest band fully occupied at $T$ = 0 K, while the partially filled band above it is known as the conduction band. Between these two bands lies a forbidden region with no allowed energy states, referred to as the band gap. Electrons residing in the conduction band are those that contribute to the electrical conductivity of the material. 

Based on this band picture, materials can be classified into three broad categories: conductors, semiconductors, and insulators. Conductors already possess electrons in the conduction band at $T=0$~K, whereas semiconductors and insulators do not, making them behave similarly at absolute zero. At elevated temperatures, however, thermally excited electrons in semiconductors can acquire enough energy to bridge the band gap, rendering the material conductive. These thermally generated carriers are known as intrinsic \textit{charge carriers}, and a semiconductor free of any impurities is accordingly called an intrinsic semiconductor.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \input{Images/Ch2/EnergyBand.tex}
    \caption{Sketch of the energy band structure for three different materials at $T=0$ K: insulators, semiconductors and conductors. The darker part represents the states containing electrons.}
    \label{fig:EnergyBand}
\end{figure}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Band theory is based on chemical bonds between atoms, which arise from electron shell interactions. The valence shell, in particular, determines an element's electrical conductivity. A completely filled valence shell makes an element chemically inert, gaseous, and poorly conductive. A nearly full shell, as in F, Cl, and Br, forms non-conductive solids called insulators. Elements with a single valence electron, such as Li, Na, and Cu, are good conductors because the electron moves freely across the lattice. Atoms like C, Si, and Ge, which have four valence electrons, form covalent bonds by sharing electrons with neighbours. These elements, known as semiconductors, bind their electrons less tightly than insulators, enabling conduction under certain conditions.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Electrical conductivity in a crystal lattice is driven by the movement of charge carriers, namely electrons and holes. When an electron vacates its position, it leaves behind an empty site in the lattice, referred to as a hole. This hole is quickly occupied by a neighbouring electron, which in turn creates a new vacancy elsewhere. Through this successive displacement mechanism, holes effectively travel across the lattice, giving rise to hole conductivity. The velocity at which electrons and holes move varies depending on both the material and the electric field applied. In an intrinsic semiconductor, electrons and holes are present in equal numbers, meaning that neither carrier type is dominant.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The conduction properties of semiconductors can be tailored by deliberately introducing impurities into the crystal lattice, a process known as doping, which is schematically illustrated for silicon in \cref{fig:DopedSemiConductor}.

To enhance electron conductivity, atoms carrying an extra valence electron are incorporated into the lattice. These impurity atoms are called donors, and the resulting material is designated as n-type, or n-doped, semiconductor. In this case, Phosphorus, which possesses five valence electrons, is a typical donor atom. P-type semiconductors, on the other hand, are obtained by introducing acceptor atoms into the intrinsic semiconductor, these being atoms with one fewer electron in their outer shell, making hole conductivity the dominant transport mechanism. Boron is commonly used as an acceptor in p-doped silicon.

\subsubsection{p--n junctions}

Semiconductor sensors are built around a p--n junction, which consists of a p-type and an n-type semiconductor brought into contact. The corresponding energy state diagram for doped semiconductors and a p--n junction is depicted in \cref{fig:PNEnergy}.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \input{Images/Ch2/PNEnergy.tex}
    \caption{Energy diagram of doped semiconductors and a p--n junction. $E_A$ and $E_D$ are the energy levels of acceptor and donor atoms respectively.}
    \label{fig:PNEnergy}
\end{figure}


Doping shifts the Fermi energy level as a result of the increased concentration of charge carriers in the  semiconductor. At the interface between the p-type and n-type regions, electrons and holes begin to recombine, leaving a positively charged region on the n-doped side and a negatively charged region on the p-doped side. The resulting built-in electric field counteracts further diffusion of charge carriers, giving rise to a carrier-free region known as the \textit{depletion zone}.

This zone can be extended by attaching electrodes to both sides of the junction to establish electrical contacts, and then applying an external voltage across the p--n junction. When a positive voltage is applied to the n-side, the induced electric field draws charge carriers away from the junction, and beyond a certain voltage threshold, the field extends across the entire thickness of the sensor, which is then said to be fully depleted. This threshold, known as the \textit{full depletion voltage}, depends on both the doping concentration and the thickness of the sensor.


\subsection{Silicon strip sensors}

The CMS detector makes use of silicon p--n junctions of the tracker for particle detection. Among the various natural semiconductors, the collaboration opted for silicon-based sensors for various reasons. It is the most common semiconductor (thus cheaper), with natural radiation tolerance and an efficient band gap, and a low electron-hole pair creation energy threshold.

A silicon strip sensor module is composed of several distinct elements, each playing a specific role in the detection chain. \Cref{fig:SiliconStripSensor} provides a schematic overview of such a module, the key components of which are described in the following.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Images/Ch2/SiliconStripSensorSketch.png}
    \caption{Sketch of a silicon strip module with its different subparts \cite{SiliconLayout}.}
    \label{fig:SiliconStripSensor}
\end{figure}


\textbf{General structure}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The sensor consists of a phosphorus-doped n-type silicon bulk forming the active detection volume. Multiple $\mathrm{p^+}$ strips are implanted on the front face, creating a p--n junction that, under reverse bias, depletes the bulk and enables charge collection.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\textbf{Signal generation}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

As a MIP traverses the depleted silicon bulk, it generates approximately 70 electron-hole pairs per micrometre of sensor thickness, each requiring an energy of 3.62 eV \cite{ElectronHolePairsEnergy}. This is significantly lower than in gaseous detectors (34.6\,eV for an $\mathrm{H_2}$-based detector). For a 300\,$\mu$m thick sensor, this corresponds to $\sim 2\times10^4$ electron-hole pairs per MIP.

Under the applied electric field, holes drift toward the $\mathrm{p^+}$ strips while electrons drift toward the $\mathrm{n^+}$ backplane, with all carriers being collected within $\mathcal{O}(10\,\mathrm{ns})$. However, this signal is several orders of magnitude smaller than the \textit{leakage current}, arising from the $\sim 10^8$ electron-hole pairs continuously generated by thermal excitation within the bulk. This parasitic current constitutes a significant source of noise and is further enhanced by radiation-induced lattice defects. To mitigate this effect, the sensors are cooled to suppress thermal generation and improve the signal-to-noise ratio for MIP charge collection \cite{ElectronHoleInSilicon}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\textbf{Front side readout}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Each strip is read out via an AC-coupled pad, which blocks the DC leakage current while transmitting the signal to the readout chip through a bond wire. A polyresistor connects the DC pad to the bias ring, allowing strip biasing while preserving AC isolation.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\textbf{Edge structures}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Two surrounding $\mathrm{p^+}$ rings protect the active volume. The bias ring is connected to ground and biases the strips, while the guard ring grades the electric field at the sensor periphery, preventing the depletion region from reaching the physical edges and avoiding breakdown. This part of the device sits on a $\mathrm{SiO_2/Si_3N_4}$ insulating layer deposited on the front surface to protect and electrically insulate the surface structures.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\textbf{Backside}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

A heavily doped $\mathrm{n^+}$ implant on the rear face ensures an ohmic contact with the aluminium backplane electrode, through which the positive high voltage is applied to fully deplete the sensor bulk.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\textbf{Geometric parameters}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The centre-to-centre distance between adjacent strips defines the strip pitch $P$, while their implant width is denoted $W$; these are the key geometric parameters of the sensor summarised in \cref{tab:tracker_modules}. Since the silicon strip tracker spans a large tracking volume, both parameters vary with the position of the module within the tracker to keep the $W/P$ ratio close to 0.25. This value has been chosen to minimise the total strip capacitance while maintaining a sufficiently large electric field peak at the $\mathrm{p^+}$ strip edges.



\subsection{Read-out pipeline}

Once a charged particle has generated electron-hole pairs in the bulk material, the drift of these electrons induces a current that is then read out. The resulting current is transmitted via a pitch adapter\footnote{Here the pitch adapter bridges the geometrical mismatch between the strip pitch and the readout electronics}, to the Front-End Hybrid (FEH), where the APV25 (a 25 $\mu$m thick CMOS \textit{Analogue Pipeline Voltage}) front-end chips are located \cite{ReadoutStrip}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Each APV25 chip reads out 128 strips. Given that strip modules comprise either 512 or 768 strips, between four and six APV25 chips are required per sensor. Within each chip, the charge signal first passes through a preamplifier, which converts it into a voltage step. A unity-gain inverter is then applied to normalise the signal polarity, ensuring a positive voltage step regardless of whether the signal originates from electrons or holes. The signal subsequently enters a CR-RC shaper\footnote{Analogue filtering stage that differentiates the input signal to remove the DC baseline (CR high-pass filter) and then integrates it to limit the bandwidth and optimise the signal-to-noise ratio (RC low-pass filter).}, which produces a 40\,MHz voltage pulse. Two operating modes are available: the \textit{peak mode}, which samples the pulse at its maximum, and the \textit{deconvolution mode}, which computes a weighted sum over three consecutive samples separated by 25\,ns each. Whilst peak mode suffers from a pulse length of approximately 200\,ns (longer than the 25\,ns LHC bunch-crossing interval), deconvolution mode effectively confines the signal to a single bunch crossing and is therefore the standard mode during physics data-taking.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The analogue output of pairs of APV25 chips is time-multiplexed and transmitted via analogue optical links to the Front-End Drivers (FEDs), located outside the CMS detector within the experimental cavern. At the FED level, the signal from each channel is digitised using a 10--bit analogue-to-digital converter (ADC), yielding what is referred to as a \textit{raw digi}. The FEDs then operate in zero-suppressed mode, performing two successive noise subtractions on a chip-by-chip basis. The first subtraction removes the \textit{pedestal}, defined as the average baseline activity of a given strip in the absence of any particle signal. The second subtraction addresses the Common Mode Noise (CMN), an electronic baseline fluctuation common to all 128 channels of a given APV25 chip, estimated as the median over those channels for each event. Both the pedestal and noise levels are evaluated during dedicated calibration runs, performed several times per year outside of standard physics data-taking periods.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Following these subtractions, channels returning negative ADC values are set to zero, since the FEDs handle positive values only. A zero-suppression threshold is then applied: individual channels with a signal-to-noise ratio $S/N$ above 5 are retained, as are groups of adjacent strips provided each neighbouring channel exceeds $S/N > 2$. The 10--bit ADC range is subsequently truncated to 8 bits according to the following scheme:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Channels with a digitised signal below 254 counts are left unchanged;
    \item Those with a digitised signal between 254 and 1023 counts are set to 254;
    \item Channels at 1024 counts are assigned a value of 255.
\end{itemize}
Channels with zero ADC counts are suppressed entirely, thereby reducing the overall data rate.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

In addition to signal digitisation, the Front-End Controllers (FEC) distribute global timing, trigger, and command information from the Timing, Trigger and Control (TTC) system to the front-end electronics via the Communication and Control Unit (CCU). The FEC also handles the configuration of the front-end electronics and monitors key operational parameters, including sensor temperature, leakage current, and bias voltage, through the Detector Control Unit (DCU).

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Finally, one can convert the charge collected in ADC to MeV by knowing the electron-hole energy pair creation and the effective gain of the read-out chain (APV25 + FED), the latter being determined experimentally during calibration runs. This conversion is shown in \cref{eq:ADCfactorToMeV}.

\begin{equation}
    Q[\mathrm{ADC \, count}]= 3.62 \cdot10^{-6} \times 265 \times Q[\mathrm{MeV}]
    \label{eq:ADCfactorToMeV}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Cluster properties}

As described in \cref{subsubsection:Hitreconstruction}, collected charges in adjacent strips are grouped into clusters. The width of the cluster, i.e., the number of strips, is affected by two physical processes: the \textit{charge sharing} and the \textit{cross-talk}.

\subsubsection{Charge sharing}

As depicted in \cref{fig:SiliconStripSensor}, a MIP crossing the n-bulk creates electron-hole pairs that can be captured by several strips depending on the incident angle of the particle. According to the Lorentz force, the movement of the charge carriers is also affected by the internal electric field between the electrodes and the external magnetic field. The latter causes a drift of about 5° in the barrel sensors, resulting in a position shift of the order of 10 to 20 $\mu$m \cite{ChargeCharingDrift}.

One could also mention the charge diffusion induced by the collisions between charge carriers, therefore causing a small drift along the path.

These two phenomena combined together have a small effect compared to the pitch size (the smaller value being 80 $\mu$m), but are taken into account in the simulation of charge spreading.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

To illustrate that, \cref{fig:clusterCenteredShifted} shows two clusters of charges collected in a silicon strip sensor. These clusters originate from a Monte-Carlo simulation of back-to-back muons generated with a $p_T$ ranging from 50 to 200 GeV/$c$ with a pseudo-rapidity range $|\eta|<2.1$ without any electronic saturation, noise or pile-up contamination.

The charge collected on each strip is expressed in ADC counts, while the strip index identifies the position of the strip within the cluster. The left cluster comes from a muon crossing a TIB module with an angle with respect to the normal of the module of 0.16 rad ($\sim9$°). The effect of charge sharing is not very important, but the effect of cross-talk is clearly visible (developed below). On the other hand, the right cluster corresponds to a muon with an incident angle of 1.23 rad ($\sim71$°). This tilted approach leads to high charge collection in strips 436 and 437 compared to the other adjacent strips.

\subsubsection{Cross-talk}
\label{subsubsection:crosstalk}

In the CMS silicon strip sensor, each strip is capacitively coupled both to the backplane and to its immediate neighbours through the inter-strip capacitance \cite{CapacitiveCoupling}. This is inherent to the sensor geometry itself: the inter-strip capacitance arises from the proximity of adjacent $\mathrm{p^+}$ implants, while the backplane capacitance couples each strip to the aluminium backplane through the bulk, as illustrated in \cref{fig:SiliconStripSensor}.

This capacitive network has a direct consequence on the charge measurement: when a particle deposits charge on a given strip, a fraction of that charge is redistributed to the adjacent strips through capacitive coupling. This effect is referred to as \textit{cross-talk}, and must be carefully accounted for in the cluster charge reconstruction.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The magnitude of the charge sharing is parametrised as follows. For a cluster of total charge $Q$, the strip with the largest charge fraction receives $x_0 \times Q$, its two immediate neighbours each receive $x_1 \times Q$, and the next-to-nearest neighbours receive $x_2 \times Q$. For a common 5-strip width cluster, this leads to the following equation: $\displaystyle x_0 + 2\cdot x_1 + 2 \cdot x_2 = 1$. If a MIP particle crosses the sensor with an incident angle close to zero with respect to the normal, this leads to pyramid-shaped clusters, as the left cluster of \cref{fig:clusterCenteredShifted}.

It should be noted that these parameters are not fixed but depend on the readout mode of the APV25 chip, since the amount of charge sharing is sensitive to the sampling time of the signal. The values of $x_0$, $x_1$ and $x_2$ have been extracted from data in deconvolution mode, using muon tracks traversing the full tracker with trajectories close to normal incidence to the sensor plane, and were evaluated both in 2010 and 2018 to monitor potential variations over time \cite{Xtalk2018CMS}. Values of those three parameters measured in the tracker barrel for different years are shown on \cref{fig:Xtalk}. Due to ageing, $x_1$ and $x_2$ are smaller than at the beginning of the last decade; thus, clusters tend to shrink.

\subsection{Radiation effects}

The performance of silicon tracking detectors inevitably degrades under the intense radiation environment of the LHC. Energy loss of traversing particles occurs through two distinct mechanisms: Ionising Energy Loss (IEL), arising from collisions with atomic electrons, and Non-Ionising Energy Loss (NIEL), in which nuclear interactions displace lattice atoms. Both processes induce radiation damage, which can be classified as bulk or surface damage.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Bulk damage, primarily driven by NIEL, arises when recoiling atoms displace neighbouring lattice sites, forming defect clusters. These introduce new energy levels within the silicon bandgap, leading to increased leakage current, charge carrier trapping, and local electric field distortions, all of which degrade charge collection efficiency and ultimately the sensor resolution.

Surface damage is instead dominated by ionising radiation, which generates positive charges trapped at the Si–Si$\mathrm{O}_2$ interface. This accumulated charge can induce inter-strip short circuits, increasing both the bulk current and the cross-talk between readout channels, thereby degrading the detector noise performance.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Beyond permanent damage, ionising particles can also flip the state of memory cells in the front-end electronics, an effect known as Single Event Upset (SEU). Unlike bulk or surface damage, SEUs are non-permanent and can be recovered by reconfiguring the affected circuits.

To cope with the cumulative effects of permanent radiation damage, detectors can be recalibrated and operated at higher bias voltages and lower temperatures. However, once the signal-to-noise ratio becomes too degraded, front-end modules must be replaced. A typical operational lifetime for the inner layers of a silicon tracker at the LHC being of the order of 5 to 10 years.

\subsection{The harmonic ionisation mean \texorpdfstring{$I_h$}{Ih}}

One way to assess the average ionisation of a track along the several silicon strip modules crossed is to use an harmonic mean function. Therefore, $I_h$ is defined to be the harmonic mean of the ionisation of a charged particle track which has deposed $N$ charge clusters $c_j = (\dedx)_j$ along the several silicon strip layers, its expression is given by \cref{eq:ih}.

\begin{equation}
    I_h = \left( \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=0}^N c_j^{-2} \right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
    \label{eq:ih}
\end{equation}

$I_h$ is, by construction, very robust against upward fluctuations of the $c_j$ which have a non-negligible chance to happen (as they are distributed on a Landau with a long tail). On the other hand, this expression is very sensitive to downward fluctuations which are unlikely to randomly happen (if the $c_j$ are indeed following a Landau), but may occur due to detector effects such as noise, baseline voltage drop, etc, ...

\subsection{Study of saturated strip clusters}
\label{subsubsection:satstripclusters}

A preliminary work on saturated strip clusters has been carried on a 2018 Monte-Carlo simulation of two back-to-back muons generated with a $p_T$ range from 50 to 200 GeV/$c$ and a pseudo-rapidity range $|\eta|<3$. The original simulated charge deposit on silicon sensors is saved without any saturation effect and zero-suppression applied. This sample gives a good amount of pure tracks leaving charge deposit at the MIP level within the tracker on top of saving crucial simulated informations for the rest. For the rest of the study, this muon simulation will be labelled as the bTbM sample.

Considering this, saturated clusters are saved in addition of the reconstructed ones (with saturation filters and zero-suppression process applied), as shown in \cref{fig:sidigiandrecocluster}.

A first step was to determine the proportion of saturated clusters in different Monte-Carlo sample compared to a dataset. Muons are particles of interest as part of the search for HSCP, thus, I decided to compared various processes involving muons to make my work less biased. 

On top of the bTbM sample, a 2018 Monte-Carlo simulation of W bosons generated in addition to jets is also considered. These two MC samples reflect standard model muon depositing at the MIP level. In contrast to that, high energy deposit are studied in a 2018 HSCP simulation of gluinos with $m_{\widetilde{g}}=2000$~GeV/$c^2$ where the HSCP is matched with a muon. Finally, an ultra-legacy\footnote{The Ultra Legacy (UL) dataset corresponds to a full reprocessing of the CMS Run 2 data with improved calibrations, alignment corrections, and reprocessed MC simulations, providing the final and definitive version of the Run 2 dataset for physics analyses.} 2018 Era D of muon data is also considered.

In each case, the track of the muon must be a high-purity track with at least 8 \dedx\, deposits to ensure good reconstruction.

As depicted in \cref{tab:WjetHSCPsat}, most of the saturated clusters are the one with a single saturated strip. With, on top of that, more than 99\% of saturated strips, which are a 254--saturation (with a charge between 254 and 1023 ADC count).

As expected, heavier particles, like gluinos, deposit more energy and thus lead to more saturation, with 44 \% of \dedx\, clusters being saturated. On the other hand, for standard model muons, the saturation affects 8.5 to 10\% of the total number of clusters of a track. Note that, the spatial distribution of those clusters are not uniform within the tracker. Most of them are situated in the TOB--TEC junction ($|\eta|\sim 1.8-2.2$) where the position of strip sensors is different: horizontal on the TOB and vertical on the TEC. Thus, a MIP crossing a vertical sensor in the central region deposes an higher \dedx\, due to the higher pathlength.

Saturated clusters are in greater numbers in the TOB layers, where the silicon sensors are 500 $\mu$m thick, leading to more energy deposit. Otherwise, the rest of the layers have between 5 to 10 \% of saturated clusters within the number of clusters of the layer.

\subsection{Shape classification}

Single saturated strip clusters are group into five different labels depending on their shape, as shown in \cref{fig:clustershape}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Center} clusters (C): Originating from particles with a near-normal approach to the sensor, they are the ones where the effect of the cross-talk is the most noticeable. In the bTbM sample, they represent 69\% of the saturated cluster fraction.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Naive saturation correction}

So far the majority of saturated clusters are the \textit{Center} ones for whose the cross-talk is more visible. One could then invert the cross-talk effect knowing the expected fraction of the total charge $Q$ that should be deposited in the first adjacent strips of the saturated maximum. As already presented in \cref{subsubsection:crosstalk}, the value of this fraction (the $x_1$ parameter) has already be measured (see \cref{fig:Xtalk}).

Thus, using equation \cref{eq:xtalkinversion} the corrected charge $Q_{\mathrm{corr}}$ is computed thanks to the charge collected on the left and right strips ($L_{\mathrm{max}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{max}}$) of the saturated maximum assuming that they each correspond to 10\% of the total charge.

\begin{equation}
    Q_{\mathrm{corr}} = \frac{1}{0.1} \cdot \frac{L_{\mathrm{max}}+R_{\mathrm{max}}}{2}
    \label{eq:xtalkinversion}
\end{equation}

A sketch of the result is presented in \cref{fig:XtalkInversion}. 

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \input{Images/Ch2/XtalkInversion.tex}
    \caption{Sketch of the cross-talk inversion on a \textit{Center} saturated cluster, assuming that the single saturated strip count for 78\% of the total charge $Q$ of the cluster.}
    \label{fig:XtalkInversion}
\end{figure}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

This saturation correction method is implemented within the HSCP analysis framework. Saturated clusters must fulfil the following criteria:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The cluster must contains between 2 to 8 strips.
    \item Only one saturated strip is allowed.
    \item The adjacent strip of the saturated maximum must be symmetrical ($|L_{\mathrm{max}}-R_{\mathrm{max}}|<40$ ADC count) and not too low ($L_{\mathrm{max}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{max}}>25$ ADC count). 
\end{itemize}

Considering this, the fraction of saturated clusters that is corrected is close to 66\%, with 85\% of \textit{Center}, 30\% of LR, and 15\% of FLFR clusters that are corrected with this method. However, none of the two-consecutive saturated strip clusters are corrected.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

One can assess the quality of reconstruction by looking after the $Q_{\mathrm{true}}/Q_{\mathrm{corr}}$ ratio and the $Q_{\mathrm{true}}-Q_{\mathrm{corr}}$ difference, with $Q_{\mathrm{true}}$ being the original charge of the cluster. Before applying the saturation correction procedure, the saturated strips are set to 254 or 255 ADC count, depending of the charge, thus simulating a saturated cluster. 

Both results are depicted in \cref{fig:QcorrOldcorrection}. This method leads to a biased correction by having a ratio of less than one. Moreover, the accuracy is not good, with a large spread of corrected charge (the standard deviation is close to 20\%). This come from the fixed value of the cross-talk inversion coefficient at $1/0.1$, a different value depending on the shape and the tracker layer would be more suitable.

On top of that, only two thirds of the saturated cluster population are corrected. This come from the restrictive selection on the saturated maximum neighbourhood ($|L_{\mathrm{max}}-R_{\mathrm{max}}|<40$ ADC count, $L_{\mathrm{max}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{max}}>25$ ADC count). I tried to change value of these two values but this lead to either a larger selection with poor correction (as more misshapen clusters are corrected), or the opposite.

\section{Saturation correction on strip clusters}

This section will focus on clusters with one single saturated strip within it. Therefore, saturated clusters will design these clusters in the following.

A discussion on clusters containing two consecutive saturated strips will then be dedicated.

A presentation of three different methods is first detailled before showing the results.

\subsection{Adaptative method as a function of the cluster shape}

\subsubsection{\textit{Center} clusters}

As shown in \cref{subsection:shapeclassification}, Clusters coming from incident normal muons are the most common ones, they are labelled as \textit{Center} clusters. To be included in this category, saturated clusters must meet the following criteria:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cluster with at least three strips.
    \item Saturated maximum not at the beginning, neither at the end of the cluster.
    \item $0.9 \leq L_{\mathrm{max}}/R_{\mathrm{max}} \leq 1.1$ to select only pyramid-shaped clusters. With $L_{\mathrm{max}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{max}}$ being the name of the left and right adjacent strip of the maximum respectively. The choice of taking $L_{\mathrm{max}}$ as the numerator as the same consequences than taking $R_{\mathrm{max}}$.
\end{itemize}

The effect of cross-talk is most noticeable in these clusters, thus, considering a cross-talk inversion is a good solution to the saturation issue. The value of the saturated maximum $Q_\mathrm{max}$ is corrected using \eqref{eq:CenterCorr}.

\begin{equation}
    Q_\mathrm{max} = a_{\mathrm{center}} \cdot \frac{L_{\mathrm{max}} + R_{\mathrm{max}}}{2}
    \label{eq:CenterCorr}
\end{equation}

with $a_{\mathrm{center}}$ taken to be the maximum of each histogram representing $Q_\mathrm{max}/[(L_{\mathrm{max}} + R_{\mathrm{max}})/2]$. Note that in the bTbM sample, the value of $Q_\mathrm{max}$ is known, even in the case of saturation. These histogram templates are done for each layer of the TIB and TOB and each rings (a wheel can have different sensor geometries, instead of rings) of the TID and TEC, example are shown in \cref{fig:centerhist}.

\subsubsection{\textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters}

Similarly to \textit{Center} clusters, \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters must fulfilled the following criteria to be labelled as so:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cluster with at least three strips.
    \item Saturated maximum not at the beginning, neither at the end of the cluster.
    \item $L_{\mathrm{max}} \geq 1.1\cdot R_{\mathrm{max}}$ or $R_{\mathrm{max}} \geq 1.1\cdot L_{\mathrm{max}}$.
\end{itemize}

Contrary to \textit{Center} clusters, the value of the adjacent strips to the maximum spread along a larger range. The value of the adjacent strip to the maximum with the highest value has been plotted in \cref{fig:adjacentStripsLR} for three different type of clusters, namely \textit{Center}, \textit{Left} and \textit{Right}. Therefore, considering a similar cross-talk inversion as in \cref{eq:CenterCorr} would be a bad correction, leading to over and under-corrected clusters.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{Images/Wolfgang.jpg}
    \caption{Distribution of the charge value of the adjacent strip of the saturated maximum with the highest charge for the \textit{Center}, \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters.}
    \label{fig:adjacentStripsLR}
\end{figure}

The idea is then to use the two different value of coefficient between the adjacent strip with the highest charge, labelled $N_\mathrm{max}$, and the other adjacent strip, labelled $N_\mathrm{min}$, to correct the saturated maximum $Q_\mathrm{max}$:

\begin{align}
    Q_\mathrm{max} &= a_\mathrm{LR} \cdot N_\mathrm{max} + b_\mathrm{LR} \cdot N_\mathrm{min} \\
    \Longleftrightarrow \quad \frac{Q_\mathrm{max}}{N_\mathrm{max}} &= a_\mathrm{LR} + \frac{b_\mathrm{LR}}{\frac{N_\mathrm{max}}{N_\mathrm{min}}}
    \label{eq:LRcorr}
\end{align}

The idea is to plot the $Q_\mathrm{max}/N_\mathrm{max}$ value as a function of $N_\mathrm{max}/N_\mathrm{min}$. Thus if \cref{eq:LRcorr} is valid, one could observe the tendency of the form $\displaystyle f_\mathrm{LR}(x)=a_\mathrm{LR}+\frac{b_\mathrm{LR}}{x}$.

Templates are drawn for each barrel layer and endcap rings, to avoid any dependency with the sensor thickness, two examples are shown in \cref{fig:LRtemplates}. The 2--D histograms follow the attended behaviour with a decreasing line along the x--axis. However, there is a large region bellow that curve showing that not every \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters sits on the $f_\mathrm{LR}(x)$ curve.

\subsubsection{\textit{FullLeft} and \textit{FullRight} clusters}

Finally, the last clusters are labelled as \textit{FullLeft} and \textit{FullRight} clusters if they satisfy the following criteria:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cluster with at least two strips.
    \item Saturated maximum at the beginning or at the end of the cluster.
    \item Adjacent strip of the saturated maximum must fulfilled $N_\mathrm{max} \geq 16$ ADC count for 320 $\mu$m strip sensors and $N_\mathrm{max} \geq 25$ ADC count for the 500 $\mu$m ones.
\end{itemize}

Then, similarly to what has been done for the other categories, the charge of the maximum is corrected thanks to the adjacent strip $N_\mathrm{max}$:

\begin{equation}
    Q_\mathrm{max} = a_\mathrm{FL,FR} \cdot N_\mathrm{max}
    \label{eq:FLFRcross}
\end{equation}

The value of $a_\mathrm{FL}$ and $a_\mathrm{FR}$ are extracted after taking the maximum of the $Q_\mathrm{max}/N_\mathrm{max}$. Examples of this histogram on \textit{FullLeft} and \textit{FullRight} clusters in one tracker layer are shown in \cref{fig:FLFRhists}. As such clusters represents very few of saturated clusters, the maximum of the histogram is chosen instead of performing a fit due to lack of statistics.

\subsection{Two strip cases}

Single saturated strip clusters represents $\sim$90\% of saturated clusters in muon datasets, while the clusters with two consecutive saturated strips stand for $\sim5-8$\%. Thus, a correction on those two types of clusters lead to a large coverage of saturated clusters.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

For such clusters, a shape classification is no longer possible due to the loss of information on the two saturated strips: it is no longer clear which was more saturated than the other.
A first-order correction is therefore applied, selecting only those clusters that satisfy the following criteria:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cluster with at least four strips.
    \item Saturated maximum not at the beginning, neither at the end of the cluster.
    \item The two saturated strips must be side by side.
\end{itemize}

Then a cross-talk inversion is done using the first adjacent strips next to the two saturated strips, namely $L_\mathrm{max}$ and $R_\mathrm{max}$. Similar to the \cref{fig:XtalkInversion} but with two saturated strips instead of one: 

\begin{equation}
    Q_\mathrm{corr} = a_\mathrm{2strips} \cdot \frac{L_\mathrm{max}+R_\mathrm{max}}{2}
    \label{eq:twostripscorr}
\end{equation}

with $a_\mathrm{2strips}$ being calibrated on the bTbM sample by looking after the average shape of such clusters and the proportion of charge being on each strip. The shape is shown in \cref{fig:2stripShape}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{Images/Wolfgang.jpg}
    \caption{Average shape of clusters with two consecutive saturated strips in the bTbM sample. The number represents the proportion of the charge hold by each strip in the cluster.}
    \label{fig:2stripShape}
\end{figure}

With this method, 77\% of these clusters are corrected, the rest are clusters with a saturated strip at the beginning or at the end. Similarly the single saturated strip clusters, the $Q_\mathrm{corr}/Q_\mathrm{true}$ ratio shows an intermediate validation on the bTbM sample, as depicted in \cref{fig:2stripcorr}.

\subsection{Validation of the method}

In the following, the correction based on the shape classification and then an adaptative correction depending on their shape will be labelled \textit{shape corr}. On the other hand, the basic approach using the a fix cross-talk inversion will be labelled \textit{basic corr}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

A preliminary validation is to check the correction for all single and two consecutive saturated strip clusters. For that, I used the same bTbM sample with a selection on the muon candidates. The results is shown in \cref{fig:1strip2stripcorr_btbM} for both the \textit{shape} and the \textit{basic} correction.

For the \textit{shape} correction, the ratio between the corrected charge $Q_\mathrm{corr}$ and the actual charge $Q_\mathrm{true}$ is in good agreement with the unity, while having a low percentage of under and over-corrected clusters: 92.7\% of corrected clusters are in the range $(1.01\pm0.05)\times Q_\mathrm{true}$ ($\mu\pm1\sigma$). While this result is expected because the correction has been fine tuned on the same bTbM sample, it is much better than the \textit{basic} correction already discussed in \cref{fig:QcorrOldcorrection}.

I then considered the Monte-Carlo simulation of W bosons produced with jets and the 2018 era D muon dataset, both already presented in \cref{subsubsection:satstripclusters}. For both I used the following selections:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $|\eta_\mu|<2.4$.
    \item Track coming from the PV ($|d_z|<0.1$ cm and $|d_{xy}|<0.02$ cm).
    \item \textit{HighPurity} tracks with $\chi^2/\mathrm{NDOF}<5$ and 80\% of valid hits.
    \item At least 10 \dedx\, measurements.
\end{itemize}

With so, only good muon tracks with enough \dedx\, deposits are retained. The W+jets process is not describing all the muons collected under the muon dataset, thus some key distributions are different such as the pseudo-rapidity of the muon $\eta_\mu$ or the transverse momentum $p_{T\,\mu}$. To match the MC to the data, the ratio between the 2--D plot of $p_{T\,\mu}$ as a function of $\eta_\mu$ of data is done with respect to MC. Then, the weight $w_i=\left(\eta_{\mu,i}\,,\,p_{T\mu,i}\right)_\mathrm{data/MC}$, which is the bin content of the ratio of the two 2--D histograms, is applied on the MC simulation.

Finally, the performance of the correction is evaluated on clusters with a maximum between 240 and 250 ADC count are selected. Their maximum is artificially set to 240 ADC count and then the correction method is applied. With so, the actual charge of the cluster $Q_\mathrm{true}$ is known and the corrected charge $Q_\mathrm{corr}$ is compared to the former by calculating the ratio of the two.

The correction developed above, labelled \textit{shape corr}, is compared to the basic approach using the cross-talk inversion, labelled \textit{basic corr}. Both are ploted in \cref{fig:1strip2stripcorr_dataWjets}. The first thing to notice is the discrepancy between the data and Monte-Carlo, even after the rescaling in order to match as close as possible the $p_{T\mu}$ and $\eta_\mu$ distributions. The main explanation is because of the W+jets sample does not describe all the standard muon processes, moreover for high $p_{T\mu}$ that may arise from Drell-Yan\footnote{The Drell-Yan process is a hard scattering mechanism in which a quark from one hadron and an antiquark from another annihilate to produce a virtual photon or Z boson, which then decays into a lepton-antilepton pair.} or $t\overline{t}$ processes. Another point worth noting is the width of the two ratios; as the correction was derived from a sample of muons for which the saturated charge was known, it is to be expected that the agreement is not as good as that shown in \cref{fig:1strip2stripcorr_btbM}. Beside this, as already discussed, the basic correction is biased with most of the clusters being under-corrected while the shape correction is in good agreement to the actual charge of the cluster.

\subsection{Improvements and criticism}
\label{subsection:Improvements}

The saturated cluster correction method developed here shows good results on the charge reconstruction of muon deposits in Monte-Carlo and data samples. A cross-talk inversion based algorithm has been adapted to fulfilled different cluster shapes and number of consecutive saturated strips. As the population of saturated clusters is mostly those with one single saturated strips and then two consecutive saturated strips, the other saturated clusters are not corrected under this approach. 

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The over-corrected clusters are mostly the \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} ones sitting under the $\displaystyle f_\mathrm{LR}(x)=a_\mathrm{LR}+\frac{b_\mathrm{LR}}{x}$ fit. Example of clusters sitting in this region are shown in \cref{fig:miscorrectedLR}. Such clusters have a high charge sharing between the maximum and the adjacent strips, leading to a bad correction later. Such clusters corresponds to $\sim32\%$ of the total \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters, meaning 68\% of the latter fall within a 10\% range of the $f_\mathrm{LR}(x)$ fit.

One could improve the correction by considering a lower threshold of the ratio between $N_\mathrm{max}$ and $N_\mathrm{min}$, that has been fixed to 10\%. I tried to adjust this value before performing a new fit and assess the correction: a lower one led to more over-corrected clusters and a higher one led to a worst \textit{Center} clusters correction (the $a_{\mathrm{center}}$ factor was not any more describing every \textit{Center} clusters). In other words: well-corrected saturated clusters are the one that have a small charge value compared to the saturated strips.

An other method, that I didn't pushed, would be to consider the value of the second adjacent strips into the model to make the distinction between high charge sharing, leading to higher charge on the second adjacent strips, and the lower one.

Another option that I studied was to consider at some point the barycentre of charge of a cluster after the shape classification. The best option was to take the difference between the strip index of the maximum $i_\mathrm{max}$ and the barycentre such as $Q_\mathrm{max}=f(i_\mathrm{max}-\mathrm{bar.})$. This method should have been an alternative for the \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters. 

This method led to a poor selection on the saturated cluster population, leading to only 80\% of them being corrected. On top of not being accurate with 20\% of clusters with $Q_\mathrm{corr}/Q_\mathrm{true}>1.05$ and with negative corrected charge due to $i_\mathrm{max}-\mathrm{bar.}$ difference.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Regarding the samples used for calibration, I was constrained by the statistical size of the bTbM sample (600,000 events), which affected the derivation of the layer-by-layer correction parameters.

A more in-depth study would benefit from using a W+jets sample, coupled with other processes with a wider $p_{T\mu}$ range such as $t\overline{t}$ or Drell-Yan, in order to also study saturated deposits outside the MIP point and thus propose parameters more in line with the data.
