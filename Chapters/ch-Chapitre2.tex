\section{Interaction of charged particles with matter}
\label{sec:interactionWithMatter}

\indent A charged particle traversing a material loses energy via ionisation or Coulomb scattering. The Bethe-Bloch formula describes the energy loss due to ionisation in a material by a particle with a mass $M$ much greater than an electron of mass $m_e$ \cite{PDG}:

\begin{equation} 
    \left\langle - \frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}x} \right\rangle = Kz^2 \frac{Z}{A} \frac{1}{\beta^2} \left[ \frac{1}{2} \ln \left( \frac{2m_ec^2\beta^2\gamma^2W_{\mathrm{max}}}{I^2} \right) - \beta^2 - \frac{\delta(\beta\gamma)}{2} \right] \, ,
    \label{eq:BetheBloch_formula}
\end{equation}
where the term $\beta\gamma= p/(Mc)$ represents the kinematics of the particle of momentum $p$. The term $\beta$ is equal to $v/c$ (where $v$ is the velocity of the particle), $A$ and $Z$ correspond to the mass number and atomic number of the material traversed respectively, $z$ is the algebraic charge of the particle and $K \approx 0.307075 \,\, \textrm{MeV\,mol}^{-1}\,\textrm{cm}^2$. $I$ is the average excitation energy of the material and $W_{\mathrm{max}}$, the maximum energy transferred in a single collision, such that:

\begin{equation}
    W_{\max} = \frac{2m_e c^2 \beta^2 \gamma^2}{1 + 2\gamma m_e/M + (m_e/M)^2} \, ,
    \label{eq:Wmax}
\end{equation}
and $\delta(\beta\gamma)$ is the density correction for the ultra-relativistic regime:

\begin{equation}
    \delta(\beta\gamma) = \begin{cases} 2\ln 10\cdot x - \overline{C} & \text{if } x \geq x_1; \\ 2\ln 10\cdot x - \overline{C} + a(x_1 - x)^k & \text{if } x_0 \leq x < x_1; \\ 0 & \text{if } x < x_0 \textrm{ (non conductors)}; \\ \delta_0 10^{2(x - x_0)} & \text{if } x < x_0 \text{ (conductors),} \end{cases}
    \label{eq:deltabetagamma}
\end{equation}
which is an empirical expression depending on the $\beta\gamma$ regime with $x=\log_{10}(\beta\gamma)$; $x_0$, $x_1$, $\overline{C}$, $a$, $k$, and $\delta_0$ are constants related to the material, one can find numerical values of them in Ref. \cite{DeltaBetaGamma}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The average stopping power $\langle \dedx \rangle $ is usually depicted in MeV $\mathrm{g}^{-1}\,\mathrm{cm}^2$. By multiplying this value by the material density, the average ionisation loss is therefore expressed in [MeV/cm]. The mean energy deposit per unit of length value depends on $p/M$, so that for slow moving particle and/or heavy ones, \dedx\, decreases as $-\ln(p/M)$ before reaching a minimum at around $1-5$ MeV/cm for $\beta\gamma\sim3-4$, and gradually increases at large $p/M$ due to the relativistic-regime term induced by $\delta(\beta\gamma)$. \cref{fig:BetheBloch} shows the energy deposition per unit distance travelled for different particles passing through silicon according to \cref{eq:BetheBloch_formula}.

    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Images/Ch2/bethebloch_multiparticle_Si.pdf}
    \caption{Average ionisation loss of muons, pions and protons in the silicon derived from \cref{eq:BetheBloch_formula}. Numerical values of the parameters were taken from \cite{DeltaBetaGamma}.}
    \label{fig:BetheBloch}
\end{figure}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Most of the particles produced by proton-proton collisions at the CMS detector are low-energy particles, which release some of their energy at the minimum of the curve. The silicon tracker has been optimised to detect such particles, labelled as \textit{Minimum Ionising Particles} (MIPs).

Note that for high relativistic particles at $\beta\gamma\sim1000$, the average deposit is 2.5 times higher than the MIP. Thus, by measuring the \dedx\,information and the track momentum, one could identify the $\beta\gamma$ regime of the particle. This information will later be used to distinguish energy deposits induced by Standard Model (SM) processes from those caused by Beyond Standard Model (BSM) HSCPs.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

One additional point is that the dominant energy-loss mechanism depends on the particle type. For muons with energies below about 100~GeV, ionisation is the primary energy-loss process. Consequently, muons can travel considerable distances even in dense materials such as iron. For instance, a 10~GeV muon loses approximately 13~MeV/cm in iron, corresponding to a range of several metres. 

At higher energies, radiative processes such as Bremsstrahlung, pair production, and photonuclear interactions become non-negligible and progressively dominate the energy loss. 

As a result, muons produced in particle accelerators are highly penetrating and typically traverse the entire detector, leaving an imprint of ionisation. This distinctive behaviour can be exploited for muon identification, since other charged particles generally undergo additional interactions besides ionisation energy loss~\cite{Thomson_2013}.


\vspace{0.5 cm}

While the Bethe–Bloch formula provides an accurate description of the average energy loss over a given path length, it becomes inadequate when considering thin absorbers such as the strip or pixel silicon sensors of the CMS tracker, where the energy deposit is measured locally, layer by layer.

In this regime, the particle experiences relatively few collisions, so the deposited energy cannot be precisely defined. It is better described by a probability distribution. Rare but large energy transfers dominate this distribution, mainly due to $\delta$-rays\footnote{A $\delta$-ray is a secondary electron ejected from an atom by an ionising particle with sufficient kinetic energy to produce further ionisation along its own track, distinct from that of the primary particle.}, which create an asymmetric tail toward large energy losses.

In 1944, Lev Landau addressed this problem analytically. Starting from the differential cross section for individual collisions, he showed that in the limit of a sufficiently thin absorber the energy-loss fluctuations follow a universal functional form, now known as the \textit{Landau distribution} \cite{Landau}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

As the tail goes to infinity, the Landau distribution does not have a finite mean and standard deviation. Instead, it is commonly described by the \textit{Most Probable Value} (MPV), defined as the position of the maximum of the Landau distribution. The MPV depends on the thickness of the material crossed by the particle and increases approximately linearly with thickness, as a thicker layer results in more ionising collisions. 

The Landau \textit{Probability Density Function} (PDF) is a function of the particle mass and momentum, the crossed material and the thickness of the latter. \cref{fig:MPV_Landau} shows this PDF for a pion with a momentum of 10 GeV/$c$ passing through two silicon sensors of different thicknesses.

Note that particles cannot actually lose more energy than their total budget; thus, the infinite tail of the Landau distribution is purely conceptual.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{Images/Ch2/landau_total_keV.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{Images/Ch2/MPV.pdf}
    \caption{Landau PDF for a 10 GeV pion passing through a 320~$\mu$m and 500~$\mu$m silicon sensor (Left). Most Probable Value of the Landau PDF as a function of the relativistic regime $\beta\gamma$ for two silicon sensors of different thickness (Right).}
    \label{fig:MPV_Landau}
\end{figure}

Another important point is the dependence of the MPV on $\beta\gamma$ and the material thickness. The slower the particle, the higher the MPV. At large $\beta\gamma$, however, the MPV converges asymptotically to a value that depends solely on the thickness of the material in the relativistic regime. An example of the evolution of the MPV for two different silicon sensor thicknesses is shown in \cref{fig:MPV_Landau}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The Landau distribution provides information on the total energy deposited in a finite silicon layer (here for 320 and 500 $\mu\mathrm{m}$). At the same time, the MPV curve gives the energy loss per unit length (MeV/cm). Multiplying the MPV at the MIP value ($\sim$2.6-2.7 MeV/cm on silicon) by the layer thickness naturally yields a deposit in the keV range, consistent with the MPV observed in the Landau PDF of \cref{fig:MPV_Landau}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Finally, one could also present a more general description of energy-loss fluctuations, provided by the \textit{Vavilov distribution}, which extends the Landau model to absorbers of finite thickness. In the limit of very thin materials, it reduces to the Landau distribution, while for thicker absorbers it gradually approaches a Gaussian shape. This behaviour reflects the Central Limit Theorem: as the number of independent collisions increases, the sum of many small energy transfers tends toward a normal distribution \cite{VavilovFunctionPixels}.

This function is used, for instance, to simulate charge deposits in pixel sensors. Particles at high pseudorapidity traverse a larger amount of material than those produced in the central region; thus, the Landau distribution is no longer representative of their energy distribution. Incorporating this effect into the simulation enables a more accurate tuning of the detector response, bringing it into closer agreement with the behaviour observed in data.



\section{Physics of the silicon tracker modules}
\label{sec:Physics_silicon_tracker}

A typical semiconductor detector consists of a sensitive layer and read-out electronics that process the analogue signals. The following section will cover the underlying physics governing semiconductor sensors and will present the typical read-out electronics chain used for data acquisition of silicon strip sensors.

\subsection{Semiconductor sensors}

\subsubsection{Basic concept of semiconductor physics}

The Pauli exclusion principle states that two identical fermions can not occupy the same quantum state simultaneously. The Fermi-Dirac distribution therefore describes the distribution of energy levels in fermionic systems:

\begin{equation}
    f(E)=\frac{1}{1+\exp\left( \displaystyle \frac{E-E_F}{k_BT} \right)} \, ,
    \label{eq:fermidirac}
\end{equation}
where $k_B$ denotes the Boltzmann constant, $T$ the temperature of the system, and $E_F$ the Fermi level.

The distribution gives the probability density of finding a fermion at a given energy, while the Fermi level corresponds to the energy at which this probability equals 1/2. At $T$ = 0 K, all energy states up to $E_F$ are filled, and as the temperature rises, electrons begin to populate higher-energy excited states. 

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Unlike isolated atoms, where electrons occupy discrete orbitals with well-defined binding energies, electrons in crystalline solids arrange themselves into continuous energy bands. The band structure of various materials is illustrated schematically in \cref{fig:EnergyBand}. The valence band is the highest band fully occupied at $T$~=~0~K, while the partially filled band above it is known as the conduction band. Between these two bands lies a forbidden region with no allowed energy states, referred to as the band gap. Electrons in the conduction band are those that contribute to the electrical conductivity of the material. 

Based on this band picture, materials can be classified into three broad categories: conductors, semiconductors, and insulators. Conductors already possess electrons in the conduction band at $T=0$~K, whereas semiconductors and insulators do not, making them behave similarly at absolute zero. At elevated temperatures, however, thermally excited electrons in semiconductors can acquire enough energy to bridge the band gap, rendering the material conductive. These thermally generated carriers are known as intrinsic \textit{charge carriers}, and a semiconductor free of any impurities is accordingly called an intrinsic semiconductor.

    \centering
    \input{Images/Ch2/EnergyBand.tex}
    \caption{Sketch of the energy band structure for three different materials: insulators, semiconductors and conductors. The orange (blue) regions represent the states that contain (no) electrons at $T=0$ K.}
    \label{fig:EnergyBand}
\end{figure}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Band theory is based on chemical bonds between atoms, which arise from interactions between electron shells. The valence shell, in particular, determines an element's electrical conductivity. A filled valence shell makes an element chemically inert, gaseous, and poorly conductive. A nearly full shell, as in F, Cl, and Br, forms non-conductive solids called insulators. Elements with a single valence electron, such as Li, Na, and Cu, are good conductors because the electron moves freely across the lattice. Atoms like C, Si, and Ge, which have four valence electrons, form covalent bonds by sharing electrons with neighbours. These elements, known as semiconductors, bind their electrons less tightly than insulators, enabling conduction under certain conditions.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Electrical conductivity in a crystal lattice is driven by the movement of charge carriers, namely electrons and holes. When an electron vacates its position, it leaves behind an empty site in the lattice, referred to as a hole. This hole is quickly occupied by a neighbouring electron, which in turn creates a new vacancy elsewhere. Through this successive displacement mechanism, holes effectively travel across the lattice, giving rise to hole conductivity. The velocity at which electrons and holes move varies with both the material and the applied electric field. In an intrinsic semiconductor, electrons and holes are present in equal numbers, meaning that neither carrier type is dominant.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The conduction properties of semiconductors can be tailored by deliberately introducing impurities into the crystal lattice, a process known as doping, which is schematically illustrated for silicon in \cref{fig:DopedSemiConductor}.

To enhance electron conductivity, atoms carrying an extra valence electron are incorporated into the lattice. These impurity atoms are called donors, and the resulting material is designated as n-type, or n-doped, semiconductor. In this case, Phosphorus, which possesses five valence electrons, is a typical donor atom. P-type semiconductors, on the other hand, are obtained by introducing acceptor atoms into the intrinsic semiconductor; these atoms have one fewer electron in their outer shell, making hole conductivity the dominant transport mechanism. Boron is commonly used as an acceptor in p-doped silicon.

\subsubsection{p--n junctions}

Semiconductor sensors are built around a p--n junction, which consists of a p-type and an n-type semiconductor brought into contact. The corresponding energy state diagram for doped semiconductors and a p--n junction is depicted in \cref{fig:PNEnergy}.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \input{Images/Ch2/PNEnergy.tex}
    \caption{Energy diagram of doped semiconductors and a p--n junction. $E_A$ and $E_D$ are the energy levels of acceptor and donor atoms, respectively. The orange (blue) regions represent the states that contain (no) electrons.}
    \label{fig:PNEnergy}
\end{figure}


Doping shifts the Fermi energy level due to the increased concentration of charge carriers in the semiconductor. At the interface between the p-type and n-type regions, electrons and holes begin to recombine, leaving a positively charged region on the n-doped side and a negatively charged region on the p-doped side. The resulting built-in electric field counteracts further diffusion of charge carriers, giving rise to a carrier-free region known as the \textit{depletion zone}.

This zone can be extended by attaching electrodes to both sides of the junction to establish electrical contacts, and then applying an external voltage across the p--n junction. When a positive voltage is applied to the n-side, the induced electric field draws charge carriers away from the junction. Beyond a certain voltage threshold, the field extends across the entire thickness of the sensor, at which point the sensor is said to be fully depleted. This threshold, known as the \textit{full depletion voltage}, depends on both the doping concentration and the sensor thickness.


\subsection{Silicon strip sensors}

The CMS detector uses the tracker's silicon p--n junctions for particle detection. Among the various natural semiconductors, the collaboration opted for silicon-based sensors for various reasons. It is the most common semiconductor (thus cheaper), with natural radiation tolerance, an efficient band gap, and a low electron-hole pair creation energy threshold.

A silicon strip sensor module comprises several distinct elements, each playing a specific role in the detection chain. \Cref{fig:SiliconStripSensor} provides a schematic overview of such a module, the key components of which are described in the following.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Images/Ch2/SiliconStripSensorSketch.png}
    \caption{Sketch of a silicon strip module with its different subparts \cite{SiliconLayout}.}
    \label{fig:SiliconStripSensor}
\end{figure}

\textbf{General structure}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The sensor consists of a phosphorus-doped n-type silicon bulk forming the active detection volume. Multiple $\mathrm{p^+}$ strips are implanted on the front face, creating a p--n junction that, under reverse bias, depletes the bulk and enables charge collection.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\textbf{Signal generation}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

As a MIP traverses the depleted silicon bulk, it generates approximately 70 electron-hole pairs per micrometre of sensor thickness, each requiring 3.62 eV of energy \cite{ElectronHolePairsEnergy}. This is significantly lower than in gaseous detectors (34.6\,eV for an $\mathrm{H_2}$-based detector). For a 300\,$\mu$m thick sensor, this corresponds to $\sim 2\times10^4$ electron-hole pairs per MIP.

Under the applied electric field, holes drift toward the $\mathrm{p^+}$ strips while electrons drift toward the $\mathrm{n^+}$ backplane, with all carriers being collected within $\mathcal{O}(10\,\mathrm{ns})$. However, this signal is several orders of magnitude smaller than the \textit{leakage current}, arising from the $\sim 10^8$ electron-hole pairs continuously generated by thermal excitation within the bulk. This parasitic current constitutes a significant source of noise and is further enhanced by radiation-induced lattice defects. To mitigate this effect, the sensors are cooled to suppress thermal generation and improve the signal-to-noise ratio for MIP charge collection \cite{ElectronHoleInSilicon}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\textbf{Front side readout}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Each strip is read out via an alternating-current (AC)-coupled pad, which blocks the DC leakage current while transmitting the signal to the read-out chip through a bond wire. A polyresistor connects the direct-current (DC) pad to the bias ring, enabling strip biasing while preserving AC isolation.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\textbf{Edge structures}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Two surrounding $\mathrm{p^+}$ rings protect the active volume. The bias ring is connected to ground and biases the strips, while the guard ring grades the electric field at the sensor periphery, preventing the depletion region from reaching the physical edges and avoiding breakdown. This part of the device sits on a $\mathrm{SiO_2/Si_3N_4}$ insulating layer deposited on the front surface to protect and electrically insulate the surface structures.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\textbf{Backside}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

A heavily doped $\mathrm{n^+}$ implant on the rear face ensures an ohmic contact with the aluminium backplane electrode, through which the positive high voltage is applied to fully deplete the sensor bulk.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\textbf{Geometric parameters}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The centre-to-centre distance between adjacent strips defines the strip pitch $P$, while their implant width is denoted $W$; these are the key geometric parameters of the sensor summarised in \cref{appendix:annexeA}. Since the silicon strip tracker spans a large tracking volume, both parameters vary with the module's position within the tracker to keep the $W/P$ ratio close to 0.25. This value has been chosen to minimise the total strip capacitance while maintaining a sufficiently large electric field peak at the $\mathrm{p^+}$ strip edges.



\subsection{Read-out pipeline}

Once a charged particle has generated electron-hole pairs in the bulk material, the drift of these electrons induces a current that is then read out. The resulting current is transmitted via a pitch adapter\footnote{The pitch adapter bridges the geometrical mismatch between the strip pitch and the read-out electronics.}, to the Front-End Hybrid (FEH), where the APV25 (a 25 $\mu$m thick CMOS Analogue Pipeline Voltage) front-end chips are located \cite{ReadoutStrip}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Each APV25 chip reads out 128 strips. Given that strip modules comprise either 512 or 768 strips, between four and six APV25 chips are required per sensor. Within each chip, the charge signal first passes through a preamplifier, which converts it into a voltage step. A unity-gain inverter is then applied to normalise the signal polarity, ensuring a positive voltage step regardless of whether the signal originates from electrons or holes. The signal subsequently enters a CR-RC shaper\footnote{A CR-RC shaper is an analogue filtering stage that differentiates the input signal to remove the DC baseline (CR high-pass filter) and then integrates it to limit the bandwidth and optimise the signal-to-noise ratio (RC low-pass filter).}, which produces a 40~MHz voltage pulse. 

The APV25 operates in two modes, each delivering distinct advantages. In \textit{peak mode}, the analogue signal is sampled once per bunch crossing at the peak of the CR–RC-shaped pulse, maximising the signal-to-noise ratio. However, the pulse spans about 200~ns --- roughly eight LHC bunch crossings --- so a single hit affects multiple crossings, complicating event assignment at high luminosity. \textit{Deconvolution mode} handles this by combining three samples spaced by 25~ns using a weighted sum that inverts the CR–RC response, confining the pulse to a single 25~ns interval and enabling each hit to be assigned to a single bunch crossing. Thus, the deconvolution mode is favoured during physics data-taking for its precise hit association.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The analogue outputs of pairs of APV25 chips are time-multiplexed and transmitted via analogue optical links to the Front-End Drivers (FEDs), located outside the CMS detector in the experimental cavern. At the FED level, the signal from each strip is digitised using a 10-bit analogue-to-digital converter (ADC), yielding a \textit{raw-digit}. The FEDs then operate in zero-suppressed mode, performing two successive noise subtractions on a chip-by-chip basis. The first subtraction removes the \textit{pedestal}, defined as the average baseline activity of a given strip in the absence of any particle signal. The second subtraction addresses the Common Mode Noise (CMN), an electronic baseline fluctuation common to all 128 strips of a given APV25 chip, estimated as the median over those strips for each event. Both the pedestal and noise levels are evaluated during dedicated calibration runs, performed several times per year outside of standard physics data-taking periods.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Following these subtractions, strips that return negative ADC values are set to zero, since the FEDs handle only positive values. A zero-suppression threshold is then applied: individual strips with a signal-to-noise ratio $S/N$ above 5 are included, as are groups of adjacent strips provided each neighbouring strip exceeds $S/N > 2$. The 10--bit~ADC range is subsequently truncated to 8 bits according to the following scheme:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Strips with a digitised signal below 254~ADC counts are left unchanged;
    \item Those with a digitised signal between 254 and 1023~ADC counts are set to 254;
    \item Strips above 1024~ADC counts are assigned a value of 255.
\end{itemize}
Strips with zero ADC counts or low $S/N$ value are suppressed entirely, thereby reducing the overall data rate.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

In addition to signal digitisation, the Front-End Controllers (FEC) distribute global timing, trigger, and command information from the Timing, Trigger and Control (TTC) system to the front-end electronics via the Communication and Control Unit (CCU). The FEC also handles the configuration of the front-end electronics and monitors key operational parameters, including sensor temperature, leakage current, and bias voltage, through the Detector Control Unit (DCU). The complete pipeline is depicted in \cref{fig:APV25_scheme}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Finally, one can convert the charge collected in the ADC to MeV by knowing the electron-hole energy-pair creation of the semiconductor and the effective gain of the read-out chain (APV25 + FED), the latter being determined experimentally during calibration runs:
\begin{equation}
    Q[\mathrm{ADC \, count}]= 3.62 \cdot10^{-6} \times 265 \times Q[\mathrm{MeV}] \, .
    \label{eq:ADCfactorToMeV}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Properties of charge deposits}

As described in \cref{subsubsection:Hitreconstruction}, collected charges in adjacent strips are grouped into clusters. The width of the cluster, i.e., the number of strips, is affected by two physical processes: the \textit{charge sharing} and the \textit{cross-talk}.

\subsubsection{Charge sharing}

As depicted in \cref{fig:SiliconStripSensor}, a MIP crossing the n-bulk creates electron-hole pairs that can be captured by several strips depending on the incident angle of the particle. According to the Lorentz force, the movement of the charge carriers is also affected by the internal electric field between the electrodes and the external magnetic field. The latter causes a drift of about $5^\text{o}$ in the barrel sensors, resulting in a position shift of the order of 10 to 20 $\mu$m \cite{ChargeCharingDrift}.

One could also mention charge diffusion induced by collisions between charge carriers, thereby causing a small drift along the path.

These two phenomena, combined, have a small effect relative to the pitch size (the smaller value being 80 $\mu$m) but are accounted for in the simulation of charge spreading.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

To illustrate that, \cref{fig:clusterCenteredShifted} shows two \textit{raw-digit} clusters of charges collected in a silicon strip sensor. These clusters originate from a Monte Carlo (MC) simulation of back-to-back muons generated without any electronic saturation or pile-up contamination.

The charge collected on each strip is expressed in ADC counts, while the strip index identifies its position within the cluster. The left cluster originates from a muon that crosses a TIB module at an angle of 0.16 rad ($\sim9^\text{o}$) to the module's normal. The effect of charge sharing is not very important, but the effect of cross-talk is clearly visible (developed below). On the other hand, the right cluster corresponds to a muon with an incident angle of 1.23 rad ($\sim 71^\text{o}$). This tilted approach leads to higher charge collection in strips 436 and 437.

\subsubsection{Cross-talk}
\label{subsubsection:crosstalk}

In the CMS silicon strip sensor, each strip is capacitively coupled to both the backplane and its immediate neighbours via inter-strip capacitance \cite{CapacitiveCoupling}. It is inherent to the sensor geometry itself: the inter-strip capacitance arises from the proximity of adjacent $\mathrm{p^+}$ implants, while the backplane capacitance couples each strip to the aluminium backplane through the bulk, as illustrated in \cref{fig:SiliconStripSensor}.

This capacitive network has a direct consequence on the charge measurement: when a particle deposits charge on a given strip, a fraction of that charge is redistributed to the adjacent strips through capacitive coupling. This effect is referred to as \textit{cross-talk}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The cross-talk is parametrised as follows. For a cluster of total charge $Q$, the strip with the largest charge fraction receives $x_0 \times Q$, its two immediate neighbours each receive $x_1 \times Q$, and the next-to-nearest neighbours receive $x_2 \times Q$. For a common 5-strip width cluster, this leads to the following equation: $\displaystyle x_0 + 2\cdot x_1 + 2 \cdot x_2 = 1$. If a MIP particle crosses the sensor with an incident angle close to zero with respect to the normal, this leads to pyramid-shaped clusters, as the left cluster of \cref{fig:clusterCenteredShifted}.

One should note that these parameters are not constant; they depend on the APV25 chip's read-out mode, as the degree of charge sharing is sensitive to the signal sampling time. The values of $x_0$, $x_1$ and $x_2$ have been extracted from data in deconvolution mode, using muon tracks traversing the full tracker with trajectories close to normal incidence to the sensor plane, and were evaluated both in 2010 and 2018 to monitor potential variations over time \cite{Xtalk2018CMS}. Values of those three parameters measured in the tracker barrel for different years are presented in \cref{fig:Xtalk}. Due to ageing, $x_1$ and $x_2$ are smaller than at the beginning of the last decade; thus, clusters tend to shrink.

\subsection{Radiation effects}

The performance of silicon tracking detectors inevitably degrades under the intense radiation environment of the LHC. Energy loss of traversing particles occurs through two distinct mechanisms: Ionising Energy Loss (IEL), arising from collisions with atomic electrons, and Non-Ionising Energy Loss (NIEL), in which nuclear interactions displace lattice atoms. Both processes induce radiation damage, which can be classified as bulk or surface damage.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Bulk damage, primarily driven by NIEL, arises when recoiling atoms displace neighbouring lattice sites, forming defect clusters. These introduce new energy levels within the silicon bandgap, leading to increased leakage current, charge carrier trapping, and local electric field distortions, all of which degrade charge collection efficiency and ultimately the sensor resolution.

Surface damage is instead dominated by ionising radiation, which generates positive charges trapped at the Si–Si$\mathrm{O}_2$ interface. This accumulated charge can induce inter-strip short circuits, increasing both the bulk current and cross-talk between read-out channels, thereby degrading the detector's noise performance.

Beyond permanent damage, ionising particles can also flip the state of memory cells in the front-end electronics, an effect known as Single Event Upset (SEU). Unlike bulk or surface damage, SEUs are non-permanent and can be recovered by reconfiguring the affected circuits.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

To cope with the cumulative effects of permanent radiation damage, detectors can be recalibrated and operated at higher bias voltages and lower temperatures. However, once the signal-to-noise ratio becomes too degraded, front-end modules must be replaced. In CMS, for instance, the innermost layer of the barrel pixel detector (BPIX layer~1), placed at a radius of only 29~mm from the beam, is exposed to a hadron fluence of up to ${\sim}3.6\times10^{15}~\mathrm{n}_{\mathrm{eq}}/\mathrm{cm^{2}}$ for an integrated luminosity of 500~fb$^{-1}$, leading to a progressive decrease of the charge-collection efficiency and a corresponding rise of the pixel threshold over the course of operation~\cite{CMSPhase1Pixel2021}. A typical operational lifetime for the inner layers of a silicon tracker at the LHC is of the order of 5 to 10 years.



\section{Study of saturated strip clusters}
\label{sec:Study_saturated_strip_clusters}

\subsection{The harmonic ionisation mean \texorpdfstring{$I_h$}{Ih}}

One way to assess the average ionisation of a track along several silicon strip modules is to use a harmonic mean function. Therefore, $I_h$ is defined to be the harmonic mean of the ionisation of a charged particle track that has deposited $N$ charge clusters $c_j = (\dedx)_j$ along several silicon strip layers:

\begin{equation}
    I_h = \left( \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N c_j^{-2} \right)^{-\frac{1}{2}} \, .
    \label{eq:ih}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

$I_h$ is, by construction, very robust against upward fluctuations of the $c_j$, which have a non-negligible chance to happen (as they are described by a Landau PDF). On the other hand, $I_h$ is very sensitive to downward fluctuations, which are unlikely to occur randomly (if the $c_j$ are indeed following a Landau distribution), but may occur due to detector effects such as noise, baseline voltage drop, etc.

\subsection{Shape classification}
\label{subsection:shapeclassification}

Clusters with a single saturated strip can be classified into different categories depending on their shape. This classification has been derived with the MC--muon sample, and grouped clusters into five distinct labels:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Center} clusters: Originating from particles with a near-normal direction to the sensor, these clusters are fully explained by the cross-talk. In the MC--muon sample, they represent 69~\% of the saturated cluster fraction.
    \item \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters (L and R): Resulting from particles with an incident angle with respect to the normal of the sensors, the cluster barycentre is shifted toward the strips with more deposit. Each accounts for 14~\% of the total population.
    \item \textit{FullLeft} and \textit{FullRight} clusters (FL and FR): These are abnormal clusters; accounting for 1.5~\% each of the total fraction. Most of them ($\sim$80~\%) result from a dead strip adjacent to the maximum. In the remaining 20~\%, their maximum is located at the beginning or the end of the APV25 read-out.
\end{itemize}

The dependency of cluster shape as a function of the tracker layer is depicted in \cref{fig:shapeFraction}. As expected, the population of \textit{Center} clusters represents the highest fraction, especially in the central region where the charged particles crossed the material without a tilted incident angle. Note that in a given tracker layer, the sum of each population does not always match the saturated cluster population, as the shape classification misses clusters with more than one saturated strip.

\subsection{Basic saturation correction}

Among saturated clusters, the \textit{Center} topology is by far the most frequent, and its three-strip shape is entirely driven by the inter-strip cross-talk. This motivates a simple first-order correction based on inverting the cross-talk: if the fraction of charge transferred from the central strip to each of its nearest neighbours is known, the original charge on the saturated strip can, in principle, be recovered from the side-strip amplitudes. This fraction corresponds to the $x_1$ parameter introduced in \cref{subsubsection:crosstalk}, whose measured values are reported in \cref{fig:Xtalk}.

Thus, the corrected charge $Q_\mathrm{basic\,corr}$ can be computed using the charge collected on the left ($L_\mathrm{max}$) and right ($R_\mathrm{max}$) strips adjacent to the saturated maximum (Max):

\begin{equation}
    Q_\mathrm{basic\,corr} = \frac{1}{0.1} \cdot \frac{L_\mathrm{max}+R_\mathrm{max}}{2} \, .
    \label{eq:xtalkinversion}
\end{equation}

A sketch of the correction process is shown in \cref{fig:XtalkInversion} where it is assumed that $L_\mathrm{max}=R_\mathrm{max}=0.1\times$Max.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \input{Images/Ch2/XtalkInversion.tex}
    \caption{Sketch of the cross-talk inversion on a saturated \textit{Center} cluster, assuming that the single saturated strip counts for 72~\% of the total charge $Q$ of the cluster.}
    \label{fig:XtalkInversion}
\end{figure}

This saturation correction method was implemented within the HSCP analysis framework utilised for the EXO-18-002 publication~\cite{HSCP_Run2}, and applied to saturated clusters fulfilling the following criteria:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The cluster must contain between 2 and 8 strips.
    \item Only one saturated strip is allowed.
    \item The deposits in the strips adjacent to the saturated maximum must be symmetrical ($|L_{\mathrm{max}}-R_{\mathrm{max}}|<40$~ADC counts) and above a certain threshold ($L_{\mathrm{max}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{max}}>25$~ADC counts). 
\end{itemize}

One can assess the quality of reconstruction by examining the $Q_\mathrm{basic\,corr}/Q_\mathrm{true}$ ratio and the $Q_\mathrm{basic\,corr}-Q_\mathrm{true}$ difference. Both results are depicted in \cref{fig:QcorrOldcorrection}. 

This basic correction introduces a bias of $\sim13~\%$ in the reconstructed charge (the ratio is not compatible with unity), as a large population of corrected clusters shows an under-correction of about -50~ADC counts. Moreover, there is a large spread in the corrected charge (a variation of $\mu/\sigma\sim13~\%$ in the ratio plot). This comes from the constant value of the cross-talk inversion coefficient at $1/0.1$. An adaptive value that depends on the tracker region would be more suitable, as in the current simulation (see \cref{fig:Xtalk}).

On top of that, only two-thirds of the saturated cluster population are corrected, with a population composed of 85~\% of \textit{Center}, 30~\% of L--R, and 15~\% of FL--FR clusters. This is because none of the clusters containing two consecutive saturated strips is corrected and the restrictive selection on the saturated maximum neighbourhood ($|L_{\mathrm{max}}-R_{\mathrm{max}}|<40$~ADC count, $L_{\mathrm{max}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{max}}>25$~ADC count). A change in these two thresholds leads to either a larger selection with poor correction or the opposite.

\section{Saturation correction based on cluster shape}
\label{sec:Saturation_correction_cluster_shape}
This section will focus on clusters that contain a single saturated strip, for which an adaptive correction is proposed. A discussion on clusters containing two consecutive saturated strips will then be dedicated.

\subsection{Correction of the \textit{Center} clusters}

As shown in \cref{subsection:shapeclassification}, clusters produced by muons hitting perpendicularly on the module surface are the most common; they are labelled as \textit{Center} clusters. To be included in this category, a saturated cluster is selected if it contains at least three strips, with the saturated strip lying strictly inside (so that both adjacent strips $L_{\mathrm{max}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{max}}$ carry charge) and satisfies $0.9 \leq L_{\mathrm{max}}/R_{\mathrm{max}} \leq 1.1$ to ensure a symmetric, pyramid-like shape.

As the shape of \textit{Center} clusters is fully explained by the cross-talk effect, considering a cross-talk inversion is a good solution to correct the saturation. The value of the saturated strip $S_\mathrm{max}$ is corrected using:

\begin{equation}
    S_\mathrm{max} = a_{\mathrm{center}} \cdot \frac{L_{\mathrm{max}} + R_{\mathrm{max}}}{2} \, ,
    \label{eq:CenterCorr}
\end{equation}
with $a_{\mathrm{center}}$ taken to be the mean of the Gaussian fit performed on the $S_\mathrm{max}/[(L_{\mathrm{max}} + R_{\mathrm{max}})/2]$ histogram templates. These templates are derived from the MC--muon sample, since the actual maximum value is known. They are generated for each layer of the TIB and TOB and for each ring of the TID and TEC, since wheels can have different sensor geometries rather than rings. Examples are shown in \cref{fig:centerhist}.

\subsection{Correction of the \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters}

\textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters obey the same geometric requirements as \textit{Center} ones (at least three strips, saturated maximum strictly inside the cluster) but with the symmetry criterion replaced by $L_{\mathrm{max}} \geq 1.1\,R_{\mathrm{max}}$ of $R_{\mathrm{max}} \geq 1.1\,L_{\mathrm{max}}$, respectively.

Unlike \textit{Center} clusters, one could not take the average of side-strip charges to perform the cross-talk inversion, as the charge is distributed unevenly on either side of the saturated maximum. The fraction of the higher-charge adjacent strip relative ($N_\mathrm{max}$) to the saturated maximum (Max), shown in \cref{fig:adjacentStripsLR}, exhibits a broad distribution in every tracker layer. This prevents the adoption of a single constant inversion factor, which would systematically under-correct \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Images/Ch2/AdjacentStripCharge_CLR_bis.pdf}
    \caption{Ratio of the charge collected by the highest-charge neighbouring strip to the charge of the maximum (Max), for the \textit{Center} (with $N_\mathrm{max}=(L_\mathrm{max}+R_\mathrm{max})/2$), \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters.}
    \label{fig:adjacentStripsLR}
\end{figure}

Thus, a generalised cross-talk inversion is developed using two correcting coefficients, one associated to the adjacent strip with the highest charge, labelled $N_\mathrm{max}$, and another one to the adjacent strip, labelled $N_\mathrm{min}$, to correct the saturated maximum $S_\mathrm{max}$:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    Q_\mathrm{max} &= a_\mathrm{LR} \cdot N_\mathrm{max} + b_\mathrm{LR} \cdot N_\mathrm{min} \\
    \Longleftrightarrow \quad \frac{Q_\mathrm{max}}{N_\mathrm{max}} &= a_\mathrm{LR} + \frac{b_\mathrm{LR}}{\frac{N_\mathrm{max}}{N_\mathrm{min}}} \, .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:LRcorr}
\end{equation}

The coefficients are extracted from the fit of the 2D distribution of $S_\mathrm{max}/N_\mathrm{max}$ as a function of $N_\mathrm{max}/N_\mathrm{min}$.

Templates are generated for each barrel layer and endcap ring to avoid sensor-thickness and $\ eta$-dependent effects; two examples are shown in \cref{fig:LRtemplates}. The 2D histograms match the expected behaviour as a function of $N_\mathrm{max}/N_\mathrm{min}$. However, a significant fraction of the events ($10-15\,\%$) shows lower $S_\mathrm{max}/N_\mathrm{max}$ values than expected by~\cref{eq:LRcorr}.

The fit is performed in two steps. A preliminary fit of the 2D distribution is first used to reject outlier \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters that populate the lower $S_\mathrm{max}/N_\mathrm{max}$ region; the surviving clusters are then fitted with the functional form
\begin{equation}
    f_\mathrm{LR}(x) = a_\mathrm{LR} + \frac{b_\mathrm{LR}}{x},
    \label{eq:fLR}
\end{equation}
from which the parameters $a_\mathrm{LR}$ and $b_\mathrm{LR}$ are extracted.

\subsection{Correction of the \textit{FullLeft} and \textit{FullRight} clusters}

A cluster is finally labelled as \textit{FullLeft} or \textit{FullRight} if it contains at least two strips, has its saturated maximum at the beginning or at the end of the cluster, and its single adjacent strip carries $N_\mathrm{max} \geq 16$~ADC counts for $320~\mu$m sensors or $N_\mathrm{max} \geq 25$~ADC counts for $500~\mu$m ones. These two values correspond to the same signal density, $16/320 = 25/500 = 0.05~\mathrm{ADC}/\mu\mathrm{m}$, mirroring the linear dependence of the MIP charge deposit on the sensor thickness. They are also set well above the cluster reconstruction zero-suppression threshold, so that the adjacent strip is guaranteed to carry a real cross-talk signal usable for the correction, rather than a noise fluctuation.

Then, similarly to what has been done for the other categories, the charge of the maximum is corrected thanks to the adjacent strip $N_\mathrm{max}$:

\begin{equation}
    S_\mathrm{max} = a_\mathrm{FL-FR} \cdot N_\mathrm{max} \, ,
    \label{eq:FLFRcross}
\end{equation}
where $a_\mathrm{FL}$ and $a_\mathrm{FR}$ are extracted after taking the maximum of the $S_\mathrm{max}/N_\mathrm{max}$ histogram templates; for which two of them are shown in \cref{fig:FLFRhists}. As \textit{FullLeft} and \textit{FullRight} clusters represent only a very small fraction of the saturated clusters, the maximum of the histogram is chosen instead of performing a fit due to insufficient statistics. Values of $a_\mathrm{FL}$ and $a_\mathrm{FR}$ are grouped in \cref{tab:FLRValue}.

\subsection{Correction when two strips saturate}
\label{subsection:twostrips}

Single saturated strip clusters represent about 86~\% of saturated clusters in Data--muon, while the clusters with two consecutive saturated strips stand for 8~\% (see~\cref{tab:WjetHSCPsat}). Thus, a correction on those two types of clusters leads to an extensive coverage of saturated clusters.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

For these clusters, shape classification becomes infeasible. The loss of information from the two saturated strips prevents determining which strip exhibited greater saturation. A first-order correction is therefore applied to clusters consisting of at least four strips, whose two saturated strips are adjacent and lie strictly inside the cluster (i.e. neither at its beginning nor at its end).

The cross-talk inversion is performed using the first adjacent strips adjacent to the two saturated strips, namely $L_\mathrm{max}$ and $R_\mathrm{max}$. Similar to the \cref{fig:XtalkInversion} but with two saturated strips instead of one: 

\begin{equation}
    Q_\mathrm{corr} = a_\mathrm{2strips} \cdot \frac{L_\mathrm{max}+R_\mathrm{max}}{2} \, ,
    \label{eq:twostripscorr}
\end{equation}
with $a_\mathrm{2strips}=1/0.0613$ being calibrated on the MC--muon sample. As this sample does not provide enough clusters with two consecutive saturated strips, the average shape of such clusters is taken, with $a_\mathrm{2strips}$ denoting the proportion of charge on each strip.

The method's performance will be evaluated directly on data for similar statistical reasons.

\subsection{Validation of the method}

A first validation involves checking the correction for all clusters with a single saturated strip and for two consecutive saturated strips, using the same MC--muon sample. The results of the shape corrections are shown in \cref{fig:1strip2stripcorr_MC--muon}.

For the shape correction, the ratio of the corrected charge $Q_\mathrm{shape\, corr}$ to the true charge $Q_\mathrm{true}$ is in good agreement with unity, while having a low percentage of under- and over-corrected clusters: 92.7~\% of corrected clusters are within $1\,\sigma$. While this result is expected because the correction has been fine-tuned on the same MC--muon sample, it is much better than the \textit{basic} correction already discussed in \cref{fig:QcorrOldcorrection}.

A second validation is performed using both the MC--W+jets and Data--muon samples. For this validation, only deposits from muon tracks passing the following selections are used:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Events with at least one muon with $p_T>24~$GeV/$c$;
    \item $I_{\textrm{PF}}^\mu<0.02$ and $I_{\textrm{rel}}^\mu<15~$GeV/$c$;
    \item $|\eta|<2.4$;
    \item Track coming from the primary vertex ($|d_z|<0.1$ cm and $|d_{xy}|<0.02$ cm);
    \item \textit{HighPurity} tracks with $\chi^2/\mathrm{NDOF}<5$ and 80~\% of valid hits in the tracker;
    \item At least 10 \dedx\,measurements.
\end{itemize}

The W+jets process does not describe all the events collected in the muon dataset; thus, some key distributions differ, including the muon pseudorapidity $\eta$ and transverse momentum $p_T$. The muon track hits in MC are reweighted as a function of $\eta$ and $p_T$.

The performance of the correction algorithm is evaluated on clusters with a maximum ADC count of 240-250. The maximum strip is artificially set to 240~ADC counts, and then the correction method is applied. With this, the true charge of the cluster, $Q_\mathrm{true}$, is known, and the corrected charge, $Q_\mathrm{corr}$, can be directly compared to it. The results of both correction methods are presented in \cref{fig:1strip2stripcorr_dataWjets}.

First and foremost, a discrepancy between the data and MC is observed despite the $\eta$ and $p_T$ rescaling. Even if the simulation is highly detailed, differences remain, as the MC samples don't reproduce the data exactly. Another point worth noting is the width of the two $Q_\mathrm{corr}/Q_\mathrm{true}$ ratios. Regarding the basic correction, this comes from the constant factor of 1/0.1 for the cross-talk correction, leading to poor selection and large distribution of the corrected charge with respect to the true ones, as already been observed on the MC--muon sample in \cref{fig:QcorrOldcorrection}. About the shape correction, however, the corrected factors ($a_\mathrm{center}$, $a_\mathrm{L}$, $a_\mathrm{R}$, $a_\mathrm{FL}$, $a_\mathrm{FR}$ and $a_\mathrm{2strips}$) were fine-tuned on the MC--muon sample, thus, might not be relevant of all the variety of \dedx\,clusters observed in the data, leading to a broader distribution of the corrected charge compared to the one shown in \cref{fig:1strip2stripcorr_MC--muon}. Besides this, as already discussed, the basic correction is biased, with most clusters under-corrected in both MC and data, while the shape correction provides a better recovery of the cluster's true charge.

\cref{tab:corrResults} shows the improvement of the method over a basic cross-talk inversion with a constant factor, both in data and MC. The proposed correction is not further biased (with the mean closer to unity), corrects a larger population of saturated clusters, and yields a higher population of well-reconstructed saturated charge.

\subsection{Improvements and criticism}
\label{subsection:Improvements}

The method developed here to correct for cluster saturation yields good results for charge reconstruction of muon deposits in both MC and data samples. An algorithm based on cross-talk inversion has been adapted to accommodate different cluster shapes and numbers of consecutive saturated strips. The population of corrected clusters includes data and SM simulations in which only one or two consecutive saturated strips are considered. Other cases of saturated clusters are not considered here.

Concerning the correction of clusters with two consecutive saturated strips presented in \cref{subsection:twostrips}, the validation was only possible using the data due to the lack of statistics in the MC samples. The $Q_\mathrm{shape\,corr}/Q_\mathrm{true}$ ratio is shown in \cref{fig:2stripcorr}. The cross-talk inversion corrects 77~\% of these clusters, with the rest being clusters with a saturated strip at the beginning or the end. The ratio is not close to unity, with a large population of clusters being under- and over-corrected. While this provides a useful first estimate, a more robust method would be desirable.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Images/Ch2/2stripsCorr_data_bis.pdf}
    \caption{Ratio of the corrected charge $Q_\mathrm{shape\,corr}$ and the true charge $Q_\mathrm{true}$ for clusters with two consecutive saturated strips using the Data--muon sample.}
    \label{fig:2stripcorr}
\end{figure}


Several ideas have been tested to improve correction results.

First, the over-corrected clusters are mostly the \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} ones sitting under the preliminary fit (see \cref{fig:LRfitcorr}). Such clusters have high charge sharing between the maximum and the adjacent strips, as shown in \cref{fig:miscorrectedLR}, leading later to poor correction. One could improve the correction for these clusters by using a lower threshold on the $N_\mathrm{max}/N_\mathrm{min}$ ratio, which is currently fixed at 10~\%. Adjusting this value before performing a new fit and assessing the correction showed that a lower threshold led to more over-corrected clusters. In contrast, a higher one led to a worse \textit{Center} clusters correction (as the $a_\mathrm{center}$ factor was no longer describing every \textit{Center} cluster).

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{Images/Ch2/LR_overcorrected_2_bis.pdf}
    \\ \vspace{-0.2 cm}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{Images/Ch2/LR_overcorrected_1_bis.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{Images/Ch2/LR_overcorrected_3_bis.pdf}
    \caption{Example of three \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters sitting in the region below the fit function used for the correction template. Such clusters have a high charge sharing.}
    \label{fig:miscorrectedLR}
\end{figure}


One could also consider the barycentre of charge in the correction, especially for the \textit{Left} and \textit{Right} clusters, where the asymmetry could be better accounted for. I tried to reconstruct the saturated maximum using the difference between the strip index of the maximum and the barycentre. This yields poorer cluster selections, with only 80~\% corrected. Moreover, this maximum index-to-barycentre-difference model drives about 20~\% of the corrected clusters to have $Q_\mathrm{bar.\,corr}/Q_\mathrm{true}>1.05$, and some are corrected with negative charge.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Another approach, which I didn't try, would be to consider the values of the second adjacent strips to the maximum in the model. Indeed, I've observed a direct correlation between the high charge sharing on these next-to-next-side and the high maximum value in the MC--muon sample.

Regarding the calibration samples, the derivation of layer-by-layer correction parameters was constrained by the limited size of the MC--muon sample (600,000 events). In addition, since muons from W+jets alone do not fully reflect the variety of energy depositions present in the data, where several processes contribute muons over a broad $p_T$ range, a more in-depth study would benefit from including complementary samples such as Drell-Yan\footnote{The Drell-Yan process is a hard scattering mechanism in which a quark from one hadron and an antiquark from another annihilate to produce a virtual photon or Z boson, which then decays into a lepton-antilepton pair.} or $t\overline{t}$. This would allow probing of saturated deposits beyond the MIP regime and yield parameters that are more representative of the data.

\section[d\textit{E}/d\textit{x} performance studies with 2024--2025 CMS Data]{d\textit{E}/d\textit{x} performance studies with 2024--2025 \\ CMS Data}
\label{sec:dedxperf}

The shape saturation-correction method has been approved by the CMS Tracking Physics Object Group. To show the improvement of the method on saturated clusters, the performance plot of the average ionisation $\langle$d$E/$d$x\rangle$ as a function of the track momentum $p$ has been performed for the early 2024 data~\cite{CMS-DP-2024-101}, and the full 2024~\cite{CMS-DP-2025-020} and 2025 data.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The procedure involves selecting all \textit{HighPurity} tracks with a $p_T$ between 0.5 and 5~GeV/$c$ originating from the primary vertex --- to observe the Bethe-Bloch rise at low $p$ --- that have at least 10 \dedx\,deposits to compute the average ionisation using the $I_h$ estimator.

The $I_h$ distribution for each $p$ slice (of 0.02~GeV/$c$ width) is fitted using a multi-Landau function to model the \dedx\,deposits of low $p$ particles --- here $\pi^\pm$, $K^\pm$, $p^\pm$ and $D^\pm$ ---, convoluted with a multi-Gaussian to mimic the thermal noise. Examples of the distributions are shown in \cref{fig:langausFit}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth,trim={0 0 1.5cm 0},clip]{Images/Ch2/LangausFit_41_bis.pdf}
    \\
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth,trim={0 0 1.5cm 0},clip]{Images/Ch2/LangausFit_30_bis.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth,trim={0 0 1.5cm 0},clip]{Images/Ch2/LangausFit_187_bis.pdf}

The maxima are fitted using a Landau function convoluted with a Gaussian one.}
    \label{fig:langausFit}

Then, by knowing the particle mass $M$ and its momentum $p$, one can derive a simpler description of the average ionisation $I_h$ as a function of $\beta\gamma=(p/Mc)$:

{\small
\begin{equation}
     I_h = p_1 \left( \frac{\sqrt{(\beta\gamma)^4+4(\beta\gamma)^2}-(\beta\gamma)^2}{2} \right)^\frac{p_2}{2}\cdot \ln\left( 1 + [ p_3\cdot\beta\gamma ]^{p_4} \right) - p_5 = f(\beta\gamma)
    \label{eq:fitAtlas}
\end{equation}
}with $p_i$ being the five parameters extracted after the fit of the proton (pion) line at low (high) $p$. The fit is then extrapolated to the other particles.

\cref{fig:dedx_vs_p_2024} shows the average ionisation as a function of $p$ for the basic (Top) and the shape (Bottom) saturation correction on the full 2024 data. The shape correction better describes the data in the low-$p$ region, where the proportion of saturated clusters is important.

\section{Impact on the search for HSCP}
\label{sec:impactdedxHSCP}

A natural way to assess the impact of the shape correction beyond tracker performance is to look at the reconstructed mass of HSCP particles. Indeed, this mass is derived from the ionisation curve, assuming that particle energy is deposited according to the Bethe-Bloch formula. HSCPs are predicted to be slow and highly ionising. Therefore, any improvement in the saturation correction should translate into a better estimate of the reconstructed mass.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The procedure mirrors that described in the 2024 and 2025 data performance study. The average ionisation as a function of $p$ is computed under three scenarios: without any saturation correction, with the basic correction, and with the shape correction. Upstream selections are applied to the HSCPs to select only well-reconstructed, isolated tracks with $p_T>50$~GeV/$c$ that fired the muon trigger. The results are shown in \cref{fig:Ih_vs_p_HSCP}. 

As saturated clusters represent $\sim45~\%$ of the clusters for HSCPs (see \cref{tab:WjetHSCPsat}), without any saturation correction, the curve is capping to lower $I_h$ values at low $p$. Similarly to the data, the shape correction provides a more accurate representation of the data in the low-$p$ region, where the proportion of saturated clusters is significant.

\textit{The vertical band at low $p$ corresponds to some tracks that don't pass the HLT muon trigger.}

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{Images/Ch2/Ih_VS_p_HSCP_NoCorr.pdf}

    \includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{Images/Ch2/Ih_VS_p_HSCP_OldCorr.pdf}

Beyond the qualitative aspect of adding a saturation correction into the HSCP framework, the mass reconstruction performance of the HSCP is also evaluated. First, the five $p_i$ parameters of the $f(\beta\gamma)$ fit function of \cref{eq:fitAtlas} are extracted on the MC--muon sample at low $p$, with the same procedure as on the data. The HSCP mass is then reconstructed using its average ionisation $I_h$ computed without saturation correction, with the basic correction, and with the shape correction. To do so, the Brent method \cite{brent2002} is applied to find the lowest zero $(\beta\gamma)_0$ of the $f(\beta\gamma) - I_h = 0$ equation. Thus, the reconstructed mass equals $p/(\beta\gamma)_0$.

The results are presented in \cref{fig:Mass_HSCP}, demonstrating the necessity of a saturation-correction method for the HSCP search. In the absence of saturation correction (Black), the reconstructed mass shows a systematic bias as $\overline{m}_{\widetilde{g}}^\mathrm{no\,corr}\approx1688 \pm 533$. Here, the closest agreement between the generated gluino mass, $m_{\widetilde{g}}^\mathrm{gen}=2000$~GeV/$c^2$, and the reconstructed mass is achieved when the shape correction is applied to the distribution: $\overline{m}_{\widetilde{g}}^\mathrm{shape\,corr}\approx2053 \pm 525$~GeV/$c^2$ compared to the basic correction results, $\overline{m}_{\widetilde{g}}^\mathrm{shape\,corr}\approx 1900 \pm 503$~GeV/$c^2$.
