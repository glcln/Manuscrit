\section{Historical background of CERN}
\label{sec:Section1}

After World War II, European physics had fallen behind.  Visionary scientists – including Pierre Auger, Niels Bohr and others – proposed creating a pan‐European laboratory to reunite researchers and share resources.  In 1949 French physicist Louis de Broglie made the first formal proposal at the Lausanne Cultural Conference.  Follow-up actions culminated in UNESCO discussions and by 1952 eleven countries had signed an agreement to form a provisional council. The name CERN (French acronym for Conseil Européen pour la Recherche Nucléaire) was then adopted.  The founding Convention was signed in 1953 and ratified in 1954 by twelve member states, formally establishing CERN.

With the organization in place, CERN quickly built its first accelerators. The Synchrocyclotron (SC) began operation in 1957 as a 600 MeV proton machine. In 1958 the SC enabled CERN’s first major discovery: the observation of a charged-pion decaying into an electron and a neutrino.  Soon after, the 28 GeV Proton Synchrotron (PS) came online in 1959, briefly making CERN home to the world’s highest-energy accelerator. (The PS would continue to serve as a beam source for decades to come.)

\vspace{0.5 cm}

During the 1960s particle detection at CERN was dominated by bubble and spark chambers, which produced vast numbers of photographic images. This method was slow and laborious.  A breakthrough came in 1968 when Georges Charpak invented the multiwire proportional chamber, a gas-filled detector with many parallel wires each connected to amplifiers.  Charpak’s chamber could count particles roughly 1,000 times faster than previous techniques, ushering in the modern electronic era of particle detectors.  For this achievement Charpak later received the 1992 Nobel Prize in Physics.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

CERN next pioneered colliding-beam accelerators. In 1971 the Intersecting Storage Rings (ISR) began operation, creating the world’s first high-energy proton–proton collisions.  This success paved the way for the Super Proton Synchrotron (SPS). Approved in the early 1970s, the SPS was a 7 km circular machine straddling the Franco–Swiss border. It first circulated beam in 1976 and achieved roughly 400 GeV collision energy (above its original 300 GeV design). Later, by injecting antiprotons, CERN converted the SPS into a proton–antiproton collider. In this mode the SPS led to the 1983 discovery of the W and Z bosons, a milestone in confirming the electroweak theory (for which Carlo Rubbia and Simon van der Meer won the Nobel Prize) \cite{WandZ}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Building on this progress, CERN inaugurated the Large Electron–Positron collider (LEP) in 1989. LEP was a 27 km tunnel (the largest circular collider then built) designed for high-precision studies of the electroweak interaction. In its first phase it ran at about 100 GeV and produced on the order of 17 million Z bosons. LEP’s experiments performed very precise measurements; notably they confirmed that there are exactly three light neutrino species (three generations of matter particles) \cite{LEPEW2006}. After roughly eleven years of operation, LEP was shut down in 2000 to make way for the next-generation collider using the same tunnel: the Large Hadron Collider.


\section{The Large Hadron Collider}

The Large Hadron Collider (LHC) \cite{EvansBryant2008}, located at CERN on the Franco-Swiss border, is the world’s largest and most powerful particle accelerator. Inaugurated in 2009, it represents the culmination of decades of progressive increases in accelerator energy at CERN. Its primary objective was the discovery of the Higgs boson — the last missing element of the Standard Model (see section XXXX) — a goal achieved in 2012 by the ATLAS \cite{ATLAS_Higgs} and CMS \cite{CMS_Higgs} collaborations. Beyond this milestone, the LHC was conceived as a versatile tool to probe particle interactions at the TeV scale, with the capability to deliver proton–proton collisions at a center-of-mass energy up to $\sqrt{s}=14 TeV$. Although this nominal value has not yet been reached, the machine currently operates at a maximum energy of 13.6 TeV. At such energies, the LHC provides a unique opportunity to test the limits of the Standard Model: both by conducting high-precision measurements that may reveal subtle deviations from theoretical predictions, and by carrying out direct searches for phenomena Beyond the Standard Model (BSM), some of which are discussed in chapters XXXX.

Technically, the LHC functions by accelerating and colliding two counter-rotating beams of protons — or, alternatively, heavy ions — at four dedicated interaction points. The ring, buried about 100 m underground, extends over a circumference of 26.66 km, composed of eight curved arcs connected by eight straight sections. Each straight section spans roughly 528 m: four of them host the major LHC experiments, while the remaining four accommodate essential machine infrastructure such as radio-frequency systems, collimators, beam dumps, and other utilities.


\vspace{2 cm}

The LHC hosts four main experiments, each designed with complementary goals and detection technologies. ATLAS (\textit{A Toroidal LHC ApparatuS}) \cite{ATLAS_detector} and CMS (\textit{Compact Muon Solenoid}) \cite{CMS_detector} are the two general-purpose detectors. Despite their different designs — ATLAS being based on a large toroidal magnetic field and CMS on a high-field solenoidal magnet — they share the common mission of probing a wide range of phenomena, from Standard Model precision measurements to searches for physics Beyond the Standard Model. Both collaborations played a central role in the discovery of the Higgs boson in 2012 and continue to provide leading results in the search for new particles, such as supersymmetric states, dark matter candidates, or exotic resonances.

In contrast, LHCb (\textbf{Large Hadron Collider beauty}) \cite{LHCb_detector} focuses on the study of beauty and charm hadrons. Its forward geometry and high-precision vertex detector are optimized to measure rare decays, CP violation, and flavor-changing processes, which provide an indirect window into possible new physics contributions. Thanks to its unique design, LHCb has become a world-leading experiment in the field of flavor physics, testing the consistency of the Standard Model in the quark sector with unprecedented precision.

Finally, ALICE (\textit{A Large Ion Collider Experiment}) \cite{ALICE_detector} is dedicated to the exploration of strongly interacting matter under extreme conditions. By colliding heavy ions such as lead nuclei, ALICE recreates in the laboratory the quark–gluon plasma, a state of matter believed to have existed microseconds after the Big Bang. The experiment investigates its properties—such as collective flow, particle correlations, and jet quenching—shedding light on the dynamics of the strong nuclear force in the high-density regime.

Together, these four experiments form a complementary research program: while ATLAS and CMS push the energy frontier with broad discovery potential, LHCb and ALICE exploit the LHC’s versatility to study the flavor structure of the Standard Model and the behavior of nuclear matter at extreme temperatures and densities.


\textbf{In addition to its four main experiments, the LHC hosts a set of smaller, highly specialized detectors that broaden its physics reach. TOTEM (located near CMS) is devoted to measuring the total cross section of proton–proton interactions, elastic scattering, and diffraction processes. Its detectors, placed far from the interaction point in so-called “Roman pots,” are capable of detecting protons scattered at very small angles, providing insights into the strong interaction at low momentum transfer. Complementary to this, LHCf investigates neutral particles produced at very small angles relative to the beamline. Its measurements are particularly valuable for calibrating models of cosmic-ray interactions in the atmosphere, thereby linking collider physics to astroparticle physics.}

\textbf{Another dedicated experiment, MoEDAL, is designed to search for highly ionizing particles such as magnetic monopoles or other stable, massive exotic states predicted by certain extensions of the Standard Model.}

\textbf{More recently, new experiments have been installed to exploit the unique forward regions of the LHC. FASER is optimized to detect light and weakly interacting particles, such as dark photons or sterile neutrinos, which could be produced in large numbers along the beam direction but escape detection in central detectors. Similarly, SND@LHC (Scattering and Neutrino Detector at the LHC) is the first dedicated experiment at a collider to measure neutrinos produced in high-energy proton collisions, opening a new window into both neutrino physics and forward hadronic production.}

\textbf{These complementary experiments significantly enrich the LHC physics program. While the flagship detectors ATLAS, CMS, LHCb, and ALICE address broad or well-defined research domains, the smaller-scale experiments target highly specific questions, from the structure of soft QCD interactions to the search for entirely new forms of matter.}
