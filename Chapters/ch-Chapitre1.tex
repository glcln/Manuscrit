\section{Historical background of CERN}
\label{sec:Section1}

After World War II, European physics had fallen behind.  Visionary scientists – including Pierre Auger, Niels Bohr and others – proposed creating a pan‐European laboratory to reunite researchers and share resources.  In 1949 French physicist Louis de Broglie made the first formal proposal at the Lausanne Cultural Conference.  Follow-up actions culminated in UNESCO discussions and by 1952 eleven countries had signed an agreement to form a provisional council. The name CERN (French acronym for Conseil Européen pour la Recherche Nucléaire) was then adopted.

With the organization in place, CERN quickly built its first accelerators. The Synchrocyclotron (SC) began operation in 1957 as a 600 MeV proton machine. In 1958 the SC enabled CERN’s first major discovery: the observation of a charged-pion decaying into an electron and a neutrino. Soon after, the 28 GeV Proton Synchrotron (PS) came online in 1959, briefly making CERN home to the world’s highest-energy accelerator. The PS would continue to serve as a beam source for decades to come.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

During the 1960s particle detection at CERN was dominated by the low-rate visual, bubble and spark chambers, which produced vast numbers of photographic images. This method was slow and laborious. A breakthrough came in 1968 when Georges Charpak invented the multiwire proportional chamber, a gas-filled detector with many parallel wires each connected to amplifiers. Charpak’s chamber could count particles roughly 1,000 times faster than previous techniques, ushering in the modern electronic era of particle detectors.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

CERN next pioneered colliding-beam accelerators. In 1971 the Intersecting Storage Rings (ISR) began operation, creating the world’s first high-energy proton–proton collisions. The ISR missed the 1974 'November revolution' and also the bottom quark discovery in 1977 because of an initial lack of low-mass triggers, slow DAQ, and insufficient acceptance. However, its legacy enabled the next generation collider experiments: proton stacking, space charge compensation, beam diagnostics, stochastic cooling, van-der-Meer luminosity scans, importance of efficient triggers and large angle-coverage... This success paved the way for the Super Proton Synchrotron (SPS). Approved in the early 1970s, the SPS was a 7 km circular machine straddling the Franco–Swiss border. It first circulated beam in 1976 and achieved roughly 400 GeV collision energy (above its original 300 GeV design). Later, by injecting antiprotons, CERN converted the SPS into a proton–antiproton collider. In this mode the SPS led to the 1983 discovery of the W and Z bosons, a milestone in confirming the electroweak theory (for which Carlo Rubbia and Simon van der Meer won the Nobel Prize) \cite{WandZ}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Building on this progress, CERN inaugurated the Large Electron–Positron collider (LEP) in 1989. LEP was a 27 km tunnel (the largest circular collider then built) designed for high-precision studies of the electroweak interaction. In its first phase it ran at about 100 GeV and produced on the order of 17 million Z bosons. LEP’s experiments performed very precise measurements; notably they confirmed that there are exactly three light neutrino species (three generations of matter particles) \cite{LEPEW2006}. After roughly eleven years of operation, LEP was shut down in 2000 to make way for the next-generation collider using the same tunnel: the Large Hadron Collider.


\section{The Large Hadron Collider}

On the September 18th 2008, the first proton beam is successfully injected within the largest particle collider ever built, namely the Large Hadron Collider (LHC). This machine and its experiments are projects of superlatives: at that time, by comparison to the Tevatron, the direct particle accelerator "competitor" based at Fermilab and in operation since 1983, the LHC had a centre-of-mass energy collision seven times higher, a factor of ten in luminosity and sixteen times narrower bunch spacing; all in one ring four times larger.

Detector has been built as technological masterpieces, incorporating everything learned from previous experiments, and designed and optimised from scratch using detailed simulation. So far, their physics performance exceeds the design expectations.


The LHC \cite{EvansBryant2008}, located at CERN on the Franco-Swiss border, is the world’s largest and most powerful particle accelerator. Inaugurated in 2009, it represents the culmination of decades of progressive increases in accelerator energy at CERN. Its primary objective was the discovery of the Higgs boson --- the last missing element of the Standard Model (see section XXXX) --- a goal achieved in 2012 by the ATLAS \cite{ATLAS_Higgs} and CMS \cite{CMS_Higgs} collaborations. Beyond this milestone, the LHC was conceived as a versatile tool to probe particle interactions at the TeV scale, with the capability to deliver proton-proton collisions at a centre-of-mass energy up to $\sqrt{s}=14$ TeV. Although this nominal value has not yet been reached, the machine currently operates at a maximum energy of 13.6 TeV. At such energies, the LHC provides a unique opportunity to test the limits of the Standard Model: both by conducting high-precision measurements that may reveal subtle deviations from theoretical predictions, and by carrying out direct searches for phenomena Beyond the Standard Model (BSM) (some of which are discussed in chapters XXXX).

\subsection{Sub-accelerator complex}
\label{subsec:SubacceleratorComplex}

Technically, the LHC functions by accelerating and colliding two counter-rotating beams of protons — or, alternatively, heavy ions — at four dedicated interaction points (IP). The ring, buried about 100 m underground, extends over a circumference of 26.66 km, composed of eight 2.45-km-long curved arcs connected by eight straight sections. Each straight section spans roughly 545 m: four of them host the major LHC experiments, while the remaining four accommodate essential machine infrastructure such as radio-frequency systems, collimators, beam dumps, and other utilities, as shown in figure \ref{fig-LHC-layout}.

    \caption{Illustration of the LHC geometry and experiments location.}
    \label{fig-LHC-layout}
\end{figure}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The LHC ring is the final accelerator to a larger sub-accelerator complex. The production starts with a linear accelerator, the LINAC4, where protons are extracted from $\textrm{H}^{-}$ ions. Because of the weakly bond between the proton and two electrons, the latter can be easily stripped from the ion by going trough multiple layers of a carbon foil. At the end of this first stage, protons reach an energy of 160 MeV.

At this point, linear accelerators reach their limit, one could then consider circular collider to profit a higher centre-of-mass energy collisions at the end, despite the use of a magnetic dipoles with an increasing magnetic field to confine the beam within the pipe. The beams are then injected in the Proton Synchrotron Booster (PSB), before entering the PS and latter the SPS, reaching respectively the energy of 2 GeV, 26 GeV and 450 GeV. The last step consists of preparing the two counterclockwise beams of the LHC by performing super-cycles to synchronize beam train insertion at the correct position. 

A schematic view of the CERN accelerator complex \cite{CernComplexAccelerator_ref} is shown in figure \ref{fig:CERNComplexAccelerator}.

Once in the LHC, protons are accelerated to their nominal energy (from 3.5 to 6.8 TeV, depending on the year). For that oscillating microwave fields generated by 16 superconducting radio-frequency cavities (8 per beam), housed in 4 cryo-modules, that generate a longitudinal oscillating voltage in sync with the protons that keeps them tightly bunched and accelerate them with a field of 2 MV each (5 MV/m). Each cavity is driven by a high-power klystron, a tube containing electron beams, intensity-modulated to a frequency of 400 MHz. With that protons receive 480 keV of energy per turn, or 5.3 GeV/s, during a 20-minute ramp-up period before reaching the nominal energy. 

The beams are confined in a low pressure environment, around $10^{-13}$ atm, in order to mitigate beam losses resulting from collisions between the proton beam and unknown flying objects inside the beam pipe.

In each curved arc of the LHC, a magnetic field generated individually by the 1232 superconducting NbTi dipoles bends the beams toward the centre of the LHC ring. The intensity of the field goes from 0.54 to 8.33 T at the nominal as the beam accelerates. These magnets need to be cooled down to 1.9 K using super-fluid helium to let the current of 11800 A passes trough the magnet coils. So far, what limits the kinetic energy of the proton beams are the bending power of dipole magnets. To focus the beam, the LHC uses quadrupole magnets (for a total of 858) that act like magnetic lenses and focus the beam, especially at the interaction points, in order to increase luminosity. However, their focusing strength depends on the particle energy. 688 sextupole magnets are therefore used to correct this energy dependence. Finally, 168 octupole magnets introduce controlled non-linear effects that help stabilize the beam against collective instabilities (Landau damping).

Protons, as well as charged particles, emit electromagnetic radiation, known as \textit{synchroton radiation}, when they are accelerated along a curved trajectory. This energy loss per turn is scaled with the particle mass $m^{-4}$. With that electrons lose energy about $10^{13}$ times faster than protons. This is one of the main reason why protons have been chosen instead of electrons for the LHC. With a dipole field of 8.3 T, this correspond to a loss of 18 keV/turn. To compensate that, the RF cavities still operate, but to a lesser extent, under nominal conditions.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

At the nominal regime\footnote{Here for the LHC Run 3 campaign}, the LHC is circulating 2808 bunches, which are groups of about 100 billion protons (at the start). Even if the capacity of the ring is 3556 bunches, only a fraction of them are filled with protons, the beam are than organised as trains (groups of bunches) separated by empty bunches. Every bunch location is time separated from its previous by 25 ns, corresponding to the nominal LHC frequency of 40 MHz, and rotates at 11245 revolutions per second with a velocity as close as 3 m/s to the speed of light. Note that at the top energy (6.8 TeV), the stored beam energy corresponds to about 350 MJ.

Because the number of protons per bunch is decreasing after each beam crossing, the intensity of the quadrupole magnets are adjusted with time to keep the bunch squeeze enough. 

In case of beam failure, the LHC Beam Dumping System is activated to safely eject both beams into 10-meter-long graphite blocks. The beam dumping procedure is also routinely conducted at the conclusion of each LHC filing: after about eight hours, the bunches are sufficiently low in protons that they cannot be concentrated any further, and after another two to four hours, the beam is dumped.

\subsection{LHC experiments}
\label{subsec:LHCExperiments}

The LHC hosts four main experiments, each designed with complementary goals and detection technologies. 

\vspace{0.5 cm}

ATLAS (\textit{A Toroidal LHC ApparatuS}) \cite{ATLAS_detector} and CMS (\textit{Compact Muon Solenoid}) \cite{CMS_detector} are the two general-purpose detectors. Despite their different designs — ATLAS being based on a large toroidal magnetic field and CMS on a high-field solenoidal magnet — they share the common mission of probing a wide range of phenomena, from Standard Model precision measurements to searches for physics Beyond the Standard Model. Both collaborations played a central role in the discovery of the Higgs boson in 2012 and continue to provide leading results in the search for new particles, such as supersymmetric states, dark matter candidates, or exotic resonances.

In contrast, LHCb (\textit{Large Hadron Collider beauty}) \cite{LHCb_detector} focuses on the study of beauty and charm hadrons. Its forward geometry and high-precision vertex detector are optimized to measure rare decays, CP violation, and flavor-changing processes, which provide an indirect window into possible new physics contributions. Thanks to its unique design, LHCb has become a world-leading experiment in the field of flavor physics, testing the consistency of the Standard Model in the quark sector with unprecedented precision.

Finally, ALICE (\textit{A Large Ion Collider Experiment}) \cite{ALICE_detector} is dedicated to the exploration of strongly interacting matter under extreme conditions. By colliding heavy ions such as lead nuclei, ALICE recreates in the laboratory the quark–gluon plasma, a state of matter believed to have existed microseconds after the Big Bang. The experiment investigates its properties—such as collective flow, particle correlations, and jet quenching—shedding light on the dynamics of the strong nuclear force in the high-density regime.

Together, these four experiments form a complementary research program: while ATLAS and CMS push the energy frontier with broad discovery potential, LHCb and ALICE exploit the LHC’s versatility to study the flavor structure of the Standard Model and the behavior of nuclear matter at extreme temperatures and densities.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

In addition to its four main experiments, the LHC hosts a set of smaller, highly specialized detectors that broaden its physics reach.

TOTEM (\textit{TOTal Elastic and diffractive cross section Measurement}, located near CMS) is devoted to measuring the total cross section of proton–proton interactions, elastic scattering, and diffraction processes. Its detectors, placed far from the interaction point in so-called “Roman pots,” are capable of detecting protons scattered at very small angles, providing insights into the strong interaction at low momentum transfer. Complementary to this, LHCf (\textit{Large Hadron Collider forward}, near ATLAS) investigates neutral particles produced at very small angles relative to the beamline. Its measurements are particularly valuable for calibrating models of cosmic-ray interactions in the atmosphere, thereby linking collider physics to astroparticle physics.

Another dedicated experiment, MoEDAL (\textit{Monopole and Exotics Detector at the LHC}, that shares the cavern at Point 8 with LHCb), is designed to search for highly ionizing particles such as magnetic monopoles or other stable, massive exotic states predicted by certain extensions of the Standard Model.

More recently, new experiments have been installed to exploit the unique forward regions of the LHC. FASER (\textit{ForwArd Search ExpeRiment}) is optimized to detect light and weakly interacting particles, such as dark photons or sterile neutrinos, which could be produced in large numbers along the beam direction but escape detection in central detectors. Similarly, SND@LHC (\textit{Scattering and Neutrino Detector at the LHC}) is the first dedicated experiment at a collider to measure neutrinos produced in high-energy proton collisions, opening a new window into both neutrino physics and forward hadronic production.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

These complementary experiments significantly enrich the LHC physics program. While the flagship detectors ATLAS, CMS, LHCb, and ALICE address broad or well-defined research domains, the smaller-scale experiments target highly specific questions, from the structure of soft QCD interactions to the search for entirely new forms of matter.


\subsection{Collisions and Luminosity}
\label{subsec:CollisionsAndLuminosity}

The following subsection describe several key parameters commonly used in particle colliders, in particular the concept of luminosity — which is directly related to the event rate — and the number of simultaneous collisions occurring within a single event. Both parameters play a crucial role in accelerator physics. Although an event is technically defined as the result of a detector readout, in our field it is often used interchangeably with the term collision.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

CMS and the LHC use \textit{instantaneous luminosity} $\mathscr{L}$ information to understand the collision environment during data-taking. The number of events $\partial N$ in a given period of time $\partial t$, knowing the process cross-section $\sigma$ is given by eq. \ref{eq:nbrEvent}:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial N}{\partial t} = \mathscr{L} \cdot \sigma
    \label{eq:nbrEvent}
\end{equation}

Given the proton bunch structure of LHC beams, and assuming gaussian beam profiles colliding heads on \cite{LumiComputation}, the instantaneous luminosity is defined as eq. \ref{eq:instaneous_luminosity}: 

\begin{equation}
    \mathscr{L} = F \cdot \frac{N_p^2n_bf_{\textrm{rev}}\gamma}{4\pi\epsilon_n\beta^{*}}
    \label{eq:instaneous_luminosity}
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $N_p$ is the number of particles per bunch,
    \item $n_b$ the number of bunches per beam,
    \item $f_{\textrm{rev}}$ the beam revolution frequency,
    \item $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}$ the relativistic factor (with $v$ the particle velocity and $c$ the speed of light),
    \item $\epsilon_n$ the normalized transverse emittance, that characterizes the spread of the proton distribution in the transverse plane,
    \item $\beta^{*}$ the betatron function at the IP, that characterizes the bunch envelop that oscillates around the design orbit due to the focusing provided by the magnetic field, assuming $\beta_x=\beta_y$ (the smaller the value of $\beta^{*}$, the tighter and the smaller the beam size is).
    \item $F$ is the geometric luminosity reduction factor due to the crossing angle at the IP.
\end{itemize}

The latter is defined as in eq. \ref{eq:FactorF}:

\begin{equation}
    F=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\left( \frac{\theta_{\textrm{IP}}\cdot\sigma_z}{2\sigma_{\textrm{IP}}} \right)^2}}
    \label{eq:FactorF}
\end{equation}

It depends on the crossing angle $\theta_\textrm{IP}$ at the IP, as well as $\sigma_z$, the RMS bunch length along the proton beam and $\sigma_{\textrm{IP}}$ the transverse RMS beam size at the IP (assuming that $\sigma_x=\sigma_y$).

As discussed in \ref{subsec:SubacceleratorComplex}, since collisions occur at a 40 MHz frequency, the number of protons per bunch decreases rapidly with time, following a exponential decay: $\mathscr{L}\propto e^{-t/\tau_0}$ with $\tau_0$ of the order of 10h at the LHC today.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Finally, the \textit{integrated luminosity} L value is used by all analysts to measure cross sections of a process being studied, or to determine the number of events expected for physics processes with well-known cross sections. L is defined as the in eq. \ref{eq:intlumi}:

\begin{equation}
    \textrm{L} = \int \mathscr{L} \, \mathrm{d}t
    \label{eq:intlumi}
\end{equation}

At the LHC, the integrated luminosity is usually defined in inverse femtobarn $\mathrm{fb}^{-1}$\footnote{$1 \, \mathrm{fb}^{-1} = 10^{-39} \, \mathrm{cm}^2$} to better compare with the magnitude of the cross sections of particle physics processes. A comparison of the integrated luminosity recorded by CMS over the years of data-taking is presented in Fig. \ref{fig:intlumi}. Summed together, the integrated luminosity from 2010 to 2025 is close to 475 $\mathrm{fb}^{-1}$.

\subsection{Pile-Up}
\label{subsec:PileUp}

Monitoring real-time value of the instantaneous luminosity is tricky if using the \ref{eq:instaneous_luminosity}. This would require to know the precise value of the parameters. An alternative is to look after processes that we know exactly the cross-section of production $\sigma_{\textrm{ref}}$ and count how many of them are produced, which is the rate $\textrm{R}_{\textrm{ref}}$. Using eq. \ref{eq:cross-section_ref}:

\begin{equation}
    \textrm{R}_{\textrm{ref}} = \mathscr{L} \cdot \sigma_{\textrm{ref}}
    \label{eq:cross-section_ref}
\end{equation}

At the LHC, the reference collision process is the proton inelastic scattering with $\sigma_{\textrm{inel}}=68.6 \, \pm 0.5 \, (\textrm{syst.}) \, \pm \, 3.2 \, (\textrm{lumi}) \, \textrm{mb}$ \cite{pileUPxsection}. As the rate is also defined as the number of collisions, one can also define it as in eq. \ref{eq:pu_rate}:

\begin{equation}
    \textrm{R}_{\textrm{ref}} = \mu \cdot f_{\textrm{rev}}
    \label{eq:pu_rate}
\end{equation}

With $\mu$ the mean number of interaction per bunch crossing at the IP, called \textit{pile-up} (PU). Finally, the instantaneous luminosity is defined as in eq. \ref{eq:lumi_measured}:

\begin{equation}
    \mathscr{L} = \frac{\mu \cdot f_{\textrm{rev}}}{\sigma_{\textrm{inel}}}
    \label{eq:lumi_measured}
\end{equation}

This leads to an average pile-up $\mu$ of about 22 under the LHC's nominal design condition. However, as the luminosity steadily increased over time by a factor of 2-2.5 today, this has resulted in a higher average pile-up of 50 with peaks reaching values over 80. The pile-up values measured by the CMS collaboration for the different years of data-taking between Run 1 and Run 3 in Fig. \ref{fig:pileup}. Notice that the value of $\sigma_{\textrm{inel}}$ is different from the one given above, here, CMS is using the conventional value used for calculating the pileup.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

One can then notice that increasing the luminosity leads to increasing the pile-up per event. This point is challenging for every detector: a higher number of collisions will lead to higher occupancy within the detector, that may then lead to a lower detection efficiency. To optimize the data acquisition and the amount of pile-up during a run, the instantaneous luminosity is then maintained around its optimized value through luminosity levelling \cite{LumiLevelling}. \subsection{Schedule}

The physics program at the LHC is divided into two primary phases: Phase-1 (2011-2026) and Phase-2 ( 2030-2041). 
So far, Phase-1 program has been divided into 3 period of data-taking, called \textit{Runs}, separated by \textit{Long Shutdowns} (LS) which are periods of upgrades and maintenance of the detectors. During a year of a Run the proton-proton collisions program lasts for about 6 month, followed by heavy ion physics and then by a technicall pause during the winter. Within a year, Runs are divided into \textit{Eras} to mark changes in experimental conditions, beam energy and detector setups. Eras are then divided into runs themselves which are characterized by different fills of the beams.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Run 1 (2009-2013) marked the first completed operational phase of the LHC. During this period, CMS recorded 30 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$ of data at the centre-of-mass energy of 7 TeV (2010-2011, corresponding to 6.1 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$) and 8 TeV (2011-2013, corresponding to 23.3 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$) with a bunch spacing of 50 ns. These data led to the discovery of the Higgs boson by the CMS and ATLAS collaborations on July 4th, 2012 \cite{ATLAS_Higgs} \cite{CMS_Higgs}.

After a LS1 of 3 years to upgrade the superconducting magnets and detector systems to support higher luminosity, Run 2 (2015-2018) started with an upgraded centre-of-mass energy of 13 TeV and a bunch spacing of 25 ns. CMS recorded an integrated luminosity of about 138 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$.

Run 3 (2022-2026) brought up the centre-of-mass energy to 13.6 TeV (still below the expected 14 TeV at the design of the LHC). So far, this period has been marked by a spectacular data collection campaign. CMS has recorded an integrated luminosity of respectively 38 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$, 30 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$, 113 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$ and 114 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$ from 2022 to 2025. Despite the ageing of the detector, a last six month of data-taking in 2026 is ongoing.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

At the end of Run 3, the transition of the HL-LHC (\textit{High Luminosity LHC}) will start during the LS3, planned to last 4 years. With the large detector and accelerator upgrades \cite{HLLHC_TDR}, the expected instantaneous luminosity will be increased by a factor 5 to 7.5 and the centre-of-mass energy to 14 TeV. Over the 12 years of planned operations, HL-LHC is expected to deliver a total of 3000-4000 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$.


\section{The Compact Muon Solenoid experiment}

Despite a less marketable name than its counterparts at the LHC, the Compact Muon Solenoid experiment \cite{CMS_detector} is remarkable on its design and programme that aims to cover a wide range of physics phenomena. CMS is located inside the 100 meters underground cavern at the IP5, at the north of the LHC ring. As the name suggest, every sub-part constituting this detector has been compacted in an cylinder 21 meters long and 15 meters in diameter for a total weight of 14500 tons (a volume six times smaller and twice lighter than ATLAS). CMS has been specifically designed to highlight high-momentum muons, with muon chambers taking up 80\% of the detector space. Lastly, the solenoid magnet generating a magnetic field of 3.8 T, bends the trajectories of charged particles to accurately measure their momentum and electric charge. In the following, the CMS detector has been carefully dissected component by component, both from the perspective of their instrumentation and their software operation, with particular emphasis on the event reconstruction algorithms that bridge raw detector signals into meaningful physics observables.

\subsection{Coordinate system}

The CMS detector has adopted a right-handed cartesian coordinate system with the collision point as its origin (in the centre of the detector). The x-axis points toward the LHC centre and the y-axis to toward the surface, both forming a perpendicular plane to the beam pipe, known as the transverse plane. The z-axis represents the longitudinal axis and points toward the Jura mountains, or more physically speaking in the counter-clockwise direction of the LHC.

Because of its cylindrical detector shape, the partial spherical coordinate system is also used. The azimutal angle $\phi$ is defined as the angle measured from the x-axis to the y-axis, the polar angle $\theta$ is measured from the z-axis and the radial distance $r$ defined in the transverse plane. In collider physics, and more generally in high energy physics, one can also define the \textit{pseudo-rapidity} $\eta$, deriving from taking the ultra-relativistic regime of the rapidity\footnote{The rapidity is defined as $\frac{1}{2}\ln\left( \frac{E+p_z}{E-p_z} \right)$ where $E$ is the energy of the particle and $p_z$ its longitudinal momentum. This quantity is zero when a particle is close to transverse plane, but tends to $\pm\infty$ when a particle is moving close to the beam axis in either direction.} (when $pc\gg mc^2$), as defined in eq. \ref{eq:pseudorapidity}:

\begin{equation}
    \eta = - \ln \left[ \tan \left( \frac{\theta}{2} \right) \right]
    \label{eq:pseudorapidity}
\end{equation}

Note that, a particle located in the transverse plane has a zero pseudo-rapidity, and as the particle approaches the z-axis, that pseudo-rapidity tends toward infinity. This quantity is invariant under Lorentz boosts along the beam axis, meaning that the difference in pseudo-rapidity $\Delta \eta$ between two particles remains unchanged in all inertial frames related by longitudinal boosts.

We can therefore define the \textit{angular distance} $\Delta R$ separating two particles $i$ and $j$ such as eq. \ref{eq:deltaR}:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta R = \sqrt{{\Delta \eta_{ij}}^2 + {\Delta \phi_{ij}}^2}
    \label{eq:deltaR}
\end{equation}  

where $\Delta \eta_{ij} = | \eta_i - \eta_j|$ and $\Delta \phi_{ij} = | \phi_i - \phi_j|$ so that $\Delta R$ is also Lorentz invariant\footnote{$\phi=\arctan(p_y/p_x)$, a longitudinal boost along the z-axis does not affect it.} for referential change along the beam axis direction. \subsection{Detector dissection}

The CMS detector is organized as an onion in which sub-detectors are arranged in layers around the beamline, each one of them having a specific role in the reconstruction of physics events. A schematic view of the CMS detector is presented Figure \ref{fig:cmsdetector}. The cylindrical structure of the detector can be divided into two parts: the \textit{barrel} region (also referred as the central region) and the two \textit{endcaps} (also referred as the forward regions).

\begin{figure}

\subsubsection{Silicon Tracker}

The silicon tracker is the closest sub-detector to the IP, its goal is to reconstruct charged particles bended trajectories --- called \textit{tracks} --- along with their momentum. As the charged particles, produced directly or not by the collisions, propagate through the tracker, they ionize the material. The generation of charges are gathered by silicon sensors. This technology provides excellent spatial resolution and radiation hardness, along with a low material budget to minimize the multiple scattering of charged particles. On top of that, silicon has two additional benefits compared to homologue tracking detector such as drift chambers or time projection chambers: high silicon density and low electron-hole creation energy, within the semiconductor material, enable thin sensors with sufficient signal; and high mobility of charge carriers in silicon allows fast charge collection ($\sim5$ ns for an electron).

The tracker is divided into two parts: the silicon pixel, the closer part to the beam pipe, and the silicon strip sub-detectors, above the later. Each of them made of multiple layers with different sensor geometries optimized based on the distance from the IP. The tracker covers a radial range of 2.9 to 11O cm and a wide range of pseudo-rapidity up to $|\eta|<2.5$ using the information available in both the barrel and the endcaps regions. This coverage is accomplished by the 18 concentric barrel layers and the 10 endcap disks.

The readout electronics are synchronized with the 25 ns LHC bunch crossing clock, with so, hits\footnote{defined as the charge deposited inside a group of pixels or strips that is collected and used to reconstruct tracks later} are associated to their corresponding bunch crossing. Pixel and strip sensors are cooled down to $-10^{\circ}\textrm{C}$ and $-15^{\circ}\textrm{C}$ respectively to reduce the thermal noise and reduce the radiation-induced damage. A layout of the CMS tracker is shown Fig. \ref{fig:tracker}.

\noindent \textbf{Pixel Tracker}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

At the start of the LHC, the pixel detector was made of three barrel layers and two disks in each forward region. During the winter break in 2016-2017, the pixel detector has been upgraded. The first one was a change in the pixel supports, with a shift from on-board to off-board electronics, in addition to a $\textrm{CO}_2$ cooling system implemented. Secondly, an additional barrel layer and endcap disk completed the pixel detector \cite{pixeldetector}. A layout of the pixel tracker is presented in Fig. \ref{fig:pixeltracker}.

The four barrel pixel modules (BPIX) cover radial distances from 2.9 to 16 cm from the IP, while the three forward disks (FPIX) are set longitudinally between 29.1 and 51.6 cm from the centre with a coverage up to $|\eta|<2.5$. The BPIX is made of 1184 modules while the 672 for the FPIX, with each module featuring a lattice of $160 \times 416$ pixels cells within a rectangular active area of $16.2 \times 64.8 \, \textrm{mm}^2$, with each cell measuring $100 \times 150 \, \mu\textrm{m}^2$ with a thickness of 285 $\mu\textrm{m}$. This fine granularity enables the pixel tracker to reach a global hit efficiency of about 99 \%, while offering hit resolutions of approximately 10 $\mu\textrm{m}$ and 25 $\mu\textrm{m}$ along the transverse and longitudinal directions.

This first part of the tracker insure the reconstruction of the primary vertex --- the initial hard-scatter interaction point --- and to discriminate it from PU. On top of that, it enables the identification of in-flight decays, such as those from B-hadrons, D-hadrons or $\tau$ leptons, with the reconstruction of secondary vertices.

\noindent \textbf{Strip Tracker}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

As the particle flux decrease the squared distance, a lower granularity detector can be used. With so, silicon micro-strip sensors are considered instead of pixel ones.

The silicon strip tracker is divided into four regions. Four layers of strip sensors in the Tracker Inner Barrel (TIB) and six in the Tracker Outer Barrel (TOB), both of them being in central region. The barrel region is closed by three disks in the Tracker Inner Disk (TID) and nine disks in the Tracker End Cap (TEC) in each forward region. With so, the silicon strip tracker cover radial distances from 20 to 110 cm and a longitudinal distance up to 282 cm, spanning a pseudo-rapidity range up to $|\eta|<2.5$ \cite{CMStracker}.

In total, 15148 modules fill the volume of the silicon strip detector. Some of them contains either one (mono) or two (stereo) silicon sensors, shown respectively in red and blue on Fig. \ref{fig:tracker}, for a total of 24244 sensors. Stereo modules are mounted back-to-back with the second sensor being tilted by a 100 mrad angle to reconstruct a three-dimensional information of the hits. TIB and TOB parts are made of concentric cylinders, with modules being parallel to the beam axis so that it ensures a good $r-\phi$ measurement, while TID and TEC are rings where the sensors are in the transverse plane, so that the the z$-\phi$ remains good in the forward region. \subsubsection{Calorimeters}

While the role of the tracker is to reconstruct charged particles trajectories, it's not enough to labelled particles. On top of that, neutral particles are not detected when they propagate through silicon sensors. To complete the tracker, two calorimeter systems are mounted above the later: an electromagnetic calorimeter (ECAL) \cite{CMSecal} and the hadronic calorimeter (HCAL) \cite{CMShcal}, both designed to measure the energy of incident particles. They consist of dense materials chosen and arranged to force the incoming particles to decay through a cascade of secondary particles of lower energy, called as \textit{particle shower}. 

The ECAL has been designed to stop electromagnetic showers, such showers result from electrons and photons losing their energy through interaction with the matter. This energy loss occurs through processes such as Bremsstrahlung (photon produced by the deceleration of a charged particles) and electron-positron pair production (where photons are converted to electron-positron pairs). As the shower progress inside the ECAL, additional secondary particles are produced, leading to low-energetic photons creation. Thus, the total collected energy corresponds to the energy of the incoming particle.

The HCAL, placed above the ECAL, targets hadronic showers. Such showers are driven by the strong nuclear force and result from charged or neutral hadron interactions, leading to the creation and decay of secondary hadrons. The latter ionize the material as they propagate and generates photons that are then collected by the scintillators. A hadronic shower consist of: electromagnetic energy (e.g.: neutral pion decaying into two photons) ($\mathcal{O}\,50\%$), visible non-electromagnetic energy (e.g. from charged pions, kaons or muons) ($\mathcal{O}\,25\%$), invisible energy (nuclear fission, excitation, neutrons) ($\mathcal{O}\,25\%$) and escaped energy ($\mathcal{O}\,2\%$).

Both calorimeters are segmented in the transverse plane to increase the accuracy of the energy and trajectory of incoming particles.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\noindent \textbf{Electromagnetic calorimeter}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The ECAL is divided into three sections: the Barrel ECAL (EB) --- covering a pseudo-rapidity range $|\eta|<1.479$ ---, the ECAL Endcap (EE) that extends the coverage to $|\eta|<3.0$ and the ECAL Preshower system (ES) located at the entry of the EE. A Layout of an ECAL slice is shown in Figure \ref{fig:ECAL}.

The calorimeter is homogeneously populates with lead tungstate ($\textrm{PbWO}_4$) scintillating crystals. This material of a density of $8.28 \, \textrm{g}/\textrm{cm}^3$ provides a small radiation length\footnote{Denoted by $X_0$, this is the mean distance a high energetic electron travels before losing $e^{-1}=0.37$ of its energy by bremsstrahlung. At first order, $X_0 \sim 1433 \cdot \frac{A}{Z(Z+1)(11.319-\ln(Z))}$ where $Z$ and $A$ are respectively the atomic number and the mass number of the nucleus.} ($X_0=0.89$ cm) as well as small Molière radius\footnote{Radius of the cone containing 90 \% of the shower energy. One can derive it using the radiation length: $R_M = 0.0265 \cdot X_0 \cdot (Z+1.2)$.} ($R_M=2.19$ cm) but also a natural tolerance to radiation. Lead tungstate has a low scintillation time, collecting 80 \% of the light within the 25 ns time between two bunch crossing. On the other hand, lead tungstate has a relatively low light yield (a few photons per MeV) wich implies the use of photo-detectors with internal amplification factor of 50 (10) in the barrel (endcap) region.

A total of 75848 crystals compose the ECAL, with 61200 in the barrel (length of $25.8 X_0\approx23$ cm) and $2 \times 7324$ in the two endcaps (length of $27 X_0 \approx 24$ cm). The front face cross-section of the crystals corresponds to a granularity of $\Delta \eta \times \Delta \phi = 0.0175 \times 0.0175$ (giving a surface of $2.2 \times 2.2 \, \textrm{cm}^2$ for the barrel crystals and $2.86 \times 2.86 \, \textrm{cm}^2$ for the endcaps ones) \cite{CMSecalcrystals}. With such values, electromagnetic showers are retrained into the crystals, thus, no secondary particles are lost in passive absorber materials. On the other hand, the absence of longitudinal segmentation in the CMS ECAL limits direct access to shower depth information, reducing intrinsic electromagnetic/hadronic discrimination power and particle identification capabilities compared to sampling calorimeters with longitudinal segmentation. As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:ECAL}, the crystals are tilted in the direction of the IP to prevent photons entering into adjacent crystals and escaping detection. 

The ES does not contain any crystals but rather two layers of lead absorber with layers of silicon strip sensors used to distinguish single photons from photons coming from neutral pions decay.

Despite the distance from the IP, the transparency of the crystals is affected with time (and radiation), changing the ECAL energy resolution. Calibration on the transparency of the crystals is performed using lasers where the light is injected when there is no bunch. The energy resolution of the ECAL, $\sigma_{\textrm{ECAL}}$, has been measured in 7 TeV data \cite{CMSecal}, and given by eq. \ref{eq:ecalresolution}:

\begin{equation}
    \left( \frac{\sigma_{\textrm{ECAL}}}{E} \right)^2 = \left( \frac{0.028}{\sqrt{E}} \right)^2 + \left( \frac{41.5 \, \textrm{MeV}}{E} \right)^2 + 0.003^2
    \label{eq:ecalresolution}
\end{equation}

with $E$ the photon or electron energy. The ECAL energy resolution is described as the quadratic sum of three terms: a stochastic term ($\propto 1/\sqrt{E}$), arising from statistical fluctuations in shower development and photon collection; a noise term ($\propto 1/E$), due to electronic noise and pileup; and a constant term, accounting for energy-independent effects such as calibration uncertainties and detector non-uniformities, which dominates at high energy.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\noindent \textbf{Hadron calorimeter}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The HCAL is segmented into four parts, as depicted in Figure \ref{fig:CMShcal}: the Barrel (HB) and the Endcap (HE), that covers a pseudo-rapidity range up to $|\eta|<3$; the Outer barrel (HO) and the Forward (HF). The latter extends the coverage to $|\eta|<5$ and situated at $|z|=11.1 \, \textrm{m}$. The HO is sitting above the magnet to contain the most energetic particle showers that would have passed through the EB and HB.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{Images/Ch1/CMShcalpicture.pdf}
    \caption{Longitudinal layout of one quarter of the CMS HCAL, showing the location of the HCAL barrel (HB), endcap (HE), outer barrel (HO) and forward (HF) calorimers \cite{CMShcal}.}
    \label{fig:CMShcal}
\end{figure}

The HB and HE are made with interspersed brass absorbers\footnote{A curious fact: some of the brass used comes from dismantled Russian World War II artillery shells.} (70\% copper and 30 \% zinc) and plastic scintillators. The absorber material has a small nuclear interaction length\footnote{mean free path of hadrons between strong collisions} ($\lambda_I \sim 16.4$ cm), with so, HCAL remains compact while stopping the maximum of hadronic showers. The plastic scintillator is motivated by its fast response (within 1 ns). The HO is composed of this plastic scintillator layers between magnet's iron flux return yokes, which serve as absorber material. Finally the HF used hard quartz fibres, alternated with steel absorber layers, instead of the plastic scintillator to deal with the higher flux of energetic particles near the beam axis. In such fibres, ionising particles release their energy in form of Cherenkov radiation, read-out by photo-multiplier tubes \cite{CMShcal}. Note that in most materials the nuclear interaction length $\lambda_I$ is much larger than the radiation length $X_0$. As a result, electromagnetic showers develop over a much shorter distance scale than hadronic showers. This allows a compact electromagnetic calorimeter to be placed upstream, where electrons and photons are fully contained, while hadrons, which interact over the larger scale $\lambda_I$, predominantly shower in the downstream hadronic calorimeter.

Technically speaking, the HCAL granularity, as the ECAL's one, is defined by the segmentation of the wedges $\Delta \eta \times \Delta \phi$. The value depends on the part of the calorimeter: $0.087 \times 0.087$ (corresponding to a $5 \times 5$ array of ECAL crystals) for the HB and HO, $0.17 \times 0.17$ for the HB and $0.175 \times 0.175$ for the HF.

Similarly to the ECAL, the relative energy resolution for the HCAL, $\sigma_{\textrm{HCAL}}$ is given by eq. \ref{eq:hcalresolution} \cite{CMShcalcrystals}. The energy resolution of a hadronic calorimeter is significantly affected by invisible energy losses, arising from nuclear breakup, neutron production, nuclear excitation, and binding energy losses. Since this fraction of the shower energy does not generate detectable ionization signals, it introduces intrinsic event-by-event fluctuations that degrade the calorimetric energy resolution.

\begin{equation}
    \left ( \frac{\sigma_{\textrm{HCAL}}}{E} \right)^2 = \left( \frac{0.943}{\sqrt{E}} \right)^2 + 0.084^2
    \label{eq:hcalresolution}
\end{equation}


\subsubsection{Solenoid magnet}

Charged particles with electric charge $q$ and velocity $\overrightarrow{v}$ bend when exposed to a magnetic field $\overrightarrow{B}$ according to the Lorentz force $\overrightarrow{F}=q\left( \overrightarrow{v} \times \overrightarrow{B}\right)$. This property is used by particle physicists in order to perform track reconstruction of such particles. 

Thus, CMS solenoid is a central component of the CMS detector. The magnet consists of a 12.5 m long superconducting coil with an external diameter of 6 m that produces a quasi-uniform magnetic field of 3.8 T along the z-axis within its volume. The coil itself is approximately 60 cm thick and in contrast to ATLAS, where the solenoid is located in front of the calorimeters, the CMS design places the magnet outside the calorimetric system. This configuration avoids additional material upstream of the ECAL and HCAL, thereby preserving the intrinsic calorimetric response and improving the overall reconstruction performance for electromagnetic objects and jets \cite{CMSmagnet}. The magnetic field variation map is shown in Figure \ref{fig:CMSmagnet}, used for precise momentum reconstruction.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth,trim={0 0 7cm 0},clip]{Images/Ch1/magnet.png}
    \caption{Color map of the magnetic field $|B|$ generated by the solenoid magnet and the corresponding magnetic field lines for a longitudinal section of CMS \cite{CMSmagnet}.}
    \label{fig:CMSmagnet}
\end{figure}

The magnet itself is made of a metal alloy of Nobium-Titanium (NbTi). To reach the superconductivity regime, the magnet is cooled down to 4.1 K by a helium liquid cryostat. Surrounding the solenoid, the iron return yoke\footnote{Interestingly, the magnet and the iron return yoke account for approximately 12500T, or 89\% of the total mass of the CMS detector.} closes the magnetic flux and provides an approximately 2 T magnetic field within the muon detection system. The field strength and direction vary across the different detector regions due to the geometry of the yoke and the magnetic flux return path.


\subsubsection{Muon chambers}

So far, Standard Model particles are stopped by the calorimeter system of CMS, but one exception remains: the muon. Such particles are frequently produced in various physical processes, thus, it is imperative to accurately measure their properties. With a mean lifetime of 2.2 $\mu\textrm{m}$ ($\sim650$ m in the particle frame), the particle is considered stable at the detector scale, meaning they go through the entire sub-detector system. Moreover, because of their large mass (200 times heavier than an electron), they exhibit less bremsstrahlung, resulting in reduced energy loss along their path. Finally, despite they are leaving hits within the tracker, muons cannot be identified directly using this information.

With all of that, the muon system , or the so-called muon chambers, is the last sub-detector covering CMS \cite{CMSmuonsystem}. It was designed to have three functions: efficient muon identification, precise momentum measurement, and triggering capability. These function are enabled by the high-field solenoidal magnet and flux-return yoke. Muon chambers are located outside the solenoid coil and HO calorimeter, they are inter-spaced between the iron plates forming the return yoke of the magnet. A layout of the muon system is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:muonsystem}. It is divided into three sub systems with the Drift Tubes (DTs) in the barrel region, the Cathode Strip Chambers (CSCs) in the forward region, completed with the Resistive Plate Chambers (RPCs). The Muon Barrel (MB) is made of 5 wheels along the beam pipe, each one divided into 12 segment of 30° to cover the volume of the CMS detector. While the Muon Endcap (ME) is divided into rings in the transverse plane.

Note that in 2017, an additional system has been added in the inner endcap, namely the Gas Electron Multiplier system (GEM), to gain operational experience and perform a preliminary investigation of detector performance for the HL-LHC program \cite{CMSmuonsystem}.

Because of a lower track rate compared to the inner tracker, less granular and more economical gas detectors are used. The muon system is subdivided into three part, each one based on a different technology. Nevertheless, still able to cover large areas with a reasonable response time.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\noindent \textbf{Drift tubes (DTs)}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

As the muon rate is lower in the barrel region, drift tube technology has been chosen to cover it (up to $|\eta|<1.2$). DTs are organized into four concentric cylinders composed of drift chambers (60 for each of the three first layers and 70 for the outermost one). Drift chambers consist of rectangular drift cells of $44 \times 13 \, \textrm{mm}^2$ cross section and 2 to 3 meter long. They contain a 50 $\mu \textrm{m}$ gold-plated stainless-steel anode wire at the centre and are filled with a gas mixture containing 85\% Ar and 15\% $\textrm{CO}_2$ that is ionized when a muon traverses this volume. The resulting charged particles drift under the applied electric field toward the electrodes, where they are amplified to produce an electrical signal. The drift time of charges allows for a transversal measurement with respect to the wire, with a resolution of about 2.6 ns. To complete the information of one muon hit, multiple drift chambers a grouped together to form 4 layers of parallel cells --- called a superlayer (SL) ---, then a chamber made of 2 SLs : one with the cells parallel to the beam axis and one orthogonal. This improve the resolution in $r-\phi$ and $r-$z coordinates, giving spacial resolution of 100 $\mu \textrm{m}$ and 500 $\mu \textrm{m}$ respectively. \noindent \textbf{Cathode Strip Chambers (CSCs)}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Located in the endcap, where the particles flux is higher, the CSCs are dedicated detector that address these conditions by having a faster response time. \subsection{Trigger system}

At the LHC design, bun crossing occures every 25 ns, raising the number of collisions at 40 million every second. Assuming that an event can be stored in 1 MB of data, that would correspond to 40 TB/s to save. This rate is beyond the CMS Data Acquisition System (DAQ) capacity that is recording, processing and storing events at a rate of about 100 MB/s. On top of that, a fraction of collisions come from low-energy proton-proton interaction, resulting to poor physics events not that do not have to be saved. In order to cope with the bunch crossing rate, a two-level triggering system and a computer farm has been adopted by the CMS DAQ \cite{CMStrigger}. 

The trigger system allows a quick selection of events of interesting, reducing the 40 MHz input rate as low as 1kHz. The later is divided in two levels: a first one purely \textit{hardware}, built by a dedicated custom-designed electronics system and a second one \textit{software} based.

As pile-up increases, so does the particle flux. Since most physics processes of interest have a significantly lower cross section than common QCD background processes, prescales are applied to the corresponding triggers in order to select only a fraction of these high-rate events, primarily for calibration and monitoring purposes.

\subsubsection{The Level-1 trigger}

The Level-1 trigger (L1) functions as a hardware system with a finite latency of 3.8 $\mu$s.The fixed latency is set by the number of steps plus data transfer times. Within this time, based on the presence of signals from the calorimeters and the muon chambers, L1 system assesses an event and decided to accept or reject it.

L1 trigger must deliver a decision every bunch crossing, despite a latency much longer. For that a pipeline decisions is built, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:L1pipeline}. Raw data are continuously stored in a front-end pipeline memory of approximately 100 bunch crossings (corresponding to the 3.8 $\mu$s latency). Upon a trigger decision, data are either accepted and forwarded to a derandomiser buffer, or rejected. The derandomiser converts the random arrival of accepted events into a steady readout stream. Two sources of dead-time must be controlled: a simple dead-time of ~1\% arising from a 4 BC veto after each L1 Accept, and a complex dead-time dependent on the derandomiser occupancy, required to remain below 1\% at 75 kHz and 6\% at 100 kHz trigger rate \cite{CMStrigger}.

After all, events rate are reduced to 100 kHz and are then transferred to the HLT for further filtering.

\subsubsection{The High-Level trigger}

The High-Level trigger (HLT) is software-based selection system using about CPUs and GPUs to cope with the increased luminosity. With a latency of 90 ms per event, it reduces physics rate from L1 input of 100 kHZ to a few kHz. Each event undergoes object reconstruction for entities like electrons, muons, photons, taus, or jets. To do so, data-processing is done by a set of algorithms --- HLT path ---, each having a dedicated physics reconstruction that keep topologies of interest. They are designed as a succession of reconstruction steps and filters, such that an event that would not pass a step does not go through all of them, hence keeping the HLT decision flow optimised \cite{CMShlt}.

Monitoring the output rates of the L1 and HLT triggers is essential for assessing the overall performance of the trigger system, as it directly determines what data is recorded for physics analyses. During data-taking, a dedicated shift crew oversees the smooth operation of the experiment, with each member assigned specific responsibilities. In particular, the trigger shifter monitors trigger rates in real-time and adjusts prescales accordingly to maintain a stable output rate and prevent the data acquisition system from being overloaded. Since the start of Run 3, I have personally contributed to this effort by completing 27 shifts of 8 hours each, dedicated to trigger system monitoring.

\subsubsection{Data storage}

Once events of interest have been assessed by the trigger system, they are encoded in a raw data-format locally on disk. The data recorded by CMS is then distributed, stored and analysed across a hierarchical computing infrastructure known as the Worldwide LHC Computing Grid (WLCG) \cite{WLCG} among the different CERN institutes, split in different tiers. Together, it combines about 1.4 million computer cores and 1.5 exabytes\footnote{$1 \, \textrm{EB} = 10^6 \,\, \textrm{TB}$} of storage.

At the top of this hierarchy, Tier-0, located at CERN, is responsible for the initial backup of raw data as well as the first calibration and reconstruction pass. It provides 20\% of the WLCG resources with its 800 PB of data storage. The processed data is then replicated to a set of Tier-1 centers, which provide long-term storage on tape and handle reprocessing campaigns as well as all types of data processing. Tier-2 centers, typically hosted at universities and research institutes, also perform all types of processing and serve as a resource for Monte Carlo simulation production. Finally, Tier-3 facilities, generally local computing clusters within research groups, are dedicated exclusively to physics analysis.


\section{Event reconstruction, identification and calibration}

Among the various particles produced at each proton-proton collision, only a few of them are stable at the detector scale. The most common are the muons $\mu^\pm$, the electrons $\textrm{e}^\pm$, the charged hadrons (proton p, pions $\pi^\pm$, kaons $\textrm{K}^\pm$) and the neutral ones (neutron n, neutral pion $\pi^0$) and the photons $\gamma$. Because of different physics interactions, they leave different experimental signatures within the CMS detector. A representation of various particles travelling inside CMS is shown on Figure \ref{fig:CMSslicePF}.

Therefore, a complete reconstruction of events is performed thanks to the Particle Flow (PF) algorithm. This algorithm, detailed in the section bellow, reconstruct the basic particles listed above by looking after tracker hits, energy deposit within the calorimeters or muon chamber hits. Afterward, the latter are used to reconstruct more complex objects such as particle \textit{jets}, \textit{missing energy} in the transverse plane (MET).

\subsection{Track reconstruction}

The reconstruction of particles inside CMS entails knowing particle's trajectory and momentum. Thus, tracks and primary vertices reconstruction is a primordial step of particle identification. A Combinatorial Track Finder (CTF) has been developed by the collaboration \cite{CTF_track_cms} \cite{CERN_CTF} to achieve that. It relies on iterative Kalman Filter (KF) \cite{KF} applied to the tracker and muon chamber hits.

Thanks to the magnetic field $B_z$ along the beam axis , particle trajectories are bend and described as helices that propagate toward the beam pipe. One can derive the expression of the transverse momentum $p_T$ of a particle with an algebraic charge $z_e$ moving in such a field, knowing the radius of curvature of the helix $\mathcal{R}_\textrm{C}$, this is given by eq. \ref{eq:ptreconstruction}.

\begin{align}
    p_T \, [\textrm{GeV/c}] = 0.3 \cdot z_e \cdot B_z \, [T] \cdot \mathcal{R}_\textrm{C} \, [\textrm{m}]
    \label{eq:ptreconstruction}
\end{align}

Despite being elegant, strong assumptions are done by assuming particles moving in a uniform magnetic field in the vacuum, without losing energy within the matter. The multi-step CTF uses multiple hits information within the tracker to reduce the quantity of \textit{fake tracks} --- wrong reconstruction of trajectories --- while keeping a high tracking efficiency. This is done in 5 main steps:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Hit reconstruction
    \item Seed generation
    \item Iterative track reconstruction
    \item Ambiguity resolution
    \item Final track fit
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{Hit reconstruction}

Hit measurement relies on charged particle energy deposit within the matter, the physics process of interaction between particles and matter is more detailed in Chapter XXX. These energy deposits or \textit{clusters} are grouped together if the signal-over-noise ratio ($S/N$) is above a given threshold after amplification and \textit{zero suppression}\footnote{Zero suppression is a data reduction technique in which only channels with signals above a predefined threshold are recorded, while channels consistent with noise are discarded.} steps. Strip clusters are built if $S/N>3$ for a single strip, then if nearby strips have $S/N>2$, they are grouped and a strip cluster is labelled if and only if the $S/N$ of the cluster is five times greater than the square-root of the sum of the RMS-noise of each individual strip inside it.

Similar algorithm is applied on pixel hits. A cluster is based on a pixel cell having $S/N>6$ and adjacent pixels are grouped if they satisfy $S/N>5$. All together, the pixel cluster shall comply with $S/N>10.1$. After all, the pixel hit resolution at the end of Run-3 is about $40 \, \mu\textrm{m} \, \times \, 75 \, \mu\textrm{m}$ in the $r-\phi$ and $z$ coordinates respectively \cite{pixelHitresolution}. While varying from 15 $\mu$m to 40 $\mu$m for the $z$ coordinate on strip modules, depending of the strip pitch \cite{stripHitresolution}.

Those values of $S/N$ threshold evolve with time, decreasing with respect to the integrated luminosity due to radiation damage \cite{stripHitresolution}. 

\subsubsection{Seed generation}
