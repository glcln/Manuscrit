\section{Historical background of CERN}
\label{sec:Section1}

After World War II, European physics had fallen behind. Visionary scientists, including Pierre Auger and Niels Bohr, among others, responded by proposing a pan-European laboratory to reunite researchers and share resources. In 1949, French physicist Louis de Broglie made the first formal proposal at the Lausanne Cultural Conference. This initiated a series of follow-up actions, which culminated in UNESCO discussions. By 1952, eleven countries had signed an agreement to form a provisional council, and the name CERN (French acronym for Conseil Européen pour la Recherche Nucléaire) was then adopted.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

With the organization in place, CERN quickly built its first accelerators. The Synchrocyclotron (SC) began operation in 1957 as a 600 MeV proton machine. In 1958, the SC enabled CERN’s first major discovery: the observation of a charged pion decaying into an electron and a neutrino. Soon after, the 28 GeV Proton Synchrotron (PS) came online in 1959, briefly making CERN home to the world’s highest-energy accelerator. The PS would continue to serve as a beam source for decades to come.

During the 1960s, particle detection at CERN was dominated by the low-rate visual, bubble, and spark chambers, which produced vast numbers of photographic images. This method was slow and laborious. A breakthrough came in 1968 when Georges Charpak invented the multiwire proportional chamber, a gas-filled detector with parallel wires connected to amplifiers. Charpak’s chamber could count particles roughly 1,000 times faster than previous techniques, ushering in the modern electronic era of particle detectors.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

CERN next pioneered colliding-beam accelerators. In 1971, the Intersecting Storage Rings (ISR) began operation, creating the world’s first high-energy proton–proton collisions. The ISR missed the 1974 'November revolution' and also the bottom quark discovery in 1977 because of an initial lack of low-mass triggers, slow DAQ, and insufficient acceptance. However, its legacy enabled the next generation collider experiments: proton stacking, space charge compensation, beam diagnostics, stochastic cooling, van-der-Meer luminosity scans, importance of efficient triggers, and large-angle coverage... This success paved the way for the Super Proton Synchrotron (SPS). Approved in the early 1970s, the SPS was a 7 km circular machine straddling the Franco–Swiss border. It first circulated beam in 1976 and achieved roughly 400 GeV collision energy (above its original 300 GeV design). Later, by injecting antiprotons, CERN converted the SPS into a proton–antiproton collider. In this mode, the SPS led to the 1983 discovery of the W and Z bosons, a milestone in confirming the electroweak theory (for which Carlo Rubbia and Simon van der Meer won the Nobel Prize) \cite{WandZ}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Building on this progress, CERN inaugurated the Large Electron–Positron collider (LEP) in 1989. LEP was a 27 km tunnel (the largest circular collider then built) designed for high-precision studies of the electroweak interaction. In its first phase, it ran at about 100 GeV and produced on the order of 17 million Z bosons. LEP’s experiments performed very precise measurements; notably, they confirmed that there are exactly three light neutrino species (three generations of matter particles) \cite{LEPEW2006}. After roughly eleven years of operation, LEP was shut down in 2000 to make way for the next-generation collider using the same tunnel: the Large Hadron Collider.


\section{The Large Hadron Collider}

On September 18th, 2008, the first proton beam is successfully injected within the largest particle collider ever built, namely the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) \cite{EvansBryant2008}. This machine and its experiments are projects of superlatives: at that time, by comparison to the Tevatron, the direct particle accelerator "competitor" based at Fermilab and in operation since 1983, the LHC had a centre-of-mass energy collision seven times higher, a factor of ten in luminosity, and sixteen times narrower bunch spacing; all in one ring four times larger.

Detectors have been built as technological masterpieces, incorporating everything learned from previous experiments, and designed and optimised from scratch using detailed simulations. So far, their physics performance exceeds the design expectations.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The LHC, located at CERN on the Franco-Swiss border, is the world’s largest and most powerful particle accelerator. It represents the culmination of decades of progressive increases in accelerator energy at CERN. Its primary objective was the discovery of the Higgs boson --- the last missing element of the Standard Model (see section XXXX) --- a goal achieved in 2012 by the ATLAS \cite{ATLAS_Higgs} and CMS \cite{CMS_Higgs} collaborations. beyond this milestone, the LHC was conceived as a versatile tool to probe particle interactions at the TeV scale, with the capability to deliver proton-proton collisions at a centre-of-mass energy up to $\sqrt{s}=14$ TeV. Although this nominal value has not yet been reached, the machine currently operates at a maximum energy of 13.6 TeV. At such energies, the LHC provides a unique opportunity to test the limits of the Standard Model: both by conducting high-precision measurements that may reveal subtle deviations from theoretical predictions, and by carrying out direct searches for phenomena beyond the Standard Model (BSM) (some of which are discussed in chapters XXXX).

\subsection{Sub-accelerator complex}
\label{subsec:SubacceleratorComplex}

Technically, the LHC functions by accelerating and colliding two counter-rotating beams of protons --- or, alternatively, heavy ions --- at four dedicated interaction points (IP). The ring, buried about 100 m underground, extends over a circumference of 26.66 km, composed of eight 2.45-km-long curved arcs connected by eight straight sections, each one spanning roughly 545 m. Four of them host the major LHC experiments, while the remaining four accommodate essential machine infrastructure such as radio-frequency systems, collimators, beam dumps, and other utilities, as shown in figure \ref{fig-LHC-layout}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{Images/Ch1/LHC-layout.png}
    \caption{Illustration of the LHC geometry and experiments location \cite{LHC_layout}.}
    \label{fig-LHC-layout}
\end{figure}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The LHC ring is the final accelerator to a larger sub-accelerator complex. The production starts with a linear accelerator, the LINAC4, where protons are extracted from $\textrm{H}^{-}$ ions. Because of the weak bond between the proton and two electrons, the latter can be easily stripped from the ion by going through multiple layers of a carbon foil. At the end of this first stage, protons reach an energy of 160 MeV.

At this point, linear accelerators reach their limit; one could then consider a circular collider to profit from higher centre-of-mass energy collisions at the end, despite the use of magnetic dipoles with an increasing magnetic field to confine the beam within the pipe. The beams are then injected in the Proton Synchrotron Booster (PSB), before entering the PS and later the SPS, reaching the energy of 2 GeV, 26 GeV, and 450 GeV. The last step consists of preparing the two counterclockwise beams of the LHC by performing super-cycles to synchronise beam train insertion at the correct position. 

A schematic view of the CERN accelerator complex \cite{CernComplexAccelerator_ref} is shown in figure \ref{fig:CERNComplexAccelerator}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Once in the LHC, protons are accelerated to their nominal energy. For that, oscillating microwave fields generated by 16 superconducting radio-frequency cavities (8 per beam) housed in 4 cryo-modules, generate a longitudinal oscillating voltage in sync with the protons that keeps them tightly bunched and accelerates them with a field of 2 MV each (5 MV/m). Each cavity is driven by a high-power klystron, a tube containing electron beams, intensity-modulated to a frequency of 400 MHz. With that, protons receive 480 keV of energy per turn, or 5.3 GeV/s, during a 20-minute ramp-up period before reaching the nominal energy. 

The beams are confined in a low-pressure environment, around $10^{-13}$ atm, in order to mitigate beam losses resulting from collisions between the proton beam and unknown floating objects inside the beam pipe.

In each curved arc of the LHC, a magnetic field generated individually by the 1232 superconducting NbTi dipoles bends the beams toward the centre of the LHC ring. The intensity of the field goes from 0.54 to 8.33 T at the nominal as the beam accelerates. These magnets need to be cooled down to 1.9 K using superfluid helium to let the current of 11800 A pass through the magnet coils. This current value, and thus the bending power of dipole magnets, is so far the limitation of the higher kinetic energy of the proton beams. To focus the beam, the LHC uses quadrupole magnets (for a total of 858) that act like magnetic lenses and focus the beam, especially at the interaction points. However, their focusing strength depends on the particle energy. 688 sextupole magnets are therefore used to correct this energy dependence. Finally, 168 octupole magnets introduce controlled non-linear effects that help stabilise the beam against collective instabilities (Landau damping).

Protons, as well as charged particles, emit electromagnetic radiation, known as \textit{synchrotron radiation}, when they are accelerated along a curved trajectory. This energy loss per turn is scaled with the particle mass $m^{-4}$. With that, electrons lose energy about $10^{13}$ times faster than protons. This is one of the main reasons why protons have been chosen instead of electrons for the LHC. With a dipole field of 8.3 T, this corresponds to a loss of 18 keV/turn. To compensate for that, the RF cavities still operate, but to a lesser extent, under nominal conditions.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

At the nominal regime\footnote{Here for the LHC Run 3 campaign}, the LHC circulates 2808 bunches, which are groups of about 100 billion protons (at the start). Even if the capacity of the ring is 3556 bunches, only a fraction of them are filled with protons. Beams are then organised as trains (groups of bunches) separated by empty bunches. Every bunch location is time separated from its previous by 25 ns, corresponding to the nominal LHC frequency of 40 MHz, and rotates at 11245 revolutions per second with a velocity as close as 3 m/s to the speed of light. Note that at the top energy (6.8 TeV), the stored beam energy corresponds to about 350 MJ.

Because the number of protons per bunch is decreasing after each beam crossing, the intensity of the quadrupole magnets is adjusted with time to keep the bunches squeezed enough. 

In case of beam failure, the LHC Beam Dumping System is activated to safely eject both beams into 10-meter-long graphite blocks. The beam dumping procedure is also routinely conducted at the conclusion of each LHC filling: after about eight hours, the bunches are sufficiently low in protons that they cannot be concentrated any further, and after another two to four hours, the beam is dumped.

\subsection{LHC experiments}
\label{subsec:LHCExperiments}

The LHC hosts four main experiments, each designed with complementary goals and detection technologies. 

ATLAS (A Toroidal LHC ApparatuS) \cite{ATLAS_detector} and CMS (Compact Muon Solenoid) \cite{CMS_detector} are the two general-purpose detectors. Despite their different designs --- ATLAS being based on a large toroidal magnetic field and CMS on a high-field solenoidal magnet --- they share the common mission of probing a wide range of phenomena, from Standard Model precision measurements to searches for physics beyond the Standard Model. Both collaborations played a central role in the discovery of the Higgs boson in 2012 and continue to provide leading results in the search for new particles, such as supersymmetric states, dark matter candidates, or exotic resonances.

In contrast, LHCb (Large Hadron Collider beauty) \cite{LHCb_detector} focuses on the study of beauty and charm hadrons. Its forward geometry and high-precision vertex detector are optimised to measure rare decays, CP violation, and flavor-changing processes, which provide an indirect window into possible new physics contributions. Thanks to its unique design, LHCb has become a world-leading experiment in the field of flavor physics, testing the consistency of the Standard Model in the quark sector with unprecedented precision.

Finally, ALICE (A Large Ion Collider Experiment) \cite{ALICE_detector} is dedicated to the exploration of strongly interacting matter under extreme conditions. By colliding heavy ions such as lead nuclei, ALICE recreates in the laboratory the quark–gluon plasma, a state of matter believed to have existed microseconds after the Big Bang. The experiment investigates its properties ---such as collective flow, particle correlations, and jet quenching --- shedding light on the dynamics of the strong nuclear force in the high-density regime.

Together, these four experiments form a complementary research program: while ATLAS and CMS push the energy frontier with broad discovery potential, LHCb and ALICE exploit the LHC’s versatility to study the flavor structure of the Standard Model and the behavior of nuclear matter at extreme temperatures and densities.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

In addition to its four main experiments, the LHC hosts a set of smaller, highly specialised detectors that broaden its physics reach.

TOTEM (TOTal Elastic and diffractive cross section Measurement, located near CMS) is devoted to measure the total cross section of proton–proton interactions, elastic scattering, and diffraction processes. Its detectors, placed far from the interaction point in so-called "Roman pots", are capable of detecting protons scattered at very small angles, providing insights into the strong interaction at low momentum transfer. Complementary to this, LHCf (Large Hadron Collider forward, near ATLAS) investigates neutral particles produced at very small angles relative to the beamline. Its measurements are particularly valuable for calibrating models of cosmic-ray interactions in the atmosphere, thereby linking collider physics to astroparticle physics.

Another dedicated experiment, MoEDAL (Monopole and Exotics Detector at the LHC, that shares the cavern at Point 8 with LHCb), is designed to search for highly ionizing particles such as magnetic monopoles or other stable, massive exotic states predicted by certain extensions of the Standard Model.

More recently, new experiments have been installed to exploit the unique forward regions of the LHC. FASER (ForwArd Search ExpeRiment) is optimised to detect light and weakly interacting particles, such as dark photons or sterile neutrinos, which could be produced in large numbers along the beam direction but escape detection in central detectors. Similarly, SND@LHC (Scattering and Neutrino Detector at the LHC) is the first dedicated experiment at a collider to measure neutrinos produced in high-energy proton collisions, opening a new window into both neutrino physics and forward hadronic production.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

These complementary experiments significantly enrich the LHC physics program. While the flagship detectors ATLAS, CMS, LHCb, and ALICE address broad or well-defined research domains, the smaller-scale experiments target highly specific questions, from the structure of soft QCD interactions to the search for entirely new forms of matter.


\subsection{Collisions and Luminosity}
\label{subsec:CollisionsAndLuminosity}

The following subsections describe several key parameters commonly used in particle colliders, in particular the concept of luminosity --- which is directly related to the event rate --- and the number of simultaneous collisions occurring within a single event. Both parameters play a crucial role in accelerator physics. Although an event is technically defined as the result of a detector readout, here, it is often used interchangeably with the term collision.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

CMS and the LHC use \textit{instantaneous luminosity} $\mathscr{L}$ information to understand the collision environment during data-taking. The number of events $\partial N$ in a given period of time $\partial t$, knowing the process cross-section $\sigma$, is given by \cref{eq:nbrEvent}.

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial N}{\partial t} = \mathscr{L} \cdot \sigma
    \label{eq:nbrEvent}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Given the proton bunch structure of the LHC beams, and assuming gaussian beam profiles colliding heads on \cite{LumiComputation}, the instantaneous luminosity is calculated thanks to \cref{eq:instaneous_luminosity}.

\begin{equation}
    \mathscr{L} = \frac{N_p^2n_bf_{\textrm{rev}}\gamma}{4\pi\epsilon_n\beta^{*}} \cdot F
    \label{eq:instaneous_luminosity}
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $N_p$ is the number of particles per bunch,
    \item $n_b$ the number of bunches per beam,
    \item $f_{\textrm{rev}}$ the beam revolution frequency,
    \item $\gamma$ the relativistic factor,
    \item $\epsilon_n$ the normalised transverse emittance, that characterises the spread of the proton distribution in the transverse plane,
    \item $\beta^{*}$ the betatron function at the IP, that characterises the bunch envelop that oscillates around the design orbit due to the focusing provided by the magnetic field, assuming $\beta_x=\beta_y$ (the smaller the value of $\beta^{*}$, the tighter and the smaller the beam size is).
    \item $F$ is the geometric luminosity reduction factor due to the crossing angle at the IP, calculated using \cref{eq:FactorF}).
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
    F=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\left( \frac{\theta_{\textrm{IP}}\cdot\sigma_z}{2\sigma_{\textrm{IP}}} \right)^2}}
    \label{eq:FactorF}
\end{equation}

Where $\theta_\textrm{IP}$ is the the crossing angle at the IP, $\sigma_z$, the RMS bunch length along the proton beam and $\sigma_{\textrm{IP}}$ the transverse RMS beam size at the IP (assuming that $\sigma_x=\sigma_y$).

\vspace{0.5 cm}

As discussed in \ref{subsec:SubacceleratorComplex}, since collisions occur at a 40 MHz frequency, the number of protons per bunch decreases rapidly with time, following a exponential decay: $\mathscr{L}\propto e^{-t/\tau_0}$ with $\tau_0$ of the order of 10h at the LHC today.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Analysts use the \textit{integrated luminosity} L to measure cross sections of a process being studied, or to determine the number of events expected for physics processes with well-known cross sections. L is given by the \cref{eq:intlumi}.

\begin{equation}
    \textrm{L} = \int \mathscr{L} \, \mathrm{d}t
    \label{eq:intlumi}
\end{equation}

At the LHC, the integrated luminosity is usually defined in inverse femtobarn $\mathrm{fb}^{-1}$\footnote{$1 \, \mathrm{fb}^{-1} = 10^{-39} \, \mathrm{cm}^2$} to better compare with the magnitude of the cross sections of particle physics processes. A comparison of the integrated luminosity recorded by CMS over the years of data-taking is presented in \cref{fig:intlumi}. Summed together, the integrated luminosity from 2010 to 2025 is close to 475 $\mathrm{fb}^{-1}$.

\subsection{Pile-Up}
\label{subsec:PileUp}

Monitoring the real-time value of the instantaneous luminosity is tricky if using the \cref{eq:instaneous_luminosity}. This would require knowing the precise value of the parameters. An alternative is to monitor processes for which we know the cross-section of production $\sigma_{\textrm{ref}}$ and count how many of them are produced, which is the rate $\textrm{R}_{\textrm{ref}}$, calculated using \cref{eq:cross-section_ref}).

\begin{equation}
    \textrm{R}_{\textrm{ref}} = \mathscr{L} \cdot \sigma_{\textrm{ref}}
    \label{eq:cross-section_ref}
\end{equation}

At the LHC, the reference collision process is the proton inelastic scattering with $\sigma_{\textrm{inel}}=68.6 \, \pm 0.5 \, (\textrm{syst.}) \, \pm \, 3.2 \, (\textrm{lumi}) \, \textrm{mb}$ \cite{pileUPxsection}. As the rate is also defined as the number of collisions, one can also define it as in \cref{eq:pu_rate}.

\begin{equation}
    \textrm{R}_{\textrm{ref}} = \mu \cdot f_{\textrm{rev}}
    \label{eq:pu_rate}
\end{equation}

With $\mu$ the mean number of interactions per bunch crossing at the IP, namely the \textit{pile-up} (PU). Finally, the instantaneous luminosity is defined as in \cref{eq:lumi_measured}.

\begin{equation}
    \mathscr{L} = \frac{\mu \cdot f_{\textrm{rev}}}{\sigma_{\textrm{inel}}}
    \label{eq:lumi_measured}
\end{equation}

This leads to an average pile-up $\mu$ of about 22 under the LHC's nominal design condition. However, as the luminosity steadily increased over time by a factor of 2-2.5 today, this has resulted in a higher average pile-up of 50, with peaks reaching values over 80. The pile-up values measured by the CMS collaboration for the different years of data-taking between Run 1 and Run 3 are shown in \cref{fig:pileup}. Notice that the value of $\sigma_{\textrm{inel}}$ is different from the one given above; here, CMS is using the conventional value used for calculating the pileup.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{Images/Ch1/pileup.pdf}
    \caption{Distribution of the average number of interactions per crossing (Pile-Up) for proton-proton collisions per year \cite{LumiIntCMS}.}
    \label{fig:pileup}
\end{figure}

One can then notice that increasing the luminosity leads to an increase in the pile-up per event. This point is challenging for every detector: a higher number of collisions will lead to higher occupancy within the detector, which may then lead to a lower detection efficiency. To optimise the data acquisition and the amount of pile-up during a run, the instantaneous luminosity is then maintained around its optimised value through luminosity levelling \cite{LumiLevelling}. \cref{fig:lumiOMS} shows the recorded luminosity, the average pile-up and the two $\beta^{*}_{x,y}$ parameters during a proton-proton run. The latter decreases with time, assuring tighter and smaller bunches.

\subsection{Schedule}

The physics program at the LHC is divided into two primary phases: Phase--1 (2011-2026) and Phase--2 ( 2030-2041). 

So far, the Phase--1 program has been divided into 3 periods of data-taking, called \textit{Runs}, separated by \textit{Long Shutdowns} (LS), which are periods of upgrades and maintenance of the detectors. During a year, the proton-proton collisions program lasts for about 6 month, followed by heavy ion physics and then by a technical pause during the winter. Within a year, Runs are divided into \textit{Eras} to mark changes in experimental conditions, beam energy, and detector setups. Eras are then divided into \textit{runs} themselves, which are characterised by different fills of the beams.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Run 1 (2009-2013) marked the first completed operational phase of the LHC. During this period, CMS recorded 30 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$ of data at the centre-of-mass energy of 7 TeV (2010-2011, corresponding to 6.1 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$) and 8 TeV (2011-2013, corresponding to 23.3 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$) with a bunch spacing of 50 ns. These data led to the discovery of the Higgs boson by the CMS and ATLAS collaborations on July 4th, 2012 \cite{ATLAS_Higgs} \cite{CMS_Higgs}.

After the first LS that lasted 3 years, the superconducting magnets and detector systems have been upgraded to support higher luminosity. Run 2 (2015-2018) started with an enhancement of the centre-of-mass energy of 13 TeV and a bunch spacing of 25 ns. CMS then recorded an integrated luminosity of about 138 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$.

Run 3 (2022-2026) brought up the centre-of-mass energy to 13.6 TeV (still below the expected 14 TeV at the design of the LHC). So far, this period has been marked by a spectacular data collection campaign. CMS has recorded an integrated luminosity of respectively 38 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$, 30 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$, 113 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$ and 114 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$ from 2022 to 2025. Despite the ageing of the detector, the last six months of data-taking in 2026 are ongoing.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

At the end of Run 3, the transition for the High Luminosity LHC (HL--LHC) will start during the LS3, planned to last 4 years. With the large detector and accelerator upgrades \cite{HLLHC_TDR}, the expected instantaneous luminosity will be increased by a factor of 5 to 7.5 and the centre-of-mass energy to 14 TeV. Over the 12 years of planned operations, HL--LHC is expected to deliver a total of 3000-4000 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$.


\section{The Compact Muon Solenoid experiment}

Despite a less marketable name than its counterparts at the LHC, the Compact Muon Solenoid experiment \cite{CMS_detector} is remarkable in its design and programme that aims to cover a wide range of physics phenomena. CMS is located inside the 100-meter underground cavern at the IP5, north of the LHC ring. As the name suggests, every sub-part constituting this detector has been compacted in a cylinder 21 meters long and 15 meters in diameter for a total weight of 14500 tons (for a volume six times smaller and twice lighter than ATLAS). CMS has been specifically designed to highlight high-momentum muons, with muon chambers taking up 80\% of the detector space. Lastly, the solenoid magnet, which generates a magnetic field of 3.8 T, bends the trajectories of charged particles to accurately measure their momentum and electric charge. In the following, the CMS detector has been carefully dissected component by component, both from the perspective of their instrumentation and their software operation, with particular emphasis on the event reconstruction algorithms that bridge raw detector signals into meaningful physics observables.

\subsection{Coordinate system}

The CMS detector has adopted a right-handed Cartesian coordinate system with the collision point as its origin (in the centre of the detector). The x-axis points toward the LHC centre and the y-axis toward the surface, both forming a perpendicular plane to the beam pipe, known as the transverse plane. The z-axis represents the longitudinal axis and points toward the Jura mountains, or more physically speaking, in the counterclockwise direction of the LHC.

Due to its cylindrical detector shape, the partial spherical coordinate system is also used. The azimuthal angle $\phi$ is defined as the angle measured from the x-axis to the y-axis, the polar angle $\theta$ is measured from the z-axis, and the radial distance $r$ is defined in the transverse plane. In collider physics, and more generally in high energy physics, one can also define the \textit{pseudo-rapidity} $\eta$, deriving from taking the ultra-relativistic regime of the rapidity\footnote{The rapidity is defined as $\frac{1}{2}\ln\left( \frac{E+p_z}{E-p_z} \right)$ where $E$ is the energy of the particle and $p_z$ its longitudinal momentum. This quantity is zero when a particle is close to the transverse plane, but tends to $\pm\infty$ when a particle is moving close to the beam axis in either direction.} (when $pc\gg mc^2$), as defined in \cref{eq:pseudorapidity}.

\begin{equation}
    \eta = - \ln \left[ \tan \left( \frac{\theta}{2} \right) \right]
    \label{eq:pseudorapidity}
\end{equation}

Note that a particle located in the transverse plane has a zero pseudo-rapidity, and as the particle approaches the z-axis, that pseudo-rapidity tends toward infinity. This quantity is invariant under Lorentz boosts along the beam axis, meaning that the difference in pseudo-rapidity $\Delta \eta$ between two particles remains unchanged in all inertial frames related by longitudinal boosts.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

We can therefore define the \textit{angular distance} $\Delta R$ separating two particles $i$ and $j$ such as \cref{eq:deltaR}.

\begin{equation}
    \Delta R = \sqrt{{\Delta \eta_{ij}}^2 + {\Delta \phi_{ij}}^2}
    \label{eq:deltaR}
\end{equation}  

where $\Delta \eta_{ij} = | \eta_i - \eta_j|$ and $\Delta \phi_{ij} = | \phi_i - \phi_j|$ so that $\Delta R$ is also Lorentz invariant\footnote{$\phi=\arctan(p_y/p_x)$, a longitudinal boost along the z-axis does not affect the azimuthal angle.} for referential change along the beam axis direction. \cref{fig:coordinate} shows the different coordinates used in CMS.

\subsection{Detector dissection}

The CMS detector is organised as an onion in which sub-detectors are arranged in layers around the beam pipe, each one of them having a specific role in the reconstruction of physics events. A schematic view of the CMS detector is presented in \cref{fig:cmsdetector}. The cylindrical structure of the detector can be divided into two parts: the \textit{barrel} region (also referred to as the central region) and the two \textit{endcaps} (also referred to as the forward regions).

\subsubsection{Silicon Tracker}

The silicon tracker is the closest sub-detector to the IP. Its goal is to reconstruct bent charged particle trajectories --- called \textit{tracks} --- along with their momentum. As the charged particles, produced directly or not by the collisions, propagate through the tracker, they ionise the material. The generation of charges is gathered by silicon sensors. This technology provides excellent spatial resolution and radiation hardness, along with a low material budget to minimise the multiple scattering of charged particles. On top of that, silicon has two additional benefits compared to homologue tracking detectors such as drift chambers or time projection chambers: high density and low electron-hole creation energy, within the semiconductor material, enable thin sensors with sufficient signal; and high mobility of charge carriers in silicon provides fast charge collection ($\sim5$ ns for an electron).

The tracker is divided into two parts: the silicon pixel, the closer part to the beam pipe, and the silicon strip sub-detectors, above the latter. Each of them is made of multiple layers with different sensor geometries optimised based on the distance from the IP. The tracker covers a radial range of 2.9 to 110 cm and a wide range of pseudo-rapidity up to $|\eta|<2.5$ using the information available in both the barrel and the endcaps regions. This coverage is accomplished by the 18 concentric barrel layers and the 10 endcap disks.

The readout electronics are synchronised with the 25 ns LHC bunch crossing clock, allowing hits\footnote{defined as the charge deposited inside a group of pixels or strips that is collected and used to reconstruct tracks later} to be associated with their corresponding bunch crossing. To reduce thermal noise and mitigate radiation-induced damage, pixel sensors are cooled to $-10^{\circ}\textrm{C}$ and strip sensors to $-15^{\circ}\textrm{C}$. A layout of the CMS tracker is shown in \cref{fig:tracker}.

\noindent \textbf{Pixel Tracker}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

At the start of the LHC, the pixel detector was made of three barrel layers and two disks in each forward region. During the winter break in 2016-2017, the pixel detector was upgraded. The first one was a change in the pixel supports, with a shift from on-board to off-board electronics, in addition to a $\textrm{CO}_2$ cooling system implemented. Secondly, an additional barrel layer and endcap disk completed the pixel detector \cite{pixeldetector}. A layout of the pixel tracker is presented in \cref{fig:pixeltracker}.

The four barrel pixel modules (BPIX) cover radial distances from 2.9 to 16 cm from the IP, while the three forward disks (FPIX) are set between 29.1 and 51.6 cm from the centre longitudinally with a coverage up to $|\eta|<2.5$. The BPIX is made of 1184 modules while the 672 for the FPIX, with each module featuring a lattice of $160 \times 416$ pixels cells within a rectangular active area of $16.2 \times 64.8 \, \textrm{mm}^2$, with each cell measuring $100 \times 150 \, \mu\textrm{m}^2$ with a thickness of 285 $\mu\textrm{m}$. This fine granularity grants the pixel tracker to reach a global hit efficiency of about 99~\%, while offering hit resolutions of approximately 10 $\mu\textrm{m}$ and 25 $\mu\textrm{m}$ along the transverse and longitudinal directions.

This first part of the tracker ensures the reconstruction of the primary vertex --- the initial hard-scatter interaction point --- from the PU vertices. On top of that, it provides the identification of in-flight decays, such as those from B-hadrons, D-hadrons, or $\tau$ leptons, with the reconstruction of secondary vertices.

\noindent \textbf{Strip Tracker}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

As the particle flux decreases with the squared distance, a lower granularity detector can be used. With this, silicon micro-strip sensors are considered instead of pixel ones.

The silicon strip tracker is divided into four regions. Four layers of strip sensors in the Tracker Inner Barrel (TIB) and six in the Tracker Outer Barrel (TOB), both of them being in the central region. The barrel region is closed by three disks in the Tracker Inner Disk (TID) and nine disks in the Tracker End Cap (TEC) in each forward region. With so, the silicon strip tracker covers radial distances from 20 to 110 cm and a longitudinal distance up to 282 cm, spanning a pseudo-rapidity range up to $|\eta|<2.5$ \cite{CMStracker}.

In total, 15148 modules fill the volume of the silicon strip detector. Some of them contain either one (mono) or two (stereo) silicon sensors, shown respectively in red and blue on \cref{fig:tracker}, for a total of 24244 sensors. Stereo modules are mounted back-to-back, with the second sensor being tilted by a 100 mrad angle to reconstruct three-dimensional information of the hits. TIB and TOB parts are made of concentric cylinders, with modules being parallel to the beam axis, in order to ensure a good $r-\phi$ measurement. Meanwhile, TID and TEC are rings where the sensors are in the transverse plane, resulting in a better z$-\phi$ resolution in the forward region.

A summary of the different geometries is given in \cref{tab:tracker_modules} in Appendix \ref{app:annexeA}.


\subsubsection{Calorimeters}

While the tracker is essential for reconstructing charged particle trajectories, it alone is insufficient for particle identification. Furthermore, neutral particles leave no signal in the silicon sensors and therefore escape tracker detection entirely. To complete the tracker, two calorimeter systems are mounted above the latter: an electromagnetic calorimeter (ECAL) \cite{CMSecal} and a hadronic calorimeter (HCAL) \cite{CMShcal}, both designed to measure the energy of incident particles. They consist of dense materials chosen and arranged to force the incoming particles to decay through a cascade of secondary particles of lower energy, called a \textit{particle shower}. 

The ECAL has been designed to stop electromagnetic showers resulting from electrons and photons losing their energy due to interaction with matter. This energy loss is caused by processes such as Bremsstrahlung (photon produced by the deceleration of a charged particle) and electron-positron pair production (where photons are converted to electron-positron pairs). As the shower progresses inside the ECAL, additional secondary particles are produced, leading to the creation of low-energetic photons. Thus, the total collected energy corresponds to the energy of the incoming particle.

The HCAL, placed above the ECAL, targets hadronic showers. Such showers are driven by the strong nuclear force and result from charged or neutral hadron interactions, leading to the creation and decay of secondary hadrons. The latter ionise the material as they propagate and generate photons that are then collected by the scintillators. A hadronic shower consists of: $\sim50\%$ electromagnetic energy (e.g., neutral pion decaying into two photons), $\sim25\%$ visible non-electromagnetic energy (e.g., from charged pions, kaons, or muons), $\sim25\%$ invisible energy (nuclear fission, excitation, neutrons), $\sim2\%$ and escaped energy.

Both calorimeters are segmented in the transverse plane to increase the accuracy of the energy and trajectory of incoming particles.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\noindent \textbf{Electromagnetic calorimeter}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The ECAL is divided into three sections: the Barrel ECAL (EB), covering a pseudo-rapidity range $|\eta|<1.479$, the ECAL Endcap (EE) that extends the coverage to $|\eta|<3.0$, and the ECAL Preshower system (ES) located at the entry of the EE. A Layout of the ECAL sub-detector is presented in \cref{fig:ECAL}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{Images/Ch1/ECALgloballayout.pdf}
    \caption{Schematic view of the CMS ECAL, showing the barrel region composed of lead tungstate crystals, and the two endcap regions equipped with supercrystals \cite{CMSecalphoto}.}
    \label{fig:ECAL}
\end{figure}

The calorimeter is homogeneously populated with lead tungstate ($\textrm{PbWO}_4$) scintillating crystals. This material of a density of $8.28 \, \textrm{g}/\textrm{cm}^3$ provides a small radiation length\footnote{Denoted by $X_0$, this is the mean distance a high energetic electron travels before losing $e^{-1}=0.37$ of its energy by bremsstrahlung. At first order, $X_0 \sim 1433 \cdot \frac{A}{Z(Z+1)(11.319-\ln(Z))}$ where $Z$ and $A$ are respectively the atomic number and the mass number of the nucleus.} ($X_0=0.89$ cm) as well as small Molière radius\footnote{Radius of the cone containing 90 \% of the shower energy. One can derive it using the radiation length: $R_M = 0.0265 \cdot X_0 \cdot (Z+1.2)$.} ($R_M=2.19$ cm) but also a natural tolerance to radiation. Lead tungstate has a low scintillation time, collecting 80 \% of the light within the 25 ns time between two bunch crossings. On the other hand, lead tungstate has a relatively low light yield (a few photons per MeV), which implies the use of photo-detectors with an internal amplification factor of 50 (10) in the barrel (endcap) region.

A total of 75848 crystals compose the ECAL, with 61200 in the barrel (length of $25.8 X_0\approx23$ cm) and $2 \times 7324$ in the two endcaps (length of $27 X_0 \approx 24$ cm). The front face cross-section of the crystals corresponds to a granularity of $\Delta\eta\times\Delta\phi = 0.0175 \times 0.0175$ (giving a surface of $2.2 \times 2.2 \, \textrm{cm}^2$ for the barrel crystals and $2.86 \times 2.86 \, \textrm{cm}^2$ for the endcaps ones) \cite{CMSecalcrystals}. With such values, electromagnetic showers are restrained into the crystals; thus, no secondary particles are lost in passive absorber materials. On the other hand, the absence of longitudinal segmentation in the CMS ECAL limits direct access to shower depth information, reducing intrinsic electromagnetic/hadronic discrimination power and particle identification capabilities compared to sampling calorimeters with longitudinal segmentation. As illustrated in \cref{fig:ECAL}, the crystals are tilted in the direction of the IP to prevent photons from entering adjacent crystals and escaping detection. 

The ES does not contain any crystals but rather two layers of lead absorber with layers of silicon strip sensors used to distinguish single photons from photons coming from the decay of neutral pions.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Despite the distance from the IP, the transparency of the crystals is affected with time (and radiation), changing the ECAL energy resolution. Calibration on the transparency of the crystals is performed using lasers, where the light is injected when there is no bunch. The energy resolution of the ECAL, $\sigma_{\textrm{ECAL}}$, has been measured in 7 TeV data \cite{CMSecal}, and given by \cref{eq:ecalresolution}.

\begin{equation}
    \left( \frac{\sigma_{\textrm{ECAL}}}{E} \right)^2 = \left( \frac{0.028}{\sqrt{E}} \right)^2 + \left( \frac{41.5 \, \textrm{MeV}}{E} \right)^2 + 0.003^2
    \label{eq:ecalresolution}
\end{equation}

with $E$ the photon or electron energy. The ECAL energy resolution is the quadratic sum of three terms: a stochastic term ($\propto 1/\sqrt{E}$), caused by statistical fluctuations in shower development and photon collection; a noise term ($\propto 1/E$), due to electronic noise and pileup; and a constant term, arising from calibration uncertainties and detector non-uniformities.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\noindent \textbf{Hadron calorimeter}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The HCAL is segmented into four parts, as depicted in \cref{fig:CMShcal}: the Barrel (HB) and the Endcap (HE), which cover a pseudo-rapidity range up to $|\eta|<3$; the Outer barrel (HO) and the Forward (HF). The latter extends the coverage to $|\eta|<5$ and situated at $|z|=11.1 \, \textrm{m}$. The HO is sitting above the magnet to contain the most energetic particle showers that would have passed through the EB and HB.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{Images/Ch1/CMShcalpicture.pdf}
    \caption{Longitudinal layout of one quarter of the CMS HCAL, showing the location of the HCAL barrel (HB), endcap (HE), outer barrel (HO) and forward (HF) calorimers \cite{CMShcal}.}
    \label{fig:CMShcal}
\end{figure}

The HB and HE are made with interspersed brass absorbers\footnote{A curious fact: some of the brass used comes from dismantled Russian World War II artillery shells.} (70\% copper and 30 \% zinc) and plastic scintillators. The absorber material has a small nuclear interaction length\footnote{mean free path of hadrons between strong collisions} ($\lambda_I \sim 16.4$ cm), so HCAL remains compact while stopping the maximum of hadronic showers. The plastic scintillator is motivated by its fast response (within 1 ns). The HO is composed of this plastic scintillator layer between the magnet's iron flux return yokes, which serve as absorber material. Finally, the HF used hard quartz fibres, alternated with steel absorber layers, instead of the plastic scintillator to deal with the higher flux of energetic particles near the beam axis. In such fibres, ionising particles release their energy in the form of Cherenkov radiation, read out by photo-multiplier tubes \cite{CMShcal}. Note that in most materials the nuclear interaction length $\lambda_I$ is much larger than the radiation length $X_0$. As a result, electromagnetic showers develop over a much shorter distance scale than hadronic showers. This allows a compact electromagnetic calorimeter to be placed upstream, where electrons and photons are fully contained, while hadrons, which interact over the larger scale $\lambda_I$, predominantly shower in the downstream hadronic calorimeter.

Technically speaking, the HCAL granularity, as the ECAL's one, is defined by the segmentation of the wedges $\Delta \eta \times \Delta \phi$. The value depends on the part of the calorimeter: $0.087 \times 0.087$ (corresponding to a $5 \times 5$ array of ECAL crystals) for the HB and HO, $0.17 \times 0.17$ for the HB, and $0.175 \times 0.175$ for the HF.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Similarly to the ECAL, the relative energy resolution for the HCAL, $\sigma_{\textrm{HCAL}}$, is given by \cref{eq:hcalresolution} \cite{CMShcalcrystals}. The energy resolution of a hadronic calorimeter is significantly affected by invisible energy losses, arising from nuclear breakup, neutron production, nuclear excitation, and binding energy losses. Since this fraction of the shower energy does not generate detectable ionisation signals, it introduces intrinsic event-by-event fluctuations that degrade the calorimetric energy resolution.

\begin{equation}
    \left ( \frac{\sigma_{\textrm{HCAL}}}{E} \right)^2 = \left( \frac{0.943}{\sqrt{E}} \right)^2 + 0.084^2
    \label{eq:hcalresolution}
\end{equation}


\subsubsection{Solenoid magnet}

Charged particles with electric charge $q$ and velocity $\overrightarrow{v}$ bend when exposed to a magnetic field $\overrightarrow{B}$ according to the Lorentz force $\overrightarrow{F}=q\left( \overrightarrow{v} \times \overrightarrow{B}\right)$. This property is used by particle physicists in order to perform track reconstruction of such particles. 

Thus, the CMS solenoid is a central component of the CMS detector. The magnet consists of a 12.5 m long superconducting coil with an external diameter of 6 m that produces a quasi-uniform magnetic field of 3.8 T along the z-axis within its volume. The coil itself is approximately 60 cm thick, and in contrast to ATLAS, where the solenoid is located in front of the calorimeters, the CMS design places the magnet outside the calorimetric system. This configuration avoids additional material upstream of the ECAL and HCAL, thereby preserving the intrinsic calorimetric response and improving the overall reconstruction performance for electromagnetic objects and jets \cite{CMSmagnet}. The magnetic field variation map is shown in \cref{fig:CMSmagnet}, used for precise momentum reconstruction.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth,trim={0 0 7cm 0},clip]{Images/Ch1/magnet.png}
    \caption{Colour map of the magnetic field $|B|$ generated by the solenoid magnet and the corresponding magnetic field lines for a longitudinal section of CMS \cite{CMSmagnet}.}
    \label{fig:CMSmagnet}
\end{figure}

The magnet itself is made of a metal alloy of Nobium-Titanium (NbTi). To reach the superconductivity regime, the magnet is cooled to 4.1 K by a liquid helium cryostat. Surrounding the solenoid, the iron return yoke\footnote{Interestingly, the magnet and the iron return yoke account for approximately 12500T, or 89\% of the total mass of the CMS detector.} closes the magnetic flux and provides an approximately 2 T magnetic field within the muon detection system. The field strength and direction vary across the different detector regions due to the geometry of the yoke and the magnetic flux return path.


\subsubsection{Muon chambers}

So far, Standard Model particles are stopped by the calorimeter system of CMS, but one exception remains: the muon. Such particles are frequently produced in various physical processes; thus, it is imperative to accurately measure their properties. With a mean lifetime of 2.2 $\mu\textrm{m}$ ($\sim650$ m in the particle frame), the particle is considered stable at the detector scale, meaning the muon goes through the entire sub-detector system. Moreover, because of their large mass (200 times heavier than an electron), they exhibit less bremsstrahlung, resulting in reduced energy loss along their path. Finally, although they are leaving hits within the tracker, muons cannot be identified directly using this information.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

With all of that, the muon system , or the so-called muon chambers, is the last sub-detector covering CMS \cite{CMSmuonsystem}. It was designed to have three functions: efficient muon identification, precise momentum measurement, and triggering capability. These functions are enabled by the high-field solenoidal magnet and flux-return yoke. Muon chambers are located outside the solenoid coil and the HO calorimeter; they are interspaced between the iron plates forming the return yoke of the magnet. A layout of the muon system is shown in \cref{fig:muonsystem}. It is divided into three subsystems with the Drift Tubes (DTs) in the barrel region, the Cathode Strip Chambers (CSCs) in the forward region, and completed with the Resistive Plate Chambers (RPCs). The Muon Barrel (MB) is made of 5 wheels along the beam pipe, each one divided into twelve 30° segments to cover the volume of the CMS detector. While the Muon Endcap (ME) is divided into rings in the transverse plane.

Note that in 2017, an additional system was added in the inner endcap, namely the Gas Electron Multiplier system (GEM), to gain operational experience and perform a preliminary investigation of detector performance for the HL--LHC program \cite{CMSmuonsystem}.

    \caption{Schematic view of a quarter of the longitudinal view of the CMS muon chambers. DTs (yellow), CSCs (green), RPCs (blue) are shown. In addition to the first Gas Electron Multiplier system (red) in the forward region \cite{CMSmuonsystem}.}

Because of a lower track rate compared to the inner tracker, less granular and more economical gas detectors are used. The muon system is subdivided into three parts, each one based on a different technology. Nevertheless, still able to cover large areas with a reasonable response time.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\noindent \textbf{Drift tubes (DTs)}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

As the muon rate is lower in the barrel region, drift tube technology has been chosen to cover it (up to $|\eta|<1.2$). DTs are organised into four concentric cylinders composed of drift chambers (60 for each of the first three layers and 70 for the outermost one). Drift chambers consist of rectangular drift cells of $44 \times 13 \, \textrm{mm}^2$ cross section and 2 to 3 meters long. They contain a 50 $\mu \textrm{m}$ gold-plated stainless-steel anode wire at the centre and are filled with a gas mixture containing 85\% Ar and 15\% $\textrm{CO}_2$ that is ionised when a muon traverses this volume. The resulting charged particles drift under the applied electric field toward the electrodes, where they are amplified to produce an electrical signal. The drift time of charges allows for a transversal measurement with respect to the wire, with a resolution of about 2.6 ns. To complete the information of one muon hit, multiple drift chambers are grouped together to form 4 layers of parallel cells --- called a superlayer (SL) ---, then a chamber made of 3 SLs : two with the cells parallel to the beam axis and one orthogonal. This improves the resolution in $r-\phi$ and $r-$z coordinates, giving spatial resolution of 100 $\mu \textrm{m}$ and 500 $\mu \textrm{m}$ respectively. A drift cell, a drift chamber, and superlayers of drift chambers are depicted in \cref{fig:DTs}.

\noindent \textbf{Cathode Strip Chambers (CSCs)}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Located in the endcap, where the particle flux is higher, the CSCs are dedicated detectors that address these conditions by having a faster response time. A CSC module consists of a trapezoidal chamber formed by 7 layers of cathode strip planes. Between each layer, the gap is filled with a mixture of $\textrm{CO}_2$ (50\%), Ar (40\%), and $\textrm{CF}_4$ (40\%) that is ionised by energetic muons crossing the chamber. As in a drift chamber, an electric field is generated between the edges and the 50 $\mu$m wire anode in the middle of the chambers. Each wire is spaced by ($\sim$3.15 mm) for a total of 5--16 wires per layer, allowing a precise measurement in the $r-\phi$ plane with a response time of about 30 ns and a time resolution of about 2 ns. After all, 540 CSCs composed the four wheels (themself made up of three rings in the first wheel and two for the others) for a pseudo-rapidity covering of $0.9<|\eta|<2.4$ \cite{CSCs}.

\noindent \textbf{Resistive Plate Chambers (RPCs)}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

RPCs design is made to address the problematic of a very fast response time to keep with the 25 ns bunch crossing rate. Their detection principle is similar to the CSCs' one, a gas mixture (95.2~\% $\textrm{C}_2\textrm{H}_2\textrm{F}_4$, 4.5~\% isobutane $\textrm{C}_4\textrm{H}_{10}$ and 0.3~\% $\textrm{SF}_6$) in a 2 mm gap is surrounded by parallel laminated bakelite plates. This offers a time resolution of about 3 ns at the cost of poorer spatial resolution. The system is composed of six wheels mounted with DTs in the MB and four in each endcap between CSC modules, summing up to $480+576$ chambers \cite{RPCs}. \subsection{Trigger system}

In the LHC design, bunch crossing occurs every 25 ns. Assuming that an event can be stored in 1 MB of data, this would correspond to 40 TB/s to save. This rate is beyond the CMS Data Acquisition System (DAQ) capacity, which records, processes, and stores events at a rate of nearly 100 MB/s. In addition, a fraction of collisions comes from low-energy proton-proton interactions, resulting in poor physics events that do not have to be saved. To cope with the bunch crossing rate, a two-level triggering system and a computer farm have been adopted by the CMS DAQ \cite{CMStrigger}. 

The trigger system provides a quick selection of interesting events, reducing the 40 MHz input rate as low as 1kHz. The latter is divided into two levels: a first one purely \textit{hardware}, built by a dedicated custom-designed electronics system, and a second one \textit{software} based.

As pile-up increases, so does the particle flux. Since most physics processes of interest have a significantly lower cross section than common QCD background processes, prescales are applied to the corresponding triggers in order to select only a fraction of these high-rate events, primarily for calibration and monitoring purposes.

\subsubsection{The Level--1 trigger}

The Level--1 trigger (L1) functions as a hardware system with a finite latency of 3.8 $\mu$s. The fixed latency is set by the number of steps plus data transfer times. Within this time, based on the presence of signals from the calorimeters and the muon chambers, the L1 system assesses an event and decides to accept or reject it.

L1 trigger must deliver a decision every bunch crossing, despite a much longer latency. A pipeline decision is therefore built, as illustrated in \cref{fig:L1pipeline}. Raw data are continuously stored in a front-end pipeline memory of approximately 100 bunch crossings (corresponding to the latency of 3.8 $\mu$s). Upon a trigger decision, data are either accepted and forwarded to a derandomiser buffer or rejected. The derandomiser converts the random arrival of accepted events into a steady readout stream. Two sources of dead-time must be controlled: a simple dead-time of 1~\% arising from a 4 BC veto after each L1 accept, and a complex dead-time dependent on the derandomiser occupancy, required to remain below 1~\% at 75 kHz and 6~\% at 100 kHz trigger rate \cite{CMStrigger}.

After all, event rates are reduced to 100 kHz and are then transferred to the HLT for further filtering.

\subsubsection{The High-Level trigger}

The High-Level Trigger (HLT) is a software-based selection system using CPUs and GPUs to cope with the increased luminosity. With a latency of 90 ms per event, it reduces physics rate from L1 input of 100 kHz to a few kHz. Each event undergoes object reconstruction for entities like electrons, muons, photons, taus, or jets. To do so, data processing is done by a set of algorithms --- HLT path ---, each with a dedicated physics reconstruction that preserves topologies of interest. They are designed as a succession of reconstruction steps and filters, such that an event that would not pass a step does not go through all of them, hence keeping the HLT decision flow optimised \cite{CMShlt}.

Monitoring the output rates of the L1 trigger and HLT is essential for assessing the overall performance of the trigger system, as it directly determines the data recorded for physics analyses. During data-taking, a dedicated shift crew oversees the smooth operation of the experiment, with each member assigned specific responsibilities. In particular, the trigger shifter monitors trigger rates in real-time and adjusts prescales accordingly to maintain a stable output rate and prevent the data acquisition system from being overloaded. Since the start of Run 3, I have personally contributed to this effort by completing 27 shifts of 8 hours each, dedicated to trigger system monitoring.

\subsubsection{Data storage}

Once events of interest have been assessed by the trigger system, they are encoded in a raw data format locally on disk. The data recorded by CMS is then distributed, stored, and analysed across a hierarchical computing infrastructure known as the Worldwide LHC Computing Grid (WLCG) \cite{WLCG} among the CERN institutes, split into different tiers. Together, it combines about 1.4 million computer cores and 1.5 exabytes\footnote{$1 \, \textrm{EB} = 10^6 \,\, \textrm{TB}$} of storage.

At the top of this hierarchy, Tier--0, located at CERN, is responsible for the initial backup of raw data as well as the first calibration and reconstruction pass. It provides 20\% of the WLCG resources with its 800 PB of data storage. The processed data is then replicated to a set of Tier--1 centres, which provide long-term storage on tape and handle reprocessing campaigns as well as all types of data processing. Tier--2 centres, typically hosted at universities and research institutes, also perform all types of processing and serve as a resource for Monte Carlo simulation production. Finally, Tier--3 facilities, generally local computing clusters within research groups, are dedicated exclusively to physics analysis.


\section{Event reconstruction, identification and calibration}

Among the various particles produced at each proton-proton collision, only a few of them are stable at the detector scale. The most common are the muons $\mu^\pm$, the electrons $\textrm{e}^\pm$, the charged hadrons (proton p, pions $\pi^\pm$, kaons $\textrm{K}^\pm$), the neutral ones (neutron n, neutral pion $\pi^0$), and the photons $\gamma$. Given different physics interactions, they leave different experimental signatures within the CMS detector. A representation of various particles travelling inside CMS is shown on \cref{fig:CMSslicePF}.

The CMS Particle Flow (PF) algorithm performs a complete reconstruction of events. This algorithm, detailed in the section below, reconstructs the basic particles listed above by looking for tracker hits, energy deposits within the calorimeters, or muon chamber hits. Afterward, the latter are used to reconstruct more complex objects such as particle \textit{jets}, \textit{missing energy} in the transverse plane (MET).

\begin{figure} [h]

\subsection{Track reconstruction}

The reconstruction of particles inside CMS entails knowing the particle's trajectory and momentum. Thus, tracks and primary vertices reconstruction is a primordial step in particle identification. A Combinatorial Track Finder (CTF) has been developed by the collaboration \cite{CTF_track_cms} \cite{CERN_CTF} to achieve that. It relies on an iterative Kalman Filter (KF) \cite{KF} applied to the tracker and muon chamber hits.

Thanks to the magnetic field $B_z$ along the beam axis, particle trajectories are bent and described as helices that propagate toward the beam pipe. One can derive the expression of the transverse momentum $p_T$ of a particle with an algebraic charge $z_e$ moving in such a field, knowing the radius of curvature of the helix $\mathcal{R}_\textrm{C}$. Given by \cref{eq:ptreconstruction}.

\begin{align}
    p_T \, [\textrm{GeV/c}] = 0.3 \cdot z_e \cdot B_z \, [\textrm{T}] \cdot \mathcal{R}_\textrm{C} \, [\textrm{m}]
    \label{eq:ptreconstruction}
\end{align}

Despite being elegant, strong assumptions are made by assuming particles moving in a uniform magnetic field in the vacuum, without losing energy within the matter. The multi-step CTF considers multiple hits of information within the tracker to reduce the number of \textit{fake tracks}, which are wrong reconstructions of trajectories, while keeping a high tracking efficiency. This is done in 5 main steps:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Hit reconstruction
    \item Seed generation
    \item Iterative track reconstruction
    \item Ambiguity resolution
    \item Final track fit
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{Hit reconstruction}

Hit measurement relies on energy deposits of charged particles within the matter; the physics process of interaction between particles and matter is more detailed in Chapter XXX. These energy deposits or \textit{clusters} are grouped together if the signal-over-noise ratio ($S/N$) is above a given threshold after amplification and \textit{zero suppression}\footnote{Zero suppression is a data reduction technique in which only channels with signals above a predefined threshold are recorded, while channels consistent with noise are discarded.} steps. Strip clusters are built if $S/N>3$ for a single strip, then if nearby strips have $S/N>2$, they are grouped, and a strip cluster is labelled if and only if the $S/N$ of the cluster is five times greater than the square root of the sum of the RMS-noise of each individual strip inside it.

A similar algorithm is applied for pixel hits. A cluster is based on a pixel cell having $S/N>6$, and adjacent pixels are grouped if they satisfy $S/N>5$. Globally, the pixel cluster shall comply with $S/N>10.1$. 

After all, the pixel hit resolution at the end of Run--3 is about $40 \, \mu\textrm{m} \, \times \, 75 \, \mu\textrm{m}$ in the $r-\phi$ and $z$ coordinates respectively \cite{pixelHitresolution}. While varying from 15 $\mu$m to 40 $\mu$m for the $z$ coordinate on strip modules, depending on the strip pitch \cite{stripHitresolution}.

Those values of $S/N$ threshold evolve with time, decreasing with respect to the integrated luminosity due to radiation damage \cite{stripHitresolution}.

\subsubsection{Seed generation}

Once the clusterisation is done, the origin of the track must be found. The track \textit{seeding} consists of grouping adjacent hits using every combination of two hits, mostly pixel hits because of their close distance from the IP. The pairs are then iteratively propagated outward to groups of three to four hits by applying KF. 

\subsubsection{Iterative track reconstruction}

Once the origin of primary tracks is done, the CTF algorithm predicts the next hit during the iterative process. Even if \ref{eq:ptreconstruction} is not the one used, similar formulas with fewer hypotheses are implemented to interpolate the track parameter while measuring the sagitta\footnote{The sagitta of a circular arc is the distance from the midpoint of the arc to the midpoint of its chord.} of the trajectory in the transverse plane and the orientation of the helix. Track information is continuously updated at each newly added hit up to the last tracker layer, or if there are no further hits. Thus, imposing constraints on the primary vertex reconstruction by reversing the process. 

\subsubsection{Ambiguity resolution}

Ambiguities in the track finding process arise when a seed gives more than one track candidate or if a track belongs to multiple seeds. To counter that, the CTF process evaluates the shared hits fraction of nearby tracks. If this ratio is above a given threshold, the track with the least number of hits is discarded, or, in case of the same total hit number, the track with the highest $\chi^2$ is rejected \cite{CTF_track_cms}.

\subsubsection{Final track fit}

Once the procedure is complete, fits are performed, and spurious hits are removed, both are done iteratively until a certain level of convergence is reached. Final track candidates are then labelled based on the number of hits, the track compatibility with each hit, the $\chi^2$ score of the track fit, or the distance between the track and the associated primary vertex --- namely the \textit{impact parameter}. After all, tracks are classified as \textit{loose}, \textit{tight}, or \textit{HighPurity}, with each consecutive label being a subset of the previous one, with more stringent criteria.

This final step is also crucial for interaction vertices labelisation. The fitted tracks are extrapolated to the beam spot and clustered in space to reconstruct proton–proton interaction vertices. Among them, the primary vertex (PV) associated with the hard-scattering process is defined as the one with the largest scalar sum of squared transverse momenta of its associated tracks ($\sum p_T^2$). Secondary vertices are also reconstructed at this stage and can be identified as originating from the decays of long-lived particles such as $b$--hadrons or $\tau$--leptons. For Run--3 data, the PV transverse and longitudinal resolution is better than $14 \, \mu\textrm{m} \, \times 19 \, \mu\textrm{m}$ \cite{Run3_pv_resolution}.

\subsubsection{Tracking during Run--3 data-taking}

CMS has developed new tools and algorithms to handle the higher integrated luminosity. Every step listed above has been slightly improved to compensate for the tracker radiation damage. Due to higher pile-up interactions, the fake track rate has become a problem, reducing the effectiveness of the CTF algorithm. Thus, mkFit has been developed to improve track pattern recognition, decreasing the track-building time \cite{mkfit}. Moreover, the BDT strategy\footnote{A Boosted Decision Tree (BDT) is a machine learning algorithm that combines multiple decision trees sequentially, where each new tree is trained to correct the errors of the previous ones, improving overall classification performance.} adopted for Run--2 is also replaced by a Deep Neural Network (DNN) to support the reduction of the fake rate. \subsubsection{Electron track reconstruction}

As discussed, electrons suffer from bremsstrahlung effects along their curved trajectory, resulting in higher energy loss. The Kalman Filter algorithm assumes Gaussian-like uncertainties, which is not valid for electrons due to the emission of high-energy photons. Therefore, after the seeding step, a Gaussian Sum Filter (GSF) algorithm is applied to properly account for sudden changes in the track trajectory and large energy losses in the detector material. This step-by-step procedure models the bremsstrahlung energy loss as a mixture of Gaussian components, which improves predictions regarding the shape and position of clusters.

\subsubsection{Muon track reconstruction}
\label{subsubsection:muon_track}

As muon particles go through the entire detector, the muon tracking makes use of the inner tracker and the muon chamber information. Similarly, KF are used to build seeds within the DTs and CSCs layers. Therefore, a second group of tracks arises after the seeding in the muon sub-systems, in addition to inner tracker tracks.

Tracks reconstructed exclusively using information from the muon chambers are referred to as \textit{standalone muons}. 

In contrast, candidates reconstructed solely in the tracker are labelled as \textit{tracker muons}. These originate from tracker tracks that are extrapolated to the muon system and loosely matched to DT and CSC segments. This approach provides the reconstruction of low transverse momentum muons, which are more likely to reach and match only the innermost muon station rather than multiple stations.

\textit{Global muons}, on the other hand, are reconstructed by matching tracker and standalone muon tracks. The inner tracker track is extrapolated to the muon system and combined with a corresponding standalone muon candidate. This category is particularly relevant for muons with transverse momentum ($p_T > 200$ GeV/$c$), as it combines information from both tracking systems to improve the momentum measurement.

This classification is useful for distinguishing between cosmic muons induced by cosmic rays interacting with the Earth's atmosphere and muons originating from collision events. This is specifically depicted in \cref{fig:StandaloneANDGlobalmuon}.

\subsection{Particle-Flow algorithm}

The Particle-Flow algorithm (PF) aims to reconstruct particles and objects from an event by combining information from the tracker, calorimeters, and muon chambers. This is the final step applied to raw data before being saved for offline analysis. The PF algorithm uses all low-level reconstructed objects from the sub-detectors to correlate measurements and, therefore, label particles along with their kinematic properties. Once a PF candidate is fully reconstructed, it is removed from the chain to reconstruct other PF elements.

\subsubsection{Muons}

Thanks to the muon track procedure described in \ref{subsubsection:muon_track}, CMS achieves a muon reconstruction efficiency greater than 99~\% for tracker muons or global muons \cite{MuonreconstructionCMS}. If their $p_T$ is below 200 GeV/$c$, the transverse momentum measurement relies mostly on the inner track fit, while for higher $p_T$, the global fit performed by the Tracker Plus First Muon Station (TPFMS) allows a better measurement \cite{MuonFitalgorithm}. A standard approach for offline muon momentum reconstruction is the Tune P algorithm~\cite{MuonreconstructionCMS}, which dynamically selects the most appropriate track fit depending on the available inner tracker and muon chamber information.

The track compatibility with one of three muon categories (tracker, standalone, or global muons) is assessed by looking at the $\chi^2$ score of the track fit and the number of hits. As for inner tracker tracks, muon tracks are also classified into different working points: \textit{loose}, \textit{medium}, or \textit{tight} muons, with each consecutive label being a subset of the previous one, with more stringent criteria.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The PF algorithm also provides dedicated isolation criteria for the muon reconstruction.

The relative particle-flow isolation $I_{\textrm{rel}}^\mu$ quantifies the amount of hadronic and electromagnetic activity in a cone of radius $\Delta R<0.4$ around the muon direction, normalised by the muon transverse momentum ${p_T}^\mu$. Its expression is given by \cref{eq:PFisoMu}).

\begin{equation}
    I_{\textrm{rel}}^\mu = \frac{1}{{p_T}^\mu} \left[
        \sum\limits_{\substack{\textrm{ch.} \\ \textrm{had.}}} p_T 
        + \max \left( 0 , 
            \sum\limits_{\substack{\textrm{neu.} \\ \textrm{had.}}} E_T 
            + \sum\limits_{\textrm{ph.}} E_T 
            - 
            0.5 \cdot \sum\limits_{\substack{\textrm{ch. had.} \\ \textrm{PU}}} p_T 
        \right) \right]
    \label{eq:PFisoMu}
\end{equation}

The numerator sums the transverse momenta of all charged hadrons, plus a pileup-corrected neutral contribution. This neutral term, taken under the max operator, adds the neutral hadron and photon transverse energies while subtracting the estimated pileup contribution, which approximates the neutral pileup energy using the charged pileup tracks. The $\max(0,\cdot)$ ensures this corrected neutral term never goes negative, preventing unphysical values. A small value of $I_{\textrm{rel}}^\mu$ indicates an isolated muon, typical of \textit{prompt muons} coming from the primary vertex, while a large value suggests the muon is embedded in a jet.

A tracker-based isolation variable is computed by summing the transverse momenta of all charged-particle tracks reconstructed in the inner tracker within a cone of radius $\Delta R < 0.3$ around the muon direction, normalised by the muon transverse momentum ${p_T}^\mu$. Only tracks consistent with originating from the same primary vertex as the muon are considered, in order to suppress contributions from pileup interactions. The muon track itself is excluded from the sum. A small value of this variable indicates that the muon is well isolated from other charged activity, as expected for prompt muons.

Finally, a mini-isolation variable is built to target muons produced in the decay chain of a boosted object using \cref{eq:PFisoMu} but with a variable cone radius given by \cref{eq:coneRadius}.

\begin{equation}
    \Delta R = \left\{ \begin{array}{rcl}
        0.2 & \mbox{for} & {p_T}^\mu<50 \, \textrm{GeV}/c \\ 
        \displaystyle \frac{10 \, \textrm{GeV}/c}{{p_T}^\mu} & \mbox{for} & 50 \leq {p_T}^\mu \leq 200 \, \textrm{GeV}/c \\
        0.05 & \mbox{for} & {p_T}^\mu>200 \, \textrm{GeV}/c
        \end{array}\right.
    \label{eq:coneRadius}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Muon reconstruction plays a crucial role in the HSCP analysis, as heavy stable charged particles are expected to behave like penetrating muon-like objects in the detector. Due to their large mass, HSCP candidates typically exhibit high transverse momentum and traverse the entire detector, reaching the muon system, similar to prompt muons. A precise and robust muon reconstruction is therefore essential to ensure high efficiency for HSCP candidates.


\subsubsection{Electrons and photons}

Electrons have a significant probability of showering when traversing the CMS tracker. The reconstruction begins with the identification of seed crystals --- local energy maxima above a given threshold --- around which the energy deposits are aggregated into clusters using a deterministic annealing or island algorithm. To account for the significant bremsstrahlung and conversion effects induced by the tracker material in front of the ECAL, these individual clusters are further merged into \textbf{superclusters} (SC), which extend in the $\phi$ direction to recover the energy radiated upstream. To do so, an \textit{hybrid} algorithm is used in the barrel region and a \textit{multi $5\times5$} in the endcaps \cite{ElectronReconstruction}. The energy inside an SC is the sum of the collected energy within its crystals. SC are labelled as seeds if they satisfy a minimum transverse energy of 1 GeV and are isolated from other SCs. These seeds are then propagated to the inner tracker, where a GSF track is fitted and matched to the supercluster to identify the particle as an electron.

Despite of muons, electron identification is affected by fake candidates. A multivariate discriminant (MVA) is trained to identify electrons and suppress background contributions, exploiting several discriminating variables such as the ECAL shower shape, the HCAL energy fraction, the GSF track quality, and the relative isolation. This isolation is computed similarly to \cref{eq:PFisoMu} by taking the ${p_T}^\textrm{e}$ instead and a fixed cone $\Delta R<0.3$.

Electron identification is defined through the MVA score. The \textit{loose} working point ($\sim90\%$ efficiency) is the least restrictive and is typically used for additional lepton vetoes or in regions with naturally low backgrounds. The \textit{medium} working point ($\sim~80\%$ efficiency) offers a balanced trade-off between signal efficiency and background rejection, making it a standard choice for generic electroweak measurements. Finally, the \textit{tight} working point ($\sim70 \%$ efficiency) applies the strictest selection, maximizing background rejection at the cost of signal efficiency, and is reserved for analyses where sample purity is critical.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Unlike electrons, photons are reconstructed by looking after ECAL seeds with transverse energy greater than 10 GeV/$c$ and not associated with a track. A dedicated conversion recovery algorithm also attempts to reconstruct photons that have converted into an $\textrm{e}^+\textrm{e}^-$ pair before reaching the ECAL, by looking for pairs of oppositely curved tracks pointing back to the supercluster. The energy of the photon is then calibrated using simulation-based corrections such as $Z \to \textrm{e}^+\textrm{e}^-$ events. Finally, photons are distinguished from other neutral particles --- most notably $\pi^0 \to \gamma\gamma$ decays, which represent the dominant source of fake isolated photons --- through shower shape variables.

\subsubsection{Jets}

Muons, electrons, and photons reconstructed by the PF algorithm are removed to consider the other entities. At this stage of the process, only charged and neutral hadrons are considered. They are mainly detected as \textit{jets}, which are collimated sprays of particles originating from the hadronisation\footnote{Process by which quarks and gluons combine to form colour-neutral hadrons.} and fragmentation of quarks and gluons produced in the hard-scattering interaction. Since free quarks and gluons cannot propagate over macroscopic distances due to colour confinement, they undergo a parton shower\footnote{Cascade of successive gluon emissions and quark-antiquark pair productions} before eventually forming stable hadrons. The resulting jet is therefore a composite object containing a mixture of charged hadrons (pions, kaons, protons), neutral hadrons (neutrons, $K^0_{L,S}$ or $\Lambda$), and photons from $\pi^0$ decays, all clustered around a common axis.

In CMS, jets are reconstructed from PF candidates using the anti--$k_T$ clustering algorithm \cite{AntiKT_cms}. This algorithm is infrared-safe\footnote{Infrared safety ensures that the jet definition is not altered by the emission of soft (low-energy) particles, meaning that adding an arbitrarily soft gluon to the event does not change the resulting jet configuration.} and collinear-safe\footnote{Collinear safety ensures that the jet definition is not affected by the collinear splitting of a particle into two particles carrying the same total momentum, meaning that replacing one particle by two collinear particles does not change the resulting jet configuration.}. To prevent the effects of PU, the Charged Hadron Subtraction (CHS) process \cite{CHS_cms} removes from the computation the charged and neutral PF candidates not originating from the PV. Anti--$k_T$ algorithm defines a distance between two objects $i$ and $j$ or between the object $i$ and the beam axis, given by \cref{eq:antikt}.

\begin{equation}
    d_{i,j}=\left\{ \begin{array}{rcl}
    \displaystyle \min \left( p_{T,i}^{2p}  \, , \, p_{T,j}^{2p} \right)\cdot \frac{\Delta R_{i,j}^2}{R^2} & \mbox{if} & i \neq j \\ 
    p_{T,i}^{2p} & \mbox{if} & i=j
    \end{array}\right.
    \label{eq:antikt}
\end{equation}

Where $p=-1$ controls the clustering behaviour in the anti--$k_T$ method, $\Delta R$ being the distance in the $\eta-\phi$ plane and $R$ the distance parameter which defines the cone size over which the PF candidates are aggregated. Two types of jets are then considered, AK4 and AK8 jets, with $R = 0.4$ or $R = 0.8$. The anti-$k_T$ algorithm proceeds iteratively over the list of PF candidates. At each iteration, the global minimum over all $d_{i,j}$ and $d_{i,i}$ is identified. If the minimum is a pair distance $d_{i,j}$, the two particles $i$ and $j$ are merged into a single pseudo-particle by summing their four-momenta. If instead the minimum is a beam distance $d_{i,i}$, particle $i$ is declared a jet and removed from the list. This procedure is repeated until no particles remain. The consequence of setting $p=-1$ is that soft particles are preferentially clustered around hard seeds first.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Pile-up contributions impact the jet resolution, especially at low $p_T$ jets, lowering the jet energy resolution. This effect is even more pronounced for Run--3 data, as the average pile-up tends to increase with year, reaching $\langle \textrm{PU} \rangle=60$ for 2025. To make this work, CMS has developed the PileUp Per Particle Identification (PUPPI) \cite{PUPPI_cms}, which is more stable and efficient than CHS for high pile-up scenarios . PF candidates are weighted based on their probability of arising from pile-up before applying the anti--$k_t$ method. Note that, at this point, two classes of jets are created: AK4PF jets (resulting from the CHS process) and AK4 PUPPI jets, the latter being the one recommended by CMS for Run--3 physics analysis.

Jet energies are then corrected through a series of jet energy corrections (JEC) applied sequentially. Relative and absolute corrections account for non-uniformities in the detector response as a function of $\eta$ and $p_T$, bringing the jet energy scale to a uniform and well-calibrated reference. Finally, residual corrections are applied to data to account for remaining discrepancies between data and simulation, derived from dedicated in-situ measurements such as the $p_T$ balance in $\gamma$+jet, $Z$+jet, and dijet events. After the full JEC chain, the jet energy resolution (JER) is also smeared in simulation to match the resolution observed in data.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

While the jets introduced previously are reconstructed from PF candidates, an alternative reconstruction can be performed using the energy deposits in the calorimeters (ECAL and HCAL), yielding the so-called \textit{CaloJets}. In this case, the inputs to the anti—$k_T$ clustering algorithm are calorimeter towers, which are projective groupings of ECAL and HCAL cells, rather than individual PF candidates. CaloJets are generally less precise than PF-based jets due to the coarser granularity of the calorimeters and the absence of tracker information, resulting in a worse jet energy resolution and a reduced ability to resolve nearby particles.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Reconstructed jets can further be classified according to their parton flavour by dedicated tagging algorithms. Heavy-flavour jets originating from $b$ or $c$ quarks are characterised by the presence of long-lived hadrons that travel a measurable distance before decaying, producing secondary vertices displaced from the primary interaction point and tracks with large impact parameter significance. These signatures, combined with additional variables such as the jet constituent kinematics and the presence of soft leptons, are exploited by multivariate algorithms to discriminate heavy-flavour jets from light-flavour ones. The current reference algorithm in CMS is \textsc{DeepJet}~\cite{DeepJet}, a deep neural network that processes the full list of jet constituents and returns a flavour probability score, against which a working point is applied to define the final jet flavour tag.


\subsubsection{Missing Energy Transverse}

Commonly named MET, the Missing Energy Transverse, results from taking the negative vectorial sum of 
the transverse momentum of all reconstructed objects, as calculated in \cref{eq:met}. As the momentum of colliding partons is mostly longitudinal, this vectorial sum in the transverse plane is supposed to be close to zero if all particles are detected. MET probes the presence of weakly or non-interacting particles such as neutrinos, or possibly new physics phenomena. Note that it cannot reveal the exact number of invisible particles, but exclusively the magnitude of the missing energy.

\begin{equation}
    \overrightarrow{p_T^\textrm{miss}} = -\sum_i \overrightarrow{p_{T,i}}
    \label{eq:met}
\end{equation}

In addition, MET may originate from detector inefficiency, limited acceptance, miscalibrations, or PF candidates reconstructed with high kinematic uncertainties.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

While the MET definition introduced previously refers to the PF-based missing transverse energy (PFMET), which exploits the full combination of all sub-detector information, an alternative estimator known as CaloMET can be computed solely from the energy deposits in the calorimeters (ECAL and HCAL), as the negative vectorial sum of all calorimeter tower energies projected onto the transverse plane. Although less precise than PFMET due to its sensitivity to calorimeter noise, non-linearities, and uninstrumented regions.


\section{CMS Phase--2 upgrade for HL--LHC campaigns}

In July 2026, the last collisions will take place in the LHC before LS3. This will initiate the Phase--2 upgrade of CMS to sustain for the future high luminosity program of the LHC, namely the HL--LHC, expected to last until 2040. The instantaneous luminosity is predicted to be at $5-7.5 \cdot 10^{34} \, \textrm{cm}^2\textrm{s}^{-1}$, which, integrated over then ten year period of time, would give an integrated luminosity of 3000 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$ \cite{HLLHCplans}. 

This upgrade will result in an increase of pile-up up to 200 simultaneous interactions per bunch crossing. Therefore, most of the detector upgrades are aimed at improving granularity to handle this particle flux, as well as higher radiation levels.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\noindent \textbf{Inner Tracker}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The inner tracker will be fully replaced by an all-silicon detector with increased granularity and reduced material budget. Its pseudorapidity coverage will be significantly extended from $|\eta| < 2.5$ to $|\eta| < 4$, improving the reconstruction of forward tracks and the pile-up rejection capabilities in the forward region \cite{Phase2Tracker}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The Phase--2 inner tracker will continue to provide energy loss per unit length $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x$ measurements based on charge deposition readout, whereas the outer tracker will switch to a binary readout, losing the analogue pulse-height information. To compensate for this loss and maintain sensitivity to highly ionising particles such as HSCPs, a dedicated high-ionisation pixel (HIP) flag will be implemented in the outer tracker. A hit is flagged as HIP when its deposited charge exceeds a programmable threshold, preliminary set at 1.4 times the minimum ionizing particle (MIP), as this value was found to enhance the gain in sensitivity \cite{Phase2Tracker}. Tracks accumulating a significant number of HIP-flagged clusters can then be discriminated from standard minimum-ionising background particles, providing an effective $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x$-like discriminator for HSCP searches even in the absence of full analogue readout.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\noindent \textbf{Barrel calorimeters}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The front-end and back-end electronics of the barrel calorimeters will be replaced to cope with the increased data rates. The ECAL will additionally benefit from an improved timing resolution of 30 ps for electrons and photons, enabling time-of-flight-based pile-up discrimination.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\noindent \textbf{Endcap calorimeters -- HGCAL}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The existing endcap calorimeters will be replaced by the High-Granularity Calorimeter (HGCAL), a sampling calorimeter providing three-dimensional shower reconstruction with precise timing information at the 30 ps level. Its fine transverse and longitudinal segmentation enables a so-called "5D"\footnote{the three spatial coordinates (x,y,z) + the deposited energy $E$ + the arrival time $t$.} particle flow reconstruction of jets, electrons, and photons, with significantly improved pile-up rejection \cite{Phase2HGCAL}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\noindent \textbf{MIP Timing Detector}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

A new dedicated timing layer, the MIP Timing Detector (MTD), will be installed between the tracker and the calorimeters, covering $|\eta| < 3$. It will provide a time-of-flight resolution of 30 ps per track, allowing the assignment of each charged particle to its originating bunch crossing vertex and thereby substantially reducing the impact of pile-up on reconstruction \cite{Phase2MTD}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\noindent \textbf{Muon system}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The muon system will be equipped with new front-end and back-end electronics, and its pseudorapidity acceptance will be extended from $|\eta| < 2.4$ to $|\eta| < 2.8$ through the addition of new GEM detector stations in the forward region.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\noindent \textbf{Trigger, DAQ and PF algorithm}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The Level--1 trigger will be deeply redesigned to incorporate tracking information already at the hardware trigger stage, enabling more refined online selections. The L1 output rate will be increased from 100 kHz to 750 kHz, and the HLT output rate from 1 kHz to 7.5 kHz, to accommodate the higher event complexity expected under HL-LHC conditions \cite{Phase2L1trigger}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The inclusion of tracking information at the L1 stage will allow the PF algorithm to be extended to the hardware trigger level. A dedicated \textit{Correlator Trigger} will combine the output of the Global Track Trigger with calorimeter and muon primitives to perform a PF~+~PUPPI reconstruction online, significantly lowering the trigger thresholds for jets and MET at fixed output rate compared to calorimeter-only or track-only approaches~\cite{Phase2L1trigger}.
