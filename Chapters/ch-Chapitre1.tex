\chapter{The CMS experiment at the LHC}
\chaptermark{The CMS experiment at the LHC}
\label{ch:chapitre1}
\minitoc

This chapter is devoted to the experimental foundations of high-energy physics, covering the tools and methods that enable precise measurements in collider experiments at CERN.

The vast majority of recent breakthroughs in particle physics, neutrino physics aside, have been achieved at high-energy accelerator facilities, and in particular at the LHC, whose accelerator complex is described in \cref{sec:LHC}. The LHC provides proton--proton collisions at unprecedented centre-of-mass energies, which are recorded by large, multi-purpose detectors such as CMS. This detector, presented in \cref{sec:CMS}, combines a broad set of complementary technologies to identify and characterise the particles emerging from each collision, to reconstruct the underlying physics process from the signals left in the detector material. The reconstruction strategies employed to this end are discussed in \cref{sec:EventReconstruction}. In practice, this amounts to inferring the hard-scattering interaction --- conveniently represented by a Feynman diagram --- from a set of raw detector responses. The chapter closes with an overview of Monte Carlo simulation techniques (\cref{sec:MC}) and of the CMS Phase-2 upgrade in preparation for the High-Luminosity LHC programme, covered in \cref{sec:HLLHC}.


\section{Historical background of CERN}
\label{sec:HistoricalCERN}

After World War II, European physics underwent a period of decline relative to other regions. This was largely due to the wartime destruction of European infrastructure, the fragmentation of national research efforts, and the emigration of leading scientists to the United States. Visionary scientists, including Pierre Auger and Niels Bohr, among others, responded by proposing a pan-European laboratory to bring together researchers and share resources. In 1949, French physicist Louis de Broglie made the first formal proposal at the Lausanne Cultural Conference. This initiated a series of follow-up actions that culminated in discussions with UNESCO. By 1952, eleven countries had signed an agreement to form a provisional council, and the name CERN (French acronym for Conseil Européen pour la Recherche Nucléaire) was then adopted.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

With the organisation in place, CERN quickly built its first accelerators. The Synchrocyclotron (SC) began operation in 1957 as a 600 MeV proton machine. In 1958, the SC enabled CERN's first major discovery: the observation of a charged pion decaying into an electron and a neutrino. Soon after, the 28 GeV Proton Synchrotron (PS) came online in 1959, briefly making CERN home to the world's highest-energy accelerator. The PS would continue to serve as a beam source for decades to come.

During the 1960s, particle detection at CERN was dominated by low-rate visual detectors such as bubble and spark chambers, which produced vast numbers of photographs. This method was slow and laborious. A breakthrough came in 1968 when Georges Charpak invented the multiwire proportional chamber, a gas-filled detector with parallel wires connected to amplifiers. Charpak's chamber could count particles roughly 1\,000 times faster than previous techniques, ushering in the modern electronic era of particle detectors.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

CERN next pioneered colliding-beam accelerators. In 1971, the Intersecting Storage Rings (ISR) began operation, creating the world's first high-energy proton--proton collisions. The ISR missed the 1974 ``November Revolution'' --- the simultaneous discovery of the $J/\psi$ meson ($c\bar{c}$) at Brookhaven and SLAC, confirming the quark model --- and the bottom quark discovery ($\Upsilon$ meson, $b\bar{b}$) at Fermilab in 1977, due to an initial lack of low-mass triggers, a slow DAQ, and insufficient acceptance. However, its legacy enabled next-generation collider experiments, including proton stacking, space-charge compensation, beam diagnostics, stochastic cooling, van der Meer luminosity scans, efficient triggers, and large-angle coverage. This success paved the way for the Super Proton Synchrotron (SPS). Approved in the early 1970s, the SPS is a 7~km circular machine straddling the Franco–Swiss border. It first circulated beam in 1976 and achieved roughly 400 GeV beam energy (above its original 300 GeV design). Later, by injecting antiprotons, CERN converted the SPS into a proton–antiproton collider. In this mode, the SPS led to the 1983 discovery of the $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ bosons, a milestone in confirming the electroweak theory, for which Carlo Rubbia and Simon van der Meer won the Nobel Prize~\cite{WandZ}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Building on this progress, CERN inaugurated the Large Electron–Positron collider (LEP) in 1989. LEP was installed in a 27 km tunnel (the largest circular collider then built), designed for high-precision studies of the electroweak interaction. In its first phase, it ran at 91.2 GeV centre-of-mass energy and produced on the order of 17 million $Z^0$ bosons. LEP's experiments performed very precise measurements. Notably, they confirmed that there are exactly three light neutrino species (three generations of matter particles)~\cite{LEPEW2006}. After roughly eleven years of operation, LEP was shut down in 2000 to push for the next-generation collider using the same tunnel: the Large Hadron Collider.


\section{The Large Hadron Collider}
\label{sec:LHC}

The LHC \cite{EvansBryant2008}, located at CERN on the Franco-Swiss border, is the world's largest particle accelerator, colliding beams at the highest centre-of-mass energy ever reached. It represents the culmination of decades of progressive increases in accelerator energy at CERN. Its primary objective was the discovery of the Higgs boson --- the last missing element of the Standard Model (see \cref{ch:chapitre3}) --- a goal achieved in 2012 by the ATLAS \cite{ATLAS_Higgs} and CMS \cite{CMS_Higgs} collaborations. Beyond this milestone, the LHC was conceived as a versatile tool to probe particle interactions at the TeV scale, capable of delivering proton--proton collisions at a centre-of-mass energy\footnote{$\sqrt{s}$ denotes the centre-of-mass energy of the collision, defined as the square root of the Mandelstam variable $s=(p_1+p_2)^2$, where $p_1$ and $p_2$ are the four-momenta of the two incoming particles, corresponding to the total energy available in the centre-of-mass frame.} up to $\sqrt{s}=14$ TeV. Although this nominal value has not yet been reached, the machine operated at a maximum energy of 13.6 TeV. At such energies, the LHC provides a unique opportunity to test the limits of the Standard Model: both by conducting high-precision measurements that may reveal subtle deviations from theoretical predictions, and by carrying out direct searches for phenomena Beyond the Standard Model (BSM) (some of which are discussed in \cref{ch:chapitre3}).

The scale of this endeavour is reflected in the machine's technical achievements. Compared to the Tevatron, a similar hadron collider operating at Fermilab between 1983 and 2011, the LHC achieved a centre-of-mass energy seven times higher, an instantaneous luminosity fifty times greater, and a bunch spacing sixteen times narrower, all within a ring four times larger in circumference. The detectors built around it match this ambition, designed and optimised using detailed simulations and incorporating all knowledge accumulated from previous experiments. Their physics performance has so far exceeded design expectations.


\subsection{Sub-accelerator complex}
\label{subsec:SubacceleratorComplex}

Technically, the LHC operates by accelerating and colliding two counter-rotating beams of protons or heavy ions at four dedicated interaction points (IP). The ring, buried about 100~m underground, has a circumference of 26.66~km and is composed of eight 2.45-km-long curved arcs connected by eight straight sections, each spanning roughly 545~m. Four of them host the major LHC experiments, while the remaining four accommodate essential machine infrastructure such as radio-frequency systems, collimators, beam dumps, and other utilities, as shown in \cref{fig-LHC-layout}.

    \caption{Illustration of the LHC geometry and experiments location \cite{LHC_layout}.}
    \label{fig-LHC-layout}
\end{figure}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The LHC ring is the final stage of a larger accelerator complex, depicted in \cref{fig:CERNComplexAccelerator}. The chain starts with LINAC4, an 86~m long linear accelerator installed 12~m below ground. Hydrogen gas is injected into a caesium plasma source, in which negative hydrogen ions H$^-$ are formed by attaching an additional electron to the hydrogen atoms. These ions are accelerated to 160~MeV over the length of the machine.

At this point, reaching significantly higher energies with a linear accelerator would require additional accelerating structures along the beam line, thereby making the machine longer. A synchrotron avoids this limitation by reusing the same cavities over many successive turns, so that the beam energy can be raised gradually without a comparable growth in size. The beam is thus transferred to the Proton Synchrotron Booster (PSB), where the H$^-$ ions cross a thin carbon foil that strips both electrons and leaves bare protons. This charge-exchange injection allows the incoming beam to be superimposed on the circulating one over many turns, accumulating a much higher intensity than a direct proton injection would permit. The protons are accelerated to 2~GeV in the PSB, then to 26~GeV in the PS, and finally to 450~GeV in the SPS, which serves as the LHC injector. Bunch trains are finally transferred into the two LHC rings, where the beams circulate in opposite directions, following a filling scheme that fixes their longitudinal positions. Once both rings are filled, the beams are ramped to their Run--3 energy of 6.8~TeV.

    \caption{The CERN accelerator complex, showing the chain of accelerators leading to the LHC. The LHC is represented by the large ring on the top of the sketch, with the four major experiments indicated by yellow dots \cite{CernComplexAccelerator_ref}.}
    \label{fig:CERNComplexAccelerator}

Once in the LHC, protons are accelerated to their nominal energy. For that, oscillating microwave fields generated by 16 superconducting Radio-Frequency (RF) cavities (8 per beam) housed in 4 cryo-modules produce a longitudinal oscillating voltage in synchronisation with the protons, keeping them tightly bunched and accelerating them with an accelerating voltage of 2~MV per cavity (a gradient of 5~MV/m). Each cavity is driven by a high-power klystron, a tube containing electron beams, which is intensity-modulated at 400 MHz. With that, protons receive 480 keV of energy per turn, or 5.4 GeV/s, during a 20-minute ramp-up period before reaching the nominal energy. 

The beams are confined in a low-pressure environment of approximately $10^{-13}$ atmospheres to mitigate beam losses from collisions between the proton beams and residual gas molecules in the beam pipe.

Along the eight arcs of the LHC, 1232 superconducting NbTi dipole magnets (154 per arc) bend the beams onto their circular orbit. As the beams are accelerated, the dipole field is ramped from 0.54~T at injection (450~GeV) to 8.1~T at 6.8~TeV, with a design value of 8.33~T for 7~TeV beams. These magnets need to be cooled to 1.9~K using superfluid helium to reach the superconducting regime, allowing a current of about 11\,500~A to flow through the coils at 6.8~TeV (11\,850~A at the 7~TeV design value). This maximum current, and thus the bending power of the dipoles, limits the beam energy: reaching the design field requires further magnet training to avoid quenches, which is why the LHC operated at 6.8~TeV per beam during Run--3. The LHC uses quadrupole magnets (for a total of 858) that act like magnetic lenses and focus the beam, especially at the interaction points. However, their focusing strength depends on the particle energy. 688 sextupole magnets are therefore used to correct this energy dependence. Finally, 168 octupole magnets introduce controlled non-linear effects that help stabilise the beam against collective instabilities (Landau damping).

Charged particles emit electromagnetic radiation when accelerated along a curved trajectory, a phenomenon known as synchrotron radiation. The energy loss per turn scales as $m^{-4}$, where $m$ denotes the particle mass. Due to their relatively large mass, protons can be accelerated to much higher energies than electrons before synchrotron losses become significant. Under identical kinetic energy and radius of curvature, electrons radiate approximately $10^{13}$ times more rapidly than protons, which motivated the selection of protons for multi-TeV accelerators such as the LHC. With a dipole field of 8.33~T, the resulting energy loss for protons is only 6.7 keV per turn, a value sufficiently small for the RF cavities to compensate during nominal operation.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

During the data-taking campaign in 2022--2026, the LHC circulated 2808 groups of about 100 billion protons called \textit{bunches}. Even though the ring can accommodate up to 3564 bunch positions, only a fraction of them are filled with protons. This is due to the finite rise time of the pulsed magnets (\textit{kickers}) used throughout the injector and dumping chain: abort gaps are kept empty between bunches so that the kicker magnets can reach their nominal field before injecting or dumping the beam. Beams are therefore organised as trains (groups of bunches) separated by these empty gaps. Each bunch location is time-separated from its previous one by 25~ns, corresponding to the nominal LHC frequency of 40~MHz, and rotates at 11\,245 revolutions per second, with a velocity as close as 3~m/s to the speed of light. Note that at the current maximum energy (13.6~TeV), the stored beam energy is about $2\times400$~MJ.

Because the number of protons per bunch decreases after each beam crossing, the quadrupole magnet intensities are adjusted over time to keep the bunches sufficiently squeezed. 

In case of a beam failure, the LHC Beam Dumping System is activated to eject both beams into 10-metre-long graphite blocks safely. The beam dumping procedure is also routinely conducted at the conclusion of each LHC fill: after approximately eight to twelve hours of operation, the bunch intensity has degraded to the point where the beam can no longer be focused to a sufficiently small size, rendering further collisions inefficient, and the beam is then dumped.

\subsection{LHC experiments}
\label{subsec:LHCExperiments}

The LHC hosts four main experiments, each designed with complementary goals and detection technologies. 

ATLAS (A Toroidal LHC ApparatuS)~\cite{ATLAS_detector} and CMS (Compact Muon Solenoid)~\cite{CMS_detector} are the two general-purpose detectors. Despite their different designs --- ATLAS being based on a large toroidal magnetic field and CMS on a high-field solenoidal magnet --- they share the common mission of probing a wide range of phenomena, from Standard Model precision measurements to searches for physics beyond the Standard Model. Both collaborations played a central role in the discovery of the Higgs boson in 2012 and continue to provide leading results in the search for new particles, such as supersymmetric states, dark matter candidates, or exotic resonances.

LHCb (Large Hadron Collider beauty)~\cite{LHCb_detector} focuses on the study of beauty and charm hadrons. Its forward geometry and high-precision vertex detector are optimised to measure rare decays, CP violation, and flavour-changing processes, which provide an indirect window into possible new physics contributions. Thanks to its unique design, LHCb has become a world-leading experiment in the field of flavour physics, testing the consistency of the Standard Model in the quark sector with unprecedented precision.

Finally, ALICE (A Large Ion Collider Experiment)~\cite{ALICE_detector} is dedicated to exploring strongly interacting matter under extreme conditions. By colliding heavy ions such as lead nuclei, ALICE recreates in the laboratory the quark–gluon plasma, a state of matter believed to have existed microseconds after the Big Bang. The experiment investigates its properties --- such as collective flow, particle correlations, and jet quenching --- shedding light on the dynamics of the strong nuclear force in the high-density regime.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

In addition to its four main experiments, the LHC hosts a set of smaller, highly specialised detectors that broaden its physics reach.

TOTEM (TOTal Elastic and diffractive cross section Measurement, located near CMS)~\cite{TOTEM:JINST} is devoted to measuring the total cross section of proton–proton interactions, elastic scattering, and diffraction processes. Its detectors, placed far from the interaction point in so-called ``Roman pots'', can detect protons scattered at very small angles, providing insights into the strong interaction at low momentum transfer. Complementary to this, LHCf (Large Hadron Collider forward, near ATLAS)~\cite{LHCf:JINST} investigates neutral particles produced at very small angles relative to the beamline. Its measurements are particularly valuable for calibrating models of cosmic-ray interactions in the atmosphere, thereby linking collider physics to astroparticle physics.

Another dedicated experiment, MoEDAL (Monopole and Exotics Detector at the LHC, which shares the cavern at Point 8 with LHCb)~\cite{MoEDAL:physics}, is designed to search for highly ionising particles such as magnetic monopoles or other stable, massive exotic states predicted by certain extensions of the Standard Model.

More recently, new experiments have been installed to exploit the LHC's unique forward regions. FASER (ForwArd Search ExpeRiment)~\cite{FASER:detector} is designed to detect light and weakly interacting particles, such as dark photons or sterile neutrinos, which could be produced in large numbers along the beam direction but would escape detection in central detectors. Similarly, SND@LHC (Scattering and Neutrino Detector at the LHC)~\cite{SNDLHC:detector} is the first dedicated collider experiment to measure neutrinos produced in high-energy proton collisions, opening a new window into both neutrino physics and forward-hadronic production. Lastly, milliQan~\cite{milliQan:demonstrator} aims to search for milli-charged particles --- hypothetical particles carrying a tiny fraction of the electron charge --- using a dedicated scintillator-based detector installed in a drainage gallery near CMS.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Looking ahead, several new experiments are planned or under consideration for the LHC's future. ANUBIS (AN Underground Belayed In-Shaft experiment)~\cite{ANUBIS:proposal} proposes to instrument one of the LHC's access shafts to search for long-lived particles decaying far from the interaction point. Similarly, MATHUSLA (Massive Timing Hodoscope for Ultra-Stable neutraL pArticles)~\cite{MATHUSLA:physics} is a large-scale surface-detector concept designed to probe extremely long lifetimes that are inaccessible to any current collider experiment. CODEX-b (COmpact Detector for EXotics at LHCb)~\cite{CODEXb:concept} would be installed in a shielded region adjacent to LHCb, using a compact yet highly hermetic detector to target weakly coupled, long-lived particles. Together, these proposed experiments reflect a growing recognition that physics beyond the Standard Model may manifest through feebly interacting or long-lived particles, a sector partially covered by the main LHC detectors.

The CERN accelerator complex also supports a fixed-target programme, in which the circulating beam is directed onto a stationary target rather than colliding with another beam. The NA62 experiment~\cite{NA62:detector}, for instance, uses a proton beam extracted from the SPS to study ultra-rare kaon decays with high precision. More directly connected to the LHC beam, the LHCb experiment operates in a fixed-target mode --- known as SMOG (System for Measuring the Overlap with Gas)~\cite{LHCb:SMOG, LHCb:SMOG2} --- by injecting a low-density noble gas into its vertex detector region, allowing it to study proton--gas and ion--gas collisions at a centre-of-mass energy of about a hundred GeV. This unique capability provides complementary measurements of nuclear parton distribution functions and hadronic production cross sections, bridging collider and fixed-target physics.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

These complementary experiments enrich the LHC physics program. While the flagship detectors ATLAS, CMS, LHCb, and ALICE address broad or well-defined research domains, the smaller-scale experiments target highly specific questions, from the structure of soft QCD interactions to the search for entirely new forms of matter.




\subsection{Collisions and luminosity}
\label{subsec:CollisionsAndLuminosity}

The following subsections describe several key parameters commonly used in particle colliders, in particular the concept of luminosity, which is directly related to the event rate, and the number of simultaneous collisions occurring within a single event. Both parameters play a crucial role in accelerator physics. Although an event is technically defined as the result of a detector readout, here it is often used interchangeably with the term ``collision''.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The CMS experiment and the LHC use the \textit{instantaneous luminosity} $\mathscr{L}$ to relate the rate of events $\partial N$ occurring within a time interval $\partial t$ of data collection to the cross section $\sigma$ of the process under study:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial N}{\partial t} = \mathscr{L} \cdot \sigma\, .
    \label{eq:nbrEvent}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Given the proton bunch structure of the LHC beams, and assuming Gaussian beam profiles colliding head-on \cite{LumiComputation}, the instantaneous luminosity is calculated thanks to:

\begin{equation}
    \mathscr{L} = \frac{N_p^2n_bf_{\textrm{rev}}\gamma}{4\pi\epsilon_n\beta^{*}} \cdot F
    \label{eq:instantaneous_luminosity}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $N_p$ is the number of particles per bunch;
    \item $n_b$ is the number of bunches per beam;
    \item $f_{\textrm{rev}}$ is the beam revolution frequency;
    \item $\gamma$ is the relativistic factor;
    \item $\epsilon_n$ is the normalised emittance, which characterises the spread of the proton distribution in the plane perpendicular to the beam axis;
    \item $\beta^{*}$ is the betatron function at the IP, which characterises the bunch envelope that oscillates around the design orbit due to the focusing provided by the magnetic field, assuming $\beta_x=\beta_y$ (the smaller the value of $\beta^{*}$, the tighter the beam size is);
    \item $F$ is the geometric luminosity reduction factor due to the crossing angle $\theta_\textrm{IP}$ at the IP, calculated using:
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
    F=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\left( \frac{\theta_{\textrm{IP}}\cdot\sigma_z}{2\sigma_{\textrm{IP}}} \right)^2}}
    \label{eq:FactorF}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_z$ is the RMS bunch length along the proton beam and $\sigma_{\textrm{IP}}$ the RMS beam size in the plane perpendicular to the beam axis at the IP (assuming that $\sigma_x=\sigma_y$).

As discussed in \cref{subsec:SubacceleratorComplex}, the number of protons per bunch decreases rapidly with time, following an exponential decay: $\mathscr{L}\propto e^{-t/\tau_0}$ with $\tau_0$ of the order of 10 hours at the LHC today.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

CMS measures instantaneous luminosity in real time using dedicated sub-detectors, called luminometers: the pixel tracking detector, the Forward Hadron calorimeter (HF), the Pixel Luminosity Telescope (PLT), and the fast Beam Conditions Monitor (BCM1F). Each luminosity estimate is based on the average number of interactions per bunch crossing.

Two complementary methods are used, depending on the detector. In the pixel detector, where signal overlaps are negligible, the rate-scaling method is applied: the pixel cluster counting (PCC) method relates the average number of pixel clusters per bunch crossing directly to the luminosity through a visible cross section. Conversely, for HF, PLT, and BCM1F --- where overlapping interactions prevent distinct signal separation --- the zero-counting method is preferred. This approach estimates luminosity by evaluating the fraction of empty bunch crossings, assuming Poisson statistics.

The measurements from all luminometers are then combined and calibrated using beam-separation (van der Meer) scans and additional beam-monitoring information to provide a precise and stable determination of luminosity.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Physicists use the \textit{integrated luminosity} $L$ as a normalisation factor, relating the number of observed events $N$ to the cross section $\sigma$ of the process under study via:

\begin{equation}
    N = \sigma \cdot L = \sigma \int \mathscr{L} \, \mathrm{d}t \,.
    \label{eq:intlumi}
\end{equation}

At the LHC, the integrated luminosity is usually defined in inverse femtobarn $\mathrm{fb}^{-1}$ ($1\,\mathrm{fb}^{-1} = 10^{39} \, \mathrm{cm}^{-2}$) to better compare with the magnitude of the cross sections of particle physics processes. 

In practice, three distinct luminosity quantities are defined. The \textit{delivered luminosity} corresponds to the total luminosity produced by the LHC at the interaction point. The \textit{recorded luminosity} is the fraction actually recorded by the detector, accounting for trigger and data-acquisition dead time. Finally, only the data recorded while every sub-detector was fully operational are used for physics analysis. This certification is performed at the granularity of a luminosity section, corresponding to about 23~s of data-taking, so that periods affected by a hardware failure or degraded performance in any sub-system are discarded. The integrated luminosity is then recomputed over the validated sections only. A comparison of the \textit{validated integrated luminosity} recorded by CMS over the years of data-taking is presented in \cref{fig:intlumi}.  Summed over the period 2010 to 2026, the validated integrated luminosity is close to 480~fb$^{-1}$ (about 30, 138 and 310~fb$^{-1}$ for Run--1, Run--2 and Run--3, respectively).

\subsection{Pile-up}
\label{subsec:PileUp}

Monitoring the real-time value of the instantaneous luminosity is tricky if using \cref{eq:instantaneous_luminosity}. This would require knowing the precise value of the parameters. An alternative is to monitor processes for which we know the cross section of production $\sigma_{\textrm{ref}}$ and count how many of them are produced, which is the rate $\textrm{R}_{\textrm{ref}}$:

\begin{equation}
    \textrm{R}_{\textrm{ref}} = \mathscr{L} \cdot \sigma_{\textrm{ref}}\,.
    \label{eq:cross-section_ref}
\end{equation}

At the LHC, the reference collision process is the proton inelastic scattering with $\sigma_{\textrm{inel}}=68.6 \, \pm 0.5 \, (\textrm{syst.}) \, \pm \, 3.2 \, (\textrm{lumi}) \, \textrm{mb}$, measured in the Run--2 data at a centre-of-mass energy of 13 TeV \cite{pileUPxsection}. As the rate is also defined as the number of collisions, one can also define it as:

\begin{equation}
    \textrm{R}_{\textrm{ref}} = \mu \cdot n_b \cdot f_{\textrm{rev}}
    \label{eq:pu_rate}
\end{equation}
with $\mu$ the mean number of interactions per bunch crossing at the IP, namely the \textit{pile-up} (PU). Finally, the instantaneous luminosity is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathscr{L} = \frac{\mu \cdot n_b \cdot f_{\textrm{rev}}}{\sigma_{\textrm{inel}}}\, .
    \label{eq:lumi_measured}
\end{equation}

Inverting this expression yields an average pile-up of $\mu \approx 22$ under the LHC's nominal design parameters. However, as the instantaneous luminosity has steadily increased over time by a factor of 2-2.5 today, this has resulted in a higher average pile-up of 60, with peaks exceeding 80. The pile-up values measured by the CMS collaboration for the different years of data-taking between Run--1 and Run--3 are shown in \cref{fig:pileup}. Notice that the value of $\sigma_{\textrm{inel}}$ differs from the one given above; here, it is the total inelastic cross section obtained over the full phase space.

One can then observe that increasing luminosity increases the pile-up per event. This point is challenging for every detector: a higher number of collisions will increase detector occupancy, which may in turn reduce detection efficiency. To optimise data acquisition and minimise pile-up, the instantaneous luminosity is maintained near its optimal value through luminosity levelling \cite{LumiLevelling}. \cref{fig:lumiOMS} shows the recorded luminosity, the average pile-up and the two $\beta^{*}_{x,y}$ parameters during a proton--proton data-taking period. These two parameters decrease over time, ensuring tighter, smaller bunches.


\subsection{The LHC calendar}

The physics program at the LHC is divided into two primary phases: Phase-1 (2011-2026) and Phase-2 (2030-2041).

So far, the Phase-1 program has been divided into 3 periods of data-taking, called \textit{Runs}, separated by \textit{Long Shutdowns} (LS), which are periods for detector upgrades and maintenance. During a year, the proton--proton collisions program lasts about 6 months, followed by heavy-ion physics and then a technical pause during the winter. Within a year, Runs are divided into \textit{Eras} to mark changes in experimental conditions, beam energy, and detector setups or software releases. Eras are then divided into \textit{run numbers}, which correspond to continuous periods of data-taking with stable detector and data-acquisition conditions, typically during a single LHC fill.

Run--1 (2010-2012) marked the first completed operational stage of the LHC. During this period, CMS recorded 30 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$ of data at the centre-of-mass energy of 7~TeV (2010-2011, corresponding to 6.1 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$) and 8~TeV (2012, corresponding to 23.3 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$) with a bunch spacing of 50 ns. After the first LS that lasted 2 years, the superconducting magnets and detector systems were upgraded to support higher luminosity. Run--3 (2022-2026) raised the centre-of-mass energy to 13.6 TeV (still below the expected 14 TeV at the LHC's design). So far, this period has been marked by a spectacular data collection campaign. \section{The Compact Muon Solenoid experiment}
\label{sec:CMS}

Despite having a less marketable name than its counterparts at the LHC, the CMS experiment \cite{CMS_detector} is remarkable for its design and programme, which aim to cover a wide range of physical phenomena. The CMS experiment is located in the 100-metre-deep underground cavern at IP5, north of the LHC ring. As the name suggests, every sub-part constituting this detector has been compacted into a cylinder 21 metres long and 15 metres in diameter, for a total weight of 14\,000 tonnes (with a volume six times smaller and twice as heavy as ATLAS). CMS has been specifically designed to detect high-momentum muons, with muon chambers occupying 80\% of the detector volume. Lastly, the solenoid magnet, which generates a magnetic field of 3.8~T, deflects the trajectories of charged particles, allowing accurate measurement of their momentum and electric charge. In the following, the CMS detector is carefully dissected component by component, both from the perspective of its instrumentation and its software operation, with particular emphasis on the event reconstruction algorithms that map raw detector signals into meaningful physics observables.

\subsection{Coordinate system}

The CMS detector uses a right-handed Cartesian coordinate system, with the collision point as its origin (at the centre of the detector). The $x$--axis points toward the LHC centre and the $y$--axis toward the surface, both forming a perpendicular plane to the beam pipe, known as the transverse plane. The $z$--axis represents the longitudinal axis and points toward the Jura mountains, or more physically speaking, in the counterclockwise direction of the LHC.

Due to its cylindrical detector shape, the partial spherical coordinate system is also used. The azimuthal angle $\phi$ is defined as the angle measured from the $x$--axis to the $y$--axis, the polar angle $\theta$ is measured from the $z$--axis, and the radial distance $r$ is defined in the transverse plane. In collider physics, and more generally in high energy physics, one can also define the \textit{pseudorapidity} $\eta$, deriving from taking the ultra-relativistic regime of the rapidity\footnote{The rapidity is defined as $\frac{1}{2}\ln\left( \frac{E+p_z}{E-p_z} \right)$ where $E$ is the energy of the particle and $p_z$ its longitudinal momentum. This quantity is zero when a particle is close to the transverse plane, but tends to $\pm\infty$ when a particle is moving close to the beam axis in either direction.} (when $pc\gg mc^2$):

\begin{equation}
    \eta = - \ln \left[ \tan \left( \frac{\theta}{2} \right) \right] \, .
    \label{eq:pseudorapidity}
\end{equation}

Note that a particle emitted perpendicular to the beam axis has pseudorapidity zero, and as the particle approaches the $z$--axis, its pseudorapidity tends toward infinity. In the ultra-relativistic regime, the difference in pseudorapidity $\Delta \eta$ between two particles is invariant under Lorentz boosts along the beam axis, meaning that $\Delta \eta$ remains unchanged in all inertial frames related by longitudinal boosts.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

We can therefore define the \textit{angular distance} $\Delta R$ separating two particles $i$ and $j$ such as:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta R = \sqrt{{\Delta \eta_{ij}}^2 + {\Delta \phi_{ij}}^2}
    \label{eq:deltaR}
\end{equation}  
where $\Delta \eta_{ij} = | \eta_i - \eta_j|$ and $\Delta \phi_{ij} = | \phi_i - \phi_j|$ so that $\Delta R$ is also Lorentz invariant\footnote{$\phi=\arctan(p_y/p_x)$, a longitudinal boost along the $z$--axis does not affect the azimuthal angle.} for referential change along the beam axis direction. \cref{fig:coordinate} shows the different coordinates used in CMS.

\end{figure}

\subsubsection{The silicon tracker}

The silicon tracker is the closest sub-detector to the IP. Its goal is to reconstruct bent charged-particle trajectories --- called \textit{tracks} --- along with their momenta. As the charged particles, produced either directly or indirectly by collisions, propagate through the tracker, they ionise the material. Silicon sensors detect charge generation. This technology provides excellent spatial resolution and radiation hardness, along with a low material budget to minimise the multiple scattering of charged particles. On top of that, silicon has two additional benefits compared to homologous tracking detectors such as drift chambers or time projection chambers: high density and low electron-hole creation energy within the semiconductor material enable thin sensors with sufficient signal, and high mobility of charge carriers in silicon provides fast charge collection ($\approx$5~ns for an electron).

The tracker is divided into two parts: the silicon pixel, the part closer to the beam pipe, and the silicon strip sub-detectors surrounding it. Each of them is made of multiple layers, with different sensor geometries optimised for the distance from the IP. The tracker covers a radial range of 2.9 to 110~cm and a wide pseudorapidity range, $|\eta|<2.5$, using information from both the barrel and endcap regions. This coverage is achieved by 14 concentric barrel layers (four in the pixel detector, four in the TIB and six in the TOB) and, on each side, 15 endcap disks (three in the FPIX, three in the TID and nine in the TEC).

The readout electronics are synchronised with the 25~ns LHC bunch crossing clock, allowing hits\footnote{A hit is defined as the charge deposited inside a group of pixels or strips that is collected and used to reconstruct tracks later.} to be associated with their corresponding bunch crossing. To reduce thermal noise and mitigate radiation-induced damage, pixel sensors are cooled to $-10^{\circ}\textrm{C}$, while strip sensors are cooled to increasingly lower coolant temperatures over time to compensate for the growing leakage current induced by accumulated radiation damage: from $-15^{\circ}\textrm{C}$ at the start of Run--2, to $-20^{\circ}\textrm{C}$ since 2018, down to $-25^{\circ}\textrm{C}$ during Run--3~\cite{CMStracker}. A layout of the CMS tracker is shown in \cref{fig:tracker}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Pixel tracker}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

At the start of the LHC, the pixel detector was made of three barrel layers and two disks in each forward region. During the winter break in 2016-2017, the pixel detector was upgraded with an additional barrel layer, and an endcap disk on top of a change in the pixel supports, shifting from on-board to off-board electronics, along with the implementation of a $\textrm{CO}_2$ cooling system~\cite{CMSPhase1Pixel2021}. A layout of the pixel tracker is presented in \cref{fig:pixeltracker}.

The four barrel pixel layers (BPIX) cover radial distances from 2.9 to 16~cm from the IP, while the three forward disks (FPIX) are set between 29.1 and 51.6~cm from the centre longitudinally with a coverage up to $|\eta|<2.5$. The BPIX is made of 1\,184 modules, while the FPIX has 672. Each module features a lattice of $160 \times 416$ pixels cells within a rectangular active area of $16.2 \times 64.8 \, \textrm{mm}^2$, with each cell measuring $100 \times 150 \, \mu\textrm{m}^2$ with a thickness of 285~$\mu\textrm{m}$. This fine granularity, combined with charge sharing between neighbouring pixels, provides a hit resolution better than the binary resolution of a cell ($100/\sqrt{12}\approx29~\mu$m and $150/\sqrt{12}\approx43~\mu$m in the transverse and longitudinal directions).

This first part of the tracker ensures reconstruction of the primary vertex, the initial hard-scatter interaction point. On top of that, it identifies in-flight decays, such as those from $B$-hadrons, $D$-hadrons, or $\tau$ leptons, by reconstructing secondary vertices.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Strip tracker}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

As the particle flux decreases with distance squared, a lower-granularity detector can be used. With this, silicon micro-strip sensors are considered instead of pixel ones.

The silicon strip tracker consists of four distinct regions, each containing specific layers of strip sensors. The central region includes four layers in the Tracker Inner Barrel (TIB) and six layers in the Tracker Outer Barrel (TOB). The barrel region is enclosed by three disks in the Tracker Inner Disk (TID) and nine disks in the Tracker End Cap (TEC) located in each forward region. With this, the silicon strip tracker covers radial distances from 20 to 110~cm and a longitudinal distance up to 282~cm, spanning a pseudorapidity range up to $|\eta|<2.5$ \cite{CMStracker}.

In total, 15\,148 modules fill the volume of the silicon strip detector. Some of them contain either one (mono) or two (stereo) silicon sensors, shown in red and blue, respectively, in \cref{fig:tracker}, for a total of 24\,244 sensors. Stereo modules are mounted back-to-back, with the second sensor tilted by 100 mrad to reconstruct three-dimensional information about the hits. TIB and TOB parts consist of concentric cylinders, with the modules oriented parallel to the beam axis to ensure accurate $r{-}\phi$ measurements. Meanwhile, TID and TEC are rings in which the sensors lie in the transverse plane, resulting in better $r{-}\phi$ resolution in the forward region.

In addition to providing precise position measurements, the silicon strip modules also record the charge deposited by particles crossing the sensors. This information can be used to estimate the specific energy loss, d$E/$d$x$, defined as the collected charge per unit of distance in the silicon. Since the energy loss depends on the particle velocity (discussed in \cref{sec:interactionWithMatter}), this observable provides valuable information for particle identification. It is particularly useful in searches for slow, highly ionising particles.

\subsubsection{Calorimeters}

While the tracker is essential for reconstructing charged particle trajectories, it alone is insufficient for particle identification. Furthermore, neutral particles leave no signal in the silicon sensors and therefore escape detection by the tracker entirely. To complete the tracker, two calorimeter systems are mounted surrounding the latter: an electromagnetic calorimeter (ECAL) \cite{CMSecal} and a hadronic calorimeter (HCAL) \cite{CMShcal}, both designed to measure the energy of incident particles. They consist of dense materials chosen and arranged to force incoming particles to interact and develop a cascade of secondary particles of lower energy, called a \textit{particle shower}. 

The ECAL has been designed to stop electromagnetic showers produced when electrons and photons lose energy through interactions with matter. This energy loss is caused by processes such as \textit{bremsstrahlung}\footnote{\textit{Bremsstrahlung} is the German word for braking radiation, indicating photons produced by the deceleration of a charged particle} and electron-positron pair production (in which photons are converted into electron-positron pairs). As the shower progresses inside the ECAL, additional secondary particles are produced, leading to the creation of low-energy photons. Thus, the total energy collected corresponds to that of the incoming particle.

The HCAL, placed surrounding the ECAL, targets hadronic showers. Such showers are driven by the strong nuclear force and arise from interactions between charged or neutral hadrons, leading to the creation and decay of secondary hadrons. The latter ionise the material as they propagate, generating photons that are then collected by the scintillators. A hadronic shower consists of: $\approx$48\% electromagnetic energy (like neutral pions decaying into two photons), $\approx$25\% visible non-electromagnetic energy (such as charged pions, kaons, or muons), $\approx$25\% invisible energy (nuclear fission, excitation, neutrons), and $\approx$2\% escaped energy.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Electromagnetic calorimeter}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

The ECAL is divided into three sections: the Barrel ECAL (EB), covering a pseudorapidity range $|\eta|<1.479$, the ECAL Endcap (EE) that extends the coverage to $|\eta|<3.0$, and the ECAL Preshower system (ES) located at the entry of the EE. A layout of the ECAL sub-detector is presented in \cref{fig:ECAL}.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{Images/Ch1/ECALgloballayout.pdf}
    \caption{Schematic view of the CMS ECAL, showing the barrel region composed of lead tungstate crystals, and the two endcap regions equipped with supercrystals (groups of several crystals combined to form larger readout units)~\cite{CMSecalphoto}.}
    \label{fig:ECAL}
\end{figure}

The calorimeter is homogeneously populated with lead tungstate ($\textrm{PbWO}_4$) scintillating crystals. This material, with a density of $8.28~\textrm{g}/\textrm{cm}^3$, provides a small radiation length\footnote{The radiation length $X_0$ is the mean distance over which a high-energy electron loses all but $e^{-1}\approx37\%$ of its energy by bremsstrahlung. At first order, $X_0 \approx 1\,433 \cdot \frac{A}{Z(Z+1)(11.319-\ln(Z))}$~g\,cm$^{-2}$, to be divided by the material density to obtain a length, where $Z$ and $A$ are respectively the atomic number and the mass number of the nucleus.} ($X_0=0.89$~cm) as well as small Molière radius\footnote{The Molière radius is the radius of the cone containing 90\% of the shower energy. One can derive it using the radiation length: $R_M = 0.0265 \cdot X_0 \cdot (Z+1.2)$.} ($R_M=2.19$~cm) but also a natural tolerance to radiation. Lead tungstate has a low scintillation time, collecting 80\% of the light within the 25~ns time between two bunch crossings. On the other hand, lead tungstate has a relatively low light yield (a few photons per MeV), which implies the use of photo-detectors with an internal amplification factor of 50 (10) in the barrel (endcap) region.

A total of 75\,848 crystals compose the ECAL, with 61\,200 in the barrel (length of $25.8 \cdot X_0\approx23$~cm) and $2 \times 7\,324$ in the two endcaps (length of $24.7 \cdot X_0 \approx 22$~cm). The front face cross section of the crystals corresponds to a granularity of $\Delta\eta\times\Delta\phi = 0.0175 \times 0.0175$ (giving a surface of $2.2 \times 2.2 \, \textrm{cm}^2$ for the barrel crystals and $2.86 \times 2.86~\textrm{cm}^2$ for the endcaps ones) \cite{CMSecalcrystals}. With such values, electromagnetic showers are confined to the crystals; thus, no secondary particles are lost in passive absorber materials. On the other hand, the absence of longitudinal segmentation limits direct access to shower-depth information, reducing its intrinsic electromagnetic/hadronic discrimination power and particle-identification capabilities. As illustrated in \cref{fig:ECAL}, the crystals are tilted by 3° relative to the IP direction to prevent photons from entering adjacent crystals and escaping detection. 

The ES does not contain any crystals but rather two layers of lead absorber with layers of silicon strip sensors used to distinguish single photons from photons coming from the decay of neutral pions.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Despite the distance from the IP, the transparency of the crystals is affected with time (and radiation), changing the ECAL energy resolution. Calibration of crystal transparency is performed using lasers, with light injected when there is no bunch. The energy resolution of the ECAL, $\sigma_{\textrm{ECAL}}$, has been measured in test beams~\cite{CMSecal}:

\begin{equation}
    \left( \frac{\sigma_{\textrm{ECAL}}}{E} \right)^2 = \left( \frac{0.028}{\sqrt{E}} \right)^2 + \left( \frac{41.5 \, \textrm{MeV}}{E} \right)^2 + 0.003^2
    \label{eq:ecalresolution}
\end{equation}
with $E$ the photon or electron energy in GeV. The ECAL energy resolution is the quadratic sum of three terms: a stochastic term ($\propto 1/\sqrt{E}$), caused by statistical fluctuations in shower development and photon collection; a noise term ($\propto 1/E$), due to electronic noise and pile-up; and a constant term, arising from calibration uncertainties and detector non-uniformities.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Hadron calorimeter}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

The HCAL is segmented into four parts, as depicted in \cref{fig:CMShcal}: the Barrel (HB) and the Endcap (HE), which cover a pseudorapidity range up to $|\eta|<3$; the Outer barrel (HO) and the Forward (HF). The latter extends the coverage to $|\eta|<5$ and situated at $|z|=11.1 \, \textrm{m}$. The HO is positioned surrounding the magnet to contain the most energetic particle showers that would otherwise pass through the EB and HB.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The HB and HE are made with interspersed brass absorbers\footnote{A curious fact: some of the brass used comes from dismantled Russian World War II artillery shells.} (70\% copper and 30\% zinc) and plastic scintillators. The absorber material has a small nuclear interaction length\footnote{The nuclear interaction length $\lambda_I$ is the mean free path of hadrons between strong collisions.} ($\lambda_I \approx 16.4$~cm), so the HCAL remains compact while stopping the maximum of hadronic showers. The plastic scintillator is motivated by its fast response (within 1 ns). The HO consists of a plastic scintillator layer between the magnet's iron flux-return yokes, which serve as the absorber. Finally, the HF uses hard quartz fibres, alternating with steel absorber layers, instead of a plastic scintillator to handle the higher flux of energetic particles near the beam axis. In such fibres, ionising particles release their energy in the form of Cherenkov radiation, read out by photo-multiplier tubes \cite{CMShcal}. Note that in most materials the nuclear interaction length $\lambda_I$ is much larger than the radiation length $X_0$. As a result, electromagnetic showers develop over a much shorter distance scale than hadronic showers. This allows a compact electromagnetic calorimeter to be placed upstream, where electrons and photons are fully contained, while hadrons, which interact over the larger scale $\lambda_I$, predominantly shower in the downstream hadronic calorimeter.

Technically speaking, the HCAL granularity, as the ECAL's one, is defined by the segmentation of the wedges $\Delta \eta \times \Delta \phi$. The value depends on the part of the calorimeter: $0.087 \times 0.087$ (corresponding to a $5 \times 5$ array of ECAL crystals) for the HB and HO, $0.17 \times 0.17$ for the HE, and $0.175 \times 0.175$ for the HF.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{Images/Ch1/CMSHcalrun3.png}
    \caption{Layout of one quarter of a longitudinal slice of the CMS HCAL in the $r{-}z$ view, showing the location of the HCAL barrel (HB), endcap (HE), outer barrel (HO) and forward (HF) calorimeters. Within a tower, layers with the same colour are routed to the same silicon photomultiplier. The location of the frontend electronics is indicated by the letters FEE~\cite{CMShcal}.}
    \label{fig:CMShcal}
\end{figure}

During the Phase-1 upgrade, the HCAL underwent significant modernisation of its readout and electronics systems. Photodetectors and front-end electronics were replaced to enhance timing performance, dynamic range, and radiation tolerance. Specifically, silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs) were installed in the barrel and endcap regions, thereby increasing signal sensitivity and improving time resolution. The HCAL was also segmented in depth to increase the accuracy of energy and trajectory measurements of incoming particles.

\newpage

Similarly to the ECAL, the relative energy resolution for the HCAL, $\sigma_{\textrm{HCAL}}$, is given by \cite{CMShcalcrystals}:

\begin{equation}
    \left ( \frac{\sigma_{\textrm{HCAL}}}{E} \right)^2 = \left( \frac{0.943}{\sqrt{E}} \right)^2 + 0.084^2 \, .
    \label{eq:hcalresolution}
\end{equation}

The energy resolution of a hadronic calorimeter is significantly affected by invisible energy losses, arising from nuclear breakup, neutron production, nuclear excitation, and binding energy losses. Since this fraction of the shower energy does not generate detectable ionisation signals, it introduces intrinsic event-by-event fluctuations that degrade the calorimetric energy resolution.



\subsubsection{Solenoid magnet}

Charged particles with electric charge $q$ and velocity $\vec{v}$ bend when exposed to a magnetic field $\vec{B}$ according to the Lorentz force $\vec{F}=q\left( \vec{v} \times \vec{B}\right)$. Particle physicists use this property to reconstruct the tracks of such particles. 

Thus, the CMS solenoid is a central component of the CMS detector. The magnet consists of a 12.5~m-long superconducting coil with an external diameter of 6 m that produces a quasi-uniform magnetic field of 3.8~T along the $z$--axis within its volume. The coil itself is approximately 60~cm thick, and, in contrast to ATLAS, where the solenoid is located in front of the calorimeters, the CMS design places the magnet outside the calorimetric system. This configuration avoids additional material upstream of the ECAL and HCAL, thereby preserving the intrinsic calorimetric response and improving the overall reconstruction performance for electromagnetic objects and jets \cite{CMSmagnet}. The magnetic field variation map is shown in \cref{fig:CMSmagnet} and is used for precise momentum reconstruction.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth,trim={0 0 7cm 0},clip]{Images/Ch1/magnet.png}
    \caption{Colour map of the magnetic field $|B|$ generated by the solenoid magnet and the corresponding magnetic field lines for a longitudinal section of CMS \cite{CMSmagnet}.}
    \label{fig:CMSmagnet}
\end{figure}

The magnet itself is made of a metal alloy of Niobium and Titanium (NbTi). To reach the superconductivity regime, the magnet is cooled to 4.5~K by a liquid helium cryostat. Surrounding the solenoid, the iron return yoke\footnote{Interestingly, the magnet and the iron return yoke account for approximately 12\,500~t, or 89\% of the total mass of the CMS detector.} closes the magnetic flux and provides an approximately 2~T magnetic field within the muon detection system. The field strength and direction vary across the detector regions due to the yoke geometry and the magnetic flux return path.


\subsubsection{Muon chambers}

CMS's calorimeter system absorbs most Standard Model particles produced in collisions. However, muons can traverse the calorimeters with only limited energy loss, while neutrinos escape detection entirely due to their extremely weak interactions. Muons are frequently produced in many physical processes; thus, it is imperative to measure their properties accurately. With a mean lifetime of 2.2~$\mu\textrm{s}$ ($\approx$650~m in the particle frame), the particle is considered stable at the detector scale, meaning the muon goes through the entire sub-detector system. Moreover, because of their large mass (200 times heavier than an electron), they exhibit less bremsstrahlung, resulting in reduced energy loss along their path. Finally, although they leave hits in the tracker, muons cannot be identified directly from this information.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

With all that said, the muon system, or so-called muon chambers, is the last CMS sub-detector~\cite{CMSmuonTDR, CMSRun3detector}. It was designed to have three functions: efficient muon identification, precise momentum measurement, and triggering capability. The high-field solenoidal magnet and flux-return yoke enable these functions. Muon chambers are located outside the solenoid coil and the HO calorimeter. They are interspaced between the iron plates forming the return yoke of the magnet. A layout of the muon system is shown in \cref{fig:muonsystem}. It is divided into three sub-systems: the Drift Tubes (DTs) in the barrel region, the Cathode Strip Chambers (CSCs) in the forward region, and the Resistive Plate Chambers (RPCs). The Muon Barrel (MB) consists of five wheels along the beam pipe, each divided into twelve 30° segments to cover the volume of the CMS detector, while the Muon Endcap (ME) is divided into rings in the transverse plane.

Note that in 2017, an additional system was added in the inner endcap, namely the Gas Electron Multiplier system (GEM), to gain operational experience and perform a preliminary investigation of detector performance for the HL-LHC program~\cite{CMSmuonsystem}. However, this system has not yet been used for physics analyses.

\vspace{0.5cm}

Because the particle flux is significantly lower than in the inner tracker, the muon system can rely on less granular and more cost-effective gas detectors. It is composed of three subsystems, each based on a different detection technology, designed to cover large areas while maintaining a sufficiently fast response time.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Drift tubes}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

As the muon rate is lower in the barrel region, drift-tube technology has been chosen to cover this region (up to $|\eta|<1.2$). DTs are organised into four concentric cylinders, each composed of drift chambers (60 in the first three layers and 70 in the outermost layer). Drift chambers consist of rectangular drift cells of $44 \times 13 \, \textrm{mm}^2$ cross section and 2 to 3 metres long. They contain a 50 $\mu \textrm{m}$ gold-plated stainless-steel anode wire at the centre and are filled with a gas mixture containing 85\% Ar and 15\% $\textrm{CO}_2$ that is ionised when a muon traverses this volume. The resulting charged particles drift under the applied electric field toward the electrodes, where they are amplified to produce an electrical signal. The drift time of charges enables a transverse measurement relative to the wire, with a resolution of about 2.6 ns. To complete the information for one muon hit, multiple drift chambers are grouped into 4 layers of parallel cells --- called a superlayer (SL) ---, then a chamber is formed from 3 SLs: two with the cells parallel to the beam axis and one orthogonal. This improves the resolution in the $r-\phi$ and $r-z$ coordinates, giving spatial resolutions of 100 $\mu \textrm{m}$ and 500 $\mu \textrm{m}$, respectively.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

\noindent \textbf{Cathode strip chambers}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Located in the endcap, where particle flux is higher, the CSCs are dedicated detectors that address these conditions with faster response times. A CSC module consists of a trapezoidal chamber formed by 7 layers of cathode strip planes. Between layers, the gap is filled with a mixture of $\textrm{CO}_2$, Ar, and $\textrm{CF}_4$ ionised by energetic muons passing through the chamber. As in a drift chamber, an electric field is generated between the edges and the 50~$\mu$m wire anode in the middle of the chambers. Each wire is spaced by $\approx$3.15~mm, for a total of about $\approx$100--1000 wires per layer, allowing precise measurements in the $r{-}\phi$ plane with a response time of about 30~ns and a time resolution of about 2~ns. After all, 540~CSCs composed the four wheels (each made up of three rings in the first wheel and two in the others) to provide pseudorapidity coverage of $0.9<|\eta|<2.4$~\cite{CSCs}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Resistive plate chambers}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

RPCs are designed to address the need for very fast response times to keep up with the 25~ns bunch-crossing rate. Their detection principle is similar to the CSCs' one, a gas mixture (95.2\% $\textrm{C}_2\textrm{H}_2\textrm{F}_4$, 4.5\% isobutane $\textrm{C}_4\textrm{H}_{10}$ and 0.3\% $\textrm{SF}_6$) in a 2~mm gap is surrounded by parallel laminated bakelite plates. This offers a time resolution of about 3 ns at the cost of poorer spatial resolution, on top of containing fluorinated greenhouse gases\footnote{Both fluorinated components are potent greenhouse gases, making the RPC systems the largest contributor to the direct greenhouse gas emissions of the LHC experiments. Ongoing R\&D within the RPC aims at replacing it ahead of the HL-LHC~\cite{RPCecolo}.}. The system is composed of five wheels mounted with DTs in the MB and four in each endcap between CSC modules, totalling $480+576$ chambers \cite{RPCs}.


\subsection{Trigger system}

In the LHC design, bunch crossing occurs every 25~ns. Assuming an event can be stored in 1~MB of data, this would correspond to 40~TB/s of storage. This rate exceeds the CMS Data Acquisition System (DAQ)'s capacity, which records, processes, and stores events at nearly 100~MB/s. In addition, a fraction of collisions arise from low-energy proton--proton interactions, resulting in spurious physics events that do not need to be saved. To cope with the bunch-crossing rate, the CMS DAQ has adopted a two-level triggering system and a computer farm~\cite{CMStrigger}. 

The trigger system provides a quick selection of interesting events, reducing the 40~MHz input rate as low as 1~kHz for Run--2 to $\approx$2.6~kHz for Run--3. The trigger procedure is divided into two levels: a first level purely hardware, built by a dedicated custom-designed electronic system, and a second level software-based.

As pile-up increases, so does the particle flux. The trigger system is designed to be highly configurable: a \textit{trigger menu} is defined to reflect the experiment's physics goals, specifying which signatures are recorded and at what rate. Since most physics processes of interest have a significantly lower cross section than common QCD background processes, prescales are applied to some triggers to select only a fraction of these high-rate events, primarily for calibration and monitoring purposes, while preserving the full rate for rarer, high-value signatures.

\subsubsection{The level--1 trigger}

The level--1 (L1) trigger functions as a hardware system with a finite latency of 3.8 $\mu$s. The fixed latency is determined by the number of steps plus the data transfer time. During this time, based on signals from the calorimeters and muon chambers, the L1 system assesses the event and decides whether to accept or reject it.

The L1 trigger must deliver a decision every bunch crossing. A pipeline decision is therefore built, as illustrated in \cref{fig:L1pipeline}. Raw data are continuously stored in the front-end pipeline memory for approximately 152 bunch crossings (corresponding to a latency of 3.8 $\mu$s). Upon a trigger decision, data are either accepted and forwarded to a derandomiser buffer or rejected. The derandomiser converts the random arrival of accepted events into a steady readout stream. Two sources of dead-time must be controlled: a simple dead-time of 1\% arising from a 4 BC veto after each L1 accept, and a complex dead-time dependent on the derandomiser occupancy, required to remain below 1\% at 75 kHz and 6\% at 100 kHz trigger rate~\cite{CMStrigger}.

After all, event rates are reduced to 100 kHz and are then transferred to the HLT for further filtering.

\subsubsection{The high-level trigger}

The High-Level Trigger (HLT) is a software-based selection system that uses CPUs and GPUs to cope with increased luminosity. With a latency of 90 ms per event, it reduces the physics rate from L1 input of 100 kHz to a few kHz. Each event undergoes particle reconstruction for electrons, muons, photons, taus, and jets. To do so, a set of algorithms processes the data (HLT paths), each with a dedicated physics reconstruction that preserves topologies of interest. They are designed as a sequence of reconstruction steps and filters, such that an event that fails a step does not pass through all of them, thereby keeping the HLT decision flow optimised~\cite{CMShlt}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Monitoring the output rates of the L1 trigger and HLT is essential for assessing the overall performance of the trigger system, as these rates directly determine the data recorded for physics analyses. During data-taking, a dedicated shift crew oversees the smooth operation of the experiment, with each member assigned specific responsibilities. The trigger shifter monitors trigger rates in real time and adjusts prescales accordingly to maintain a stable output rate and prevent the data acquisition system from being overloaded. Since the start of Run--3, I have personally contributed to this effort by completing 27 8-hour shifts dedicated to monitoring the trigger system.


\subsection{Data storage}

Once the trigger system has assessed events of interest, they are encoded in a raw data format locally on disk. The data recorded by CMS is then distributed, stored, and analysed across a hierarchical computing infrastructure known as the Worldwide LHC Computing Grid (WLCG) \cite{WLCG} among CERN institutes, which are split into different tiers. Together, it combines about 1.4 million computer cores and 1.5 Exabytes ($1 \, \textrm{EB} = 10^6 \,\, \textrm{TB}$) of storage.

At the top of this hierarchy, Tier--0, located at CERN, is responsible for the initial backup of raw data and the first calibration and reconstruction pass. It provides 20\% of the WLCG resources with 800 PB of data storage. The processed data is then replicated to a set of Tier--1 centres, which provide long-term storage on tape and handle reprocessing campaigns as well as all types of data processing. Tier--2 centres, typically hosted at universities and research institutes, also perform all types of processing and serve as a resource for Monte Carlo simulation production. Finally, Tier--3 facilities, generally local computing clusters within research groups, are dedicated exclusively to physics analysis.

\subsubsection{The dataset format}

CMS data are produced and stored in several successive formats. These formats differ in event content and the level of reconstruction detail, reflecting a trade-off: formats with more reconstruction detail (such as RAW and RECO) provide more complete information but require significantly more storage space, while formats with less detail (such as \textsc{AOD}, \textsc{MiniAOD}, and \textsc{NanoAOD}) offer smaller file sizes at the expense of reduced event information and flexibility. The RAW format contains the detector readout and trigger information as recorded online. It is processed at Tier--0 into the RECO format. RECO includes the full event reconstruction (tracks, calorimeter clusters, particle-flow objects, primary vertices, etc.) and detailed detector-level information. This results in large event sizes ($\mathcal{O}$(MB/event)). A more compact format, \textsc{AOD} (Analysis Object Data), retains high-level reconstructed objects (leptons, jets, missing transverse momentum) together with a reduced set of tracking and calorimeter information. This typically reduces the size by about a factor of 5--10. The \textsc{MiniAOD} format~\cite{CMS:MiniAOD} further compresses the event content ($\approx$30--80 kB/event). It stores only the essential high-level physics objects, particle-flow candidates in a packed representation, and minimal auxiliary information needed for most analyses. Finally, \textsc{NanoAOD}~\cite{CMS:NanoAOD} provides an ultra-light format ($\approx$1--2 kB/event). It consists of a predefined set of high-level variables, optimised for analysis speed and portability, at the cost of limited flexibility~\cite{CMS:WorkBookDataFormats}.

\subsubsection{The parking and scouting datasets}

Beyond the standard data stream, two complementary strategies have been developed within CMS to extend its physics reach into regions of phase space that would otherwise be inaccessible with conventional trigger thresholds: \textit{data parking} and \textit{data scouting} \cite{ParkingAndScooting}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Data parking}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

The primary bottleneck in the CMS data-acquisition chain is not disk storage bandwidth, but rather the computing power required for prompt offline reconstruction at the Tier--0 facility. Data parking exploits this asymmetry: events passing dedicated HLT paths are written to tape in their full RAW format, but are not immediately reconstructed. Instead, their reconstruction is deferred to periods when computing resources are available, typically during LHC long shutdowns or between data-taking periods \cite{CMShlt}. This strategy allows trigger thresholds to be significantly lowered, since the per-event CPU cost of prompt reconstruction no longer constrains the storage rate. The parking stream operated at an additional rate of $\approx$3~kHz in Run--2, a rate maintained in Run--3, where reconstruction is now performed without delay using the former Run--2 HLT farm resources~\cite{ParkingAndScooting}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Data scouting}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

Data scouting addresses the same bandwidth constraint from a different angle. Rather than delaying reconstruction, scouting events are fully reconstructed online within the HLT farm and written to disk with a strongly reduced event content, consisting only of the high-level physics objects --- muons, jets, electrons, photons, and particle-flow candidates (since Run--3) --- relevant to the analysis, with no raw detector channel data retained. This reduction in event size, from $\approx$1~MB for a standard RAW event to $\approx$7~kB, allows the scouting stream to operate at roughly one order of magnitude higher rate than the standard stream~\cite{CMStrigger}. In Run--3, scouting data were recorded at rates of up to 30~kHz in 2022, corresponding to approximately 30\% of the L1 trigger output rate. This is made possible in part by a GPU-accelerated particle-flow reconstruction relying on pixel-only tracks built with the \textsc{Patatrack} algorithm, which reduced the average HLT processing time by over 40\%~\cite{ParkingAndScooting}. The physics programme enabled by scouting is particularly well suited to low-mass resonances, dark matter searches, and flavour-physics signatures at low transverse momentum, where standard trigger thresholds would otherwise suppress the signal entirely.



\section{Event reconstruction, identification and calibration}
\label{sec:EventReconstruction}

Among the various particles produced at each proton--proton collision, only a few of them are stable at the detector scale: the muons $\mu^\pm$, the electrons $e^\pm$, the charged hadrons (proton $p,\overline{p}$, pions $\pi^\pm$, kaons $K^\pm$), the neutral ones (neutron $n$, neutral kaon $K^0_L$), and the photons $\gamma$. Depending on the physics interaction, they leave different experimental signatures within the CMS detector. A representation of various particles travelling inside CMS is shown on \cref{fig:CMSslicePF}.

The CMS Particle Flow (PF) algorithm performs a complete reconstruction of events. This algorithm, detailed in the section below, reconstructs the basic particles listed above by looking for tracker hits, energy deposits within the calorimeters, or muon chamber hits. Afterwards, the PF particles are used to reconstruct more complex objects.

\subsection{Track reconstruction}

Identifying and labelling particles in the CMS detector requires knowledge of their trajectories and momenta. Thus, the reconstruction of tracks and primary vertices is a primordial step in particle identification. The collaboration has developed a Combinatorial Track Finder (CTF) \cite{CTF_track_cms, CERN_CTF} to achieve that. It relies on an iterative Kalman Filter (KF) \cite{KF} applied to the tracker and muon chamber hits.

Thanks to the magnetic field $B_z$ along the beam axis, particle trajectories are bent into helices whose axis is parallel to the beam. One can derive the expression for the transverse momentum $p_{\mathrm{T}}$ of a particle with an algebraic charge $z_e$ moving in such a field, knowing the radius of curvature of the helix $\mathcal{R}_\textrm{C}$:

\begin{align}
    p_{\mathrm{T}} \, [\textrm{GeV}/c] = 0.3 \cdot z_e \cdot B_z \, [\textrm{T}] \cdot \mathcal{R}_\textrm{C} \, [\textrm{m}]
    \label{eq:ptreconstruction}
\end{align}

Despite its elegance, the approach makes strong assumptions: particles move in a uniform magnetic field in a vacuum, without losing energy within matter. The multi-step CTF incorporates multiple hits within the tracker to reduce the number of fake tracks, i.e., incorrect trajectory reconstructions, while maintaining high tracking efficiency. This is done in 5 main steps: hit reconstruction, seed generation, iterative track reconstruction, ambiguity resolution, and final track fit.


\subsubsection{Hit reconstruction}
\label{subsubsection:Hitreconstruction}

Hit measurement relies on energy deposits of charged particles within the matter; the physics process of interaction between particles and matter is more detailed in \cref{ch:chapitre2}. These energy deposits or \textit{clusters} are grouped if the signal-over-noise ratio ($S/N$) is above a given threshold after amplification and \textit{zero suppression}\footnote{Zero suppression is a data reduction technique in which only channels with signals above a predefined threshold are recorded, while channels consistent with noise are discarded.} steps. Strip clusters are built if $S/N>3$ for a single strip, then if nearby strips have $S/N>2$, they are grouped, and a strip cluster is labelled if and only if the total signal of the cluster is five times greater than the square root of the sum of the RMS noise of each strip $i$ inside it ($S>5\cdot \sqrt{\sum_i N_{\mathrm{strip}\,i}^2}$).

A similar algorithm is applied for pixel hits. A cluster is defined as a pixel cell with $S/N>6$, and adjacent pixels are grouped if they satisfy $S/N>5$. Globally, the pixel cluster shall meet the $S/N>10$ criterion.

After all, the widths of the pixel hit residual distributions measured in Run--3 are about 40~$\mu$m and 75~$\mu$m in the $r{-}\phi$ and $z$ coordinates, respectively~\cite{pixelHitresolution}, while varying from 15~$\mu$m to 40~$\mu$m for the $r{-}\phi$ coordinate on strip modules, depending on the strip pitch~\cite{CMS-DP-2024-101}. Globally, the $S/N$ values evolve, decreasing with increasing integrated luminosity due to radiation damage~\cite{CMS-DP-2024-101}.

\subsubsection{Seed generation}

Once clustering is complete, the track's origin must be identified. The track \textit{seeding} consists of grouping upper and lower bordering hits by every possible pair of hits, mostly pixel hits due to their proximity to the IP. The pairs are then iteratively propagated outward to groups of three to four hits using KF.

\subsubsection{Iterative track reconstruction}

Once the origin of primary tracks is done, the CTF algorithm predicts the next hit during the iterative process. Even if \cref{eq:ptreconstruction} is not the one used, similar formulas with fewer hypotheses are implemented to interpolate the track parameter while measuring the sagitta\footnote{The sagitta of a circular arc is the distance from the midpoint of the arc to the midpoint of its chord.} of the trajectory in the transverse plane and the orientation of the helix. Track information is continuously updated at each newly added hit up to the last tracker layer, or if there are no further hits.

The CMS collaboration has developed new tools and algorithms to handle the higher integrated luminosity and compensate for the tracker's radiation damage. Due to increased pile-up interactions, the fake track rate has become a problem, reducing the effectiveness of the CTF algorithm used during Run--1 and Run--2. Thus, the mkFit tool has been developed and implemented for Run--3 to improve track-pattern recognition and reduce track-building time~\cite{mkfit}.

\subsubsection{Ambiguity resolution}

Ambiguities in the track-finding process arise when a seed yields more than one track candidate or when a track belongs to multiple seeds. To counter that, the CTF process evaluates the fraction of shared hits among nearby tracks. If this ratio exceeds a given threshold, the track with the fewest hits is discarded; if the total number of hits is the same, the track with the highest $\chi^2$ is rejected~\cite{CTF_track_cms}.

\subsubsection{Final track fit}
\label{sec:final_track_fit}

Once the procedure is complete, fits are performed, and spurious hits are removed, both are done iteratively until a certain level of convergence is reached. Final track candidates are then labelled based on the number of hits, the track's compatibility with each hit, the $\chi^2$ score of the track fit, or the distance between the track and the associated primary vertex (namely the \textit{impact parameter}). Finally, tracks are classified as \textit{loose}, \textit{tight}, or \textit{HighPurity}, with each consecutive label being a subset of the previous one and applying more stringent criteria.

This final step is also crucial for identifying interaction vertices. The fitted tracks are extrapolated to the beam spot and clustered in space to reconstruct proton–proton interaction vertices. Among them, the primary vertex (PV) associated with the hard-scattering process is defined, since Run--2, as the one with the largest scalar sum of squared transverse momenta, $\sum p_{\textrm{T}}^2$, computed from its associated tracks as well as from the reconstructed jets and missing transverse energy ($E_{\textrm{T}}^{\textrm{miss}}$). Secondary vertices are also reconstructed at this stage and can be identified as originating from the decays of long-lived particles such as $b$-hadrons or $\tau$-leptons. For Run--2 data, the PV transverse and longitudinal resolution is better than $14\,\mu\textrm{m} \times 19\,\mu\textrm{m}$~\cite{Run3_pv_resolution}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Track candidates produced by each successive CTF iteration must be classified as real or fake combinations of hits. Because this decision depends on several correlated quantities, a sequence of simple rectangular cuts cannot fully exploit these correlations and would need to be re-optimised for each iteration separately, given that the track topology and purity vary significantly from one iteration to the next. During Run--1 and Run--2, a Boosted Decision Tree (BDT) strategy\footnote{A BDT is a machine learning algorithm that combines multiple decision trees sequentially, where each new tree is trained to correct the errors of the previous ones, improving overall classification performance.} has therefore been adopted within the CTF algorithm, combining these track-quality variables into a single discriminant trained separately for each iteration. To further reduce the fake rate under the higher pileup and combinatorial background expected for Run--3, a Deep Neural Network (DNN) has been developed to replace the BDT, exploiting more complex, non-linear correlations between the input variables than the BDT can capture.

One can also address a limit on the track transverse momentum $p_{\mathrm{T}}$ that the CMS tracker can reconstruct. Slow-moving light particles loop in the inner tracker, resulting in poor $p_{\mathrm{T}}$ reconstruction, and tracking efficiency decreases for charged particles with $p_{\mathrm{T}}<1$~GeV/$c$. Moreover, ultra-relativistic particles are less deflected by the magnetic field, leading to inaccurate measurements of their sagitta and, consequently, their track momentum.

\subsubsection{Electron track reconstruction}

As discussed, electrons experience bremsstrahlung along their curved trajectories, resulting in greater energy loss. The Kalman Filter algorithm assumes Gaussian-like uncertainties, which is not valid for electrons due to the emission of high-energy photons. Therefore, after the seeding step, a Gaussian Sum Filter (GSF) algorithm is applied to properly account for sudden changes in the track trajectory and large energy losses in the detector material. This step-by-step procedure models the bremsstrahlung energy loss as a mixture of Gaussian components, thereby improving predictions of cluster shape and position.

\subsubsection{Muon track reconstruction}
\label{subsubsection:muon_track}

As muon particles pass through the entire detector, muon tracking uses information from the tracker and the muon chambers. Similarly, KFs are used to build seeds within the DTs and CSCs layers. Therefore, a second group of tracks arises after the seeding in the muon sub-systems, in addition to inner tracker tracks.

Tracks reconstructed exclusively using information from the muon chambers are referred to as \textit{standalone muons}. In contrast, candidates reconstructed solely in the tracker are labelled as \textit{tracker muons}. These originate from tracker tracks that are extrapolated to the muon system and loosely matched to DT and CSC segments. This approach reconstructs low-transverse-momentum muons, which are more likely to reach and match only the innermost muon station rather than multiple stations. \textit{Global muons}, on the other hand, are reconstructed by matching tracker and standalone muon tracks. The inner tracker track is extrapolated to the muon system and combined with a corresponding standalone muon candidate. This category is particularly relevant for muons with high transverse momentum ($p_{\mathrm{T}}\gtrsim200$~GeV/$c$), as it combines information from both tracking systems to improve the momentum measurement.

Despite this classification, cosmic muons produced by cosmic rays interacting with the Earth's atmosphere are not distinguished from muons originating from collision events. For example, \cref{fig:StandaloneANDGlobalmuon} presents two cosmic muons: one identified as a standalone muon (left) and the other tagged as two global muons (right). To exclude these anomalous trajectories, the transverse and longitudinal impact parameters, $d_{xy}$ and $d_z$, which quantify the distance of closest approach of the track to the primary vertex in the transverse plane and along the beam axis, respectively, are utilised. Muons from collision events typically exhibit small $d_{xy}$ and $d_z$ values, whereas cosmic muons, which do not originate from the interaction point, display significantly larger values for both parameters.

\vspace{0.5 cm}


\subsection{Particle-Flow algorithm}

The PF algorithm aims to reconstruct particles and objects from an event by combining information from the tracker, calorimeters, and muon chambers. The PF algorithm uses all low-level reconstructed objects from the sub-detectors to correlate measurements and thereby label particles and their kinematic properties~\cite{PFalgo}. Once a PF candidate is fully reconstructed, it is removed from the chain to reconstruct other PF elements.

\subsubsection{Muons}
\label{sec:muons}

Thanks to the muon track procedure described in \cref{subsubsection:muon_track}, the CMS achieves a muon reconstruction efficiency greater than 99\% for both tracker and global muons~\cite{CMSmuonPerf2018}. If their $p_{\mathrm{T}}$ is below 200~GeV/$c$, the transverse momentum is measured primarily from the inner-track fit. In contrast, for higher $p_{\mathrm{T}}$, the global fit performed by the Tracker Plus First Muon Station (TPFMS) allows a better measurement~\cite{MuonFitalgorithm}. A standard approach for offline muon momentum reconstruction is the TuneP algorithm~\cite{CMSmuonPerf2018}, which dynamically selects the most appropriate track fit depending on the available inner tracker and muon chamber information.

The track compatibility with one of three muon categories (tracker, standalone, or global muons) is assessed by looking at the $\chi^2$ score of the track fit and the number of hits. As for inner tracker tracks, muon tracks are also classified as a function of their quality using three different working points: \textit{loose}, \textit{medium}, or \textit{tight} muons, with each consecutive label being a subset of the previous one, with more stringent criteria.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The PF algorithm also provides dedicated isolation criteria for the muon reconstruction. The relative particle-flow isolation $I_{\textrm{rel}}^\mu$ quantifies the amount of hadronic and electromagnetic activity in a cone of radius $\Delta R<0.4$ around the muon direction, normalised by the muon transverse momentum $p_{\mathrm{T}}^\mu$. Its expression is given by:

\begin{equation}
    I_{\textrm{rel}}^\mu = \frac{1}{p_{\mathrm{T}}^\mu} \left[
        \sum\limits_{\substack{\textrm{ch.} \\ \textrm{had.}}} p_{\mathrm{T}} 
        + \max \left( 0 , 
            \sum\limits_{\substack{\textrm{neut.} \\ \textrm{had.}}} E_{\mathrm{T}} 
            + \sum\limits_{\textrm{ph.}} E_{\mathrm{T}} 
            - 
            0.5 \cdot \sum\limits_{\substack{\textrm{ch. had.} \\ \textrm{PU}}} p_{\mathrm{T}} 
        \right) \right]\, .
    \label{eq:PFisoMu}
\end{equation}

The numerator sums the transverse momenta of all charged hadrons (ch. had.), plus a pile-up-corrected neutral contribution. This neutral term, taken under the max operator, adds the neutral hadron (neut. had.) and photon (ph.) transverse energies while subtracting the estimated pile-up contribution, which approximates the neutral pile-up energy using the charged tracks associated with other primary vertices and considered as pile-up (ch. had. PU). The $\max(0,\cdot)$ ensures this corrected neutral term never goes negative, preventing unphysical values. A small value of $I_{\textrm{rel}}^\mu$ indicates an isolated muon, typical of \textit{prompt muons} coming from the primary vertex. In contrast, a large value suggests the muon is embedded in a jet.

A tracker-based isolation variable is similarly defined, relying solely on charged PF candidates with track information. The tracker isolation $I_{\textrm{trk}}^\mu$ sums the transverse momenta of charged PF candidates within a cone of radius $\Delta R < 0.3$ around the muon direction:
\begin{equation}
    I_{\textrm{trk}}^\mu = \sum_{\substack{i \,\in\, \Delta R < 0.3 \\ i \,\neq\, \mu}} p_{\mathrm{T},i}\, ,
    \label{eq:TrkisoMu}
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over all charged PF candidates $i$ within the cone, excluding the muon track itself. A smaller cone radius than that used for $I_{\textrm{rel}}^\mu$ is chosen to further reduce pile-up contamination. Rather than applying an explicit pile-up subtraction term as in \cref{eq:PFisoMu}, pile-up mitigation here relies directly on requiring that each track originate from the same primary vertex as the muon. The tracker isolation is a purely tracking-based, calorimeter-independent variable, where a small value of $I_{\textrm{trk}}^\mu$ indicates that the muon is well isolated from other charged activity, as expected for prompt muons. In contrast, a large value indicates that the muon is embedded in a jet.

Finally, a mini-isolation variable is built to target muons produced in the decay chain of a boosted object using \cref{eq:PFisoMu}, but with a variable cone radius given by:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta R = \left\{ \begin{array}{rcl}
        0.2 & \mbox{for} & p_{\mathrm{T}}^\mu<50 \, \textrm{GeV}/c \, ,\\ 
        \displaystyle \frac{10 \, \textrm{GeV}/c}{p_{\mathrm{T}}^\mu} & \mbox{for} & 50 \leq p_{\mathrm{T}}^\mu \leq 200 \, \textrm{GeV}/c \, ,\\
        0.05 & \mbox{for} & p_{\mathrm{T}}^\mu>200 \, \textrm{GeV}/c \, .
        \end{array}\right.
    \label{eq:coneRadius}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Muon reconstruction and identification performance is assessed in data using the \textit{tag-and-probe} method applied to $Z^0 \to \mu\mu$ and $J/\psi \to \mu\mu$ resonances, which provide unbiased muon samples with well-controlled kinematics. A well-identified \textit{tag} muon is paired with a loosely selected \textit{probe} muon to form a resonance candidate; the efficiency of a given selection is then measured on the probe. The ratio of efficiencies measured in data and simulation, known as a \textit{scale factor}, is used to correct the simulation in physics analyses. In Run--2, the reconstruction efficiency for tracker and global muons exceeded 99\% over the full detector acceptance~\cite{CMSmuonPerf2018}. For Run--3, the tight identification efficiency is typically above 95\% in the barrel and above 85\% in the endcap regions, as measured on 2022 and 2023 datasets~\cite{CMS-DP-2024-067}. The PF isolation efficiency for prompt muons passing tight identification is above 95\% and shows good stability against pile-up.

  

Residual biases in the transverse momentum measurement arising from detector misalignment and imperfect knowledge of the magnetic field are corrected using the Rochester correction algorithm~\cite{RochesterAlgorithm}, which derives $p_{\mathrm{T}}$-dependent correction factors in bins of charge, $\eta$, and $\phi$ by constraining the reconstructed $Z^0 \to \mu\mu$ invariant mass to the reference distribution obtained from simulation. The relative $p_{\mathrm{T}}$ resolution of muons with $20 < p_{\mathrm{T}} < 100$~GeV/$c$ is 1.3--2.0\% in the barrel and better than 6\% in the endcaps~\cite{CMSmuonPerf2012}. For Run--3, the momentum calibration has been updated using the 2022--2024 datasets.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Muon reconstruction plays a crucial role in searches for long-lived particles, as HSCPs are expected to behave as penetrating muon-like objects in the detector. Due to their large mass, HSCP candidates typically exhibit high transverse momentum and traverse the entire detector, reaching the muon system, similar to prompt muons. A precise and robust muon reconstruction is therefore essential to ensure high efficiency for HSCP candidates.


\subsubsection{Electrons and photons}

Electrons have a significant probability of showering as they traverse the CMS tracker. The reconstruction begins with the identification of seed crystals --- local energy maxima in the ECAL above a given threshold --- around which the energy deposits are aggregated into clusters using a deterministic annealing or island algorithm \cite{islandAlgorithm}. To account for the significant bremsstrahlung and conversion effects induced by the tracker material in front of the ECAL, these individual clusters are further merged into superclusters (SC), which extend in the $\phi$ direction to recover the energy radiated upstream. To do so, a hybrid algorithm is used in the barrel region and a \textit{multi $5\times5$} one in the endcaps~\cite{ElectronReconstruction}. The energy inside an SC is the sum of the collected energy within its crystals. SC are labelled as seeds if they satisfy a minimum transverse energy of 1~GeV and are isolated from other SCs. These seeds are then propagated to the inner tracker, where a GSF track is fitted and matched to the supercluster to identify the particle as an electron.

Electron identification is affected by fake candidates. Thus, a multivariate discriminant analysis (MVA) is trained to identify electrons and suppress background contributions by exploiting several discriminating variables, such as the ECAL shower shape, the HCAL energy fraction, the GSF track quality, and the relative isolation. This isolation is computed similarly to \cref{eq:PFisoMu}, using $p_{\mathrm{T}}^e$ instead and a fixed cone with $\Delta R<0.3$.

Electron identification is defined through the MVA score. The \textit{loose} working point ($\approx$90\% efficiency) is the least restrictive and is typically used for additional lepton vetoes or in regions with naturally low backgrounds. The \textit{medium} working point ($\approx$80\% efficiency) offers a balanced trade-off between signal efficiency and background rejection, making it a standard choice for generic electroweak measurements. Finally, the \textit{tight} working point ($\approx$70\% efficiency) applies the strictest selection, maximising background rejection at the cost of signal efficiency, and is reserved for analyses where sample purity is critical.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Unlike electrons, photons are reconstructed by looking for ECAL seeds with transverse energy greater than 10~GeV that are not associated with a track. A dedicated conversion recovery algorithm also attempts to reconstruct photons that have converted into an $e^+e^-$ pair before reaching the ECAL by looking for pairs of oppositely curved tracks that point back to the supercluster. The photon energy is then calibrated using simulation-based corrections, such as $Z^0 \to e^+e^-$ events. In the endcaps, detector-level rejection is achieved by the ES, which, due to its fine strip pitch, resolves the two collimated showers produced by a $\pi^0$ decay. In the barrel, where no preshower is present and decay photons are less collimated, photons are differentiated from other neutral particles, particularly $\pi^0 \to \gamma\gamma$ decays (the primary source of fake isolated photons), by employing shower-shape variables.


\subsubsection{Charged and neutral hadrons}

Muons, electrons, and photons reconstructed by the PF algorithm are removed to consider the other entities. At this stage of the process, only charged and neutral hadrons remain. They are mainly detected as \textit{jets}, which are collimated sprays of particles originating from the hadronisation\footnote{The hadronisation is the process by which quarks and gluons combine to form colour-neutral hadrons.} and fragmentation of quarks and gluons produced in the hard-scattering interaction. Since free quarks and gluons cannot propagate over macroscopic distances due to colour confinement, they undergo a parton shower\footnote{The parton shower is a cascade of successive gluon emissions and quark-antiquark pair productions.} before eventually forming hadrons. Various charged hadrons ($\pi^\pm$, $K^\pm$, $p,\overline{p}$) and neutral hadrons (neutrons, $K^0_L$) can therefore produce energy deposits in the HCAL.

Charged hadrons are identified by linking inner tracker tracks to matched HCAL clusters. When a track is geometrically associated with an HCAL cluster, the tracker-measured momentum is preferred over the calorimeter energy measurement, since the tracker provides superior resolution at low and moderate $p_{\mathrm{T}}$. If the calorimeter energy significantly exceeds the track momentum, the excess is attributed to an overlapping neutral component. Neutral hadrons, carrying no electric charge and therefore leaving no track, are identified solely from HCAL energy deposits that cannot be associated with any charged track. The energy of neutral hadrons is measured exclusively from the calorimeter, making their reconstruction inherently less precise than that of charged hadrons. As a consequence, roughly 65\% of the energy of a central PF jet at $p_{\mathrm{T}} \approx 100$~GeV/$c$ is carried by charged hadrons, about 25\% by photons, and the remaining 10\% by neutral hadrons~\cite{ChargedNeutralHadronPerf}.



\subsubsection{Jets}

On average, approximately 65\% of the energy in a jet is in the form of charged particles (mostly $\pi^\pm$), 25\% of the energy is in the form of photons from $\pi^0 \to \gamma\gamma$ decays, and 10\% is in the form of neutral hadrons (mostly neutrons and $K_L$s)~\cite{Thomson_2013}. Thus, identifying PF muons, electrons, photons, and charged and neutral hadrons is essential for jet reconstruction.

In the CMS detector, jets are reconstructed from the PF candidates using the anti--$k_t$ clustering algorithm~\cite{AntiKT_cms}. This algorithm is infrared-safe\footnote{Infrared safety ensures that the jet definition is not altered by the emission of soft (low-energy) particles, meaning that adding an arbitrarily soft gluon to the event does not change the resulting jet configuration.} and collinear-safe\footnote{Collinear safety ensures that the jet definition is not affected by the collinear splitting of a particle into two particles carrying the same total momentum, meaning that replacing one particle by two collinear particles does not change the resulting jet configuration.}. To mitigate the effects of PU, the Charged Hadron Subtraction (CHS) process~\cite{CHS_cms} excludes from the computation charged PF candidates that do not originate from the PV. Anti--$k_t$ algorithm defines a distance between two objects $i$ and $j$ or between the object $i$ and the beam axis:

\begin{equation}
    d_{i,j}=\left\{ \begin{array}{ccl}
    \displaystyle \min \left( p_{\mathrm{T},i}^{2k}  \, , \, p_{\mathrm{T},j}^{2k} \right)\cdot \frac{\Delta R_{i,j}^2}{R^2} & \mbox{if} & i \neq j \\ 
    \displaystyle p_{\mathrm{T},i}^{2k} & \mbox{if} & i=j
    \end{array}\right.
    \label{eq:antikt}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $k=-1$ controls the clustering behaviour in the anti--$k_t$ method, $\Delta R$ is the distance in the $\eta{-}\phi$ plane, and $R$ is the distance parameter that defines the cone size over which the PF candidates are aggregated. Two types of jets are then considered, AK4 and AK8 jets, with $R = 0.4$ or $R = 0.8$. The anti--$k_t$ algorithm proceeds iteratively over the list of PF candidates. At each iteration, the global minimum over all $d_{i,j}$ and $d_{i,i}$ is identified. If the minimum is a pair distance $d_{i,j}$, the two particles $i$ and $j$ are merged into a single pseudo-particle by summing their four-momenta. If instead the minimum is a beam distance $d_{i,i}$, particle $i$ is declared a jet and removed from the list. This procedure is repeated until no particles remain. The consequence of setting $k=-1$ is that soft particles are preferentially clustered around hard seeds first. Note that, independently of the value of $k$, for high-energy jets, the separation between individual particles is typically smaller than the calorimeter segmentation, and not all particles in the jet can be resolved.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Pile-up contributions degrade jet resolution, especially for low-$p_{\mathrm{T}}$ jets, reducing the jet energy resolution. This effect is even more pronounced for Run--3 data, as the average pile-up tends to increase with year, reaching $\langle \textrm{PU} \rangle=60$ in 2024, 2025 and 2026. To make this work, CMS has developed the Pile-Up Per Particle Identification (PUppi)~\cite{PUPPI_cms}, which is more stable and efficient than CHS in high-pile-up scenarios. PF candidates are weighted by their probability of arising from pile-up before applying the anti--$k_t$ method. 

At this point, two classes of jets are created: AK4PF jets (resulting from the CHS process) and AK4 PUppi jets, the latter of which are recommended by CMS for Run--3 physics analysis.

Jet energies are then corrected through a series of jet energy corrections (JEC) applied sequentially. Relative and absolute corrections account for non-uniformities in the detector response as functions of $\eta$ and $p_{\mathrm{T}}$, thereby bringing the jet energy scale to a uniform, well-calibrated reference. Finally, residual corrections are implemented to the data to account for remaining discrepancies between the data and the simulation, derived from dedicated in situ measurements such as the $p_{\mathrm{T}}$ balance in $\gamma$+jet, $Z^0$+jet, and dijet events. After the full JEC chain, the jet energy resolution (JER) is also smeared in simulation to match the resolution observed in data~\cite{CMS-DP-2026-034}. The latest results show that the jet energy scale is stable over Run--3, at the order of a percent~\cite{CMS-DP-2025-042, CMS-DP-2025-044}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

While the previously introduced jets are reconstructed from PF candidates, an alternative reconstruction can be performed using energy deposits in the calorimeters (ECAL and HCAL), yielding the so-called \textit{CaloJets}. In this case, the inputs to the anti--$k_t$ clustering algorithm are calorimeter towers, which are projective groupings of ECAL and HCAL cells, rather than individual PF candidates. CaloJets are generally less precise than PF-based jets due to the coarser granularity of the calorimeters and the absence of tracker information, leading to poorer jet energy resolution and reduced ability to resolve nearby particles. However, their low latency makes them particularly well-suited for the L1 trigger, where the full PF reconstruction is not yet available, and fast calorimetric information alone must be used to make the trigger decision.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Reconstructed jets can be further classified by parton flavour using dedicated tagging algorithms. Heavy-flavour jets originating from $b$ or $c$ quarks are characterised by the presence of long-lived hadrons that travel a measurable distance before decaying, producing secondary vertices displaced from the primary interaction point and tracks with large impact parameter significance. These signatures, combined with additional variables such as the jet constituent kinematics and the presence of soft leptons, are exploited by multivariate algorithms to discriminate heavy-flavour jets from light-flavour ones. The Run--2 reference algorithm in CMS is \textsc{DeepJet}~\cite{DeepJet}, a deep neural network that processes the full list of jet constituents and returns a flavour probability score, against which a working point is applied to define the final jet flavour tag. For Run--3, \textsc{DeepJet} has been superseded by the Unified Particle Transformer (\textsc{UParT})~\cite{UParT}, a transformer-based architecture that introduces pairwise interaction features between jet constituents and secondary vertices, and employs adversarial training to improve robustness against simulation mismodelling. Beyond $b$- and $c$-jet tagging, \textsc{UParT} performs quark-gluon separation and hadronic $\tau$ classification within a single unified model. The model also incorporates an initial attempt at $s$-jet identification by dividing the standard light-flavour training class into distinct $u$, $d$, and $s$ categories~\cite{UParT}. This $s$-jet classifier is currently exploratory, achieving an $s$-jet efficiency of approximately 20\% with a misidentification probability of $10^{-3}$, and is not utilised in my present analysis.


\subsubsection{Tau leptons}

The tau lepton decays before reaching the innermost tracker layer and must therefore be reconstructed entirely from its decay products. Leptonic decays ($\approx$35\%) are recovered through the standard electron and muon reconstruction described above. Hadronic decays ($\approx$65\%) yield final states with one or three charged pions and zero, one, or two $\pi^0$, and are reconstructed by the Hadron-Plus-Strip (HPS) algorithm~\cite{TauPaper}. PF jets serve as seeds; for each jet, charged PF hadrons are combined with dynamic $\eta \times \phi$ windows grouping electrons and photons that reconstruct the $\pi^0 \to \gamma\gamma$ signature, to test compatibility with four decay mode hypotheses: $h^\pm$, $h^\pm\pi^0$, $h^\pm\pi^0\pi^0$, and $h^\pm h^\mp h^\pm$. Candidates with charge other than $\pm 1$ or with constituents outside a $p_{\mathrm{T}}$-dependent signal cone are rejected.

Since the resulting $\tau_h$ candidates are easily confused with quark- or gluon-induced jets, electrons, and muons, a dedicated identification step follows. Since the end of Run--2, the \textsc{DeepTau} algorithm~\cite{DeepTau}, based on a convolutional neural network that combines high-level candidate variables with low-level PF information from the tracker, calorimeters, and muon system, provides four output scores against which working points are defined. For Run--3, the updated version incorporates a domain adaptation subnetwork to reduce sensitivity to simulation mismodelling~\cite{DeepTauRun3}. Jet-level taggers such as \textsc{UParT} also provide $\tau_h$ classification and are currently under evaluation as alternative reconstruction and identification chains~\cite{CMS-DP-2025-073}. However, \textsc{DeepTau} applied to HPS candidates remains the standard in Run--3, as it operates on fully reconstructed $\tau_h$ candidates with identified decay modes and offers separately calibrated discriminants against jets, electrons, and muons.


\subsubsection{Missing transverse energy}
\label{subsubsec:METdef}

Commonly named MET (for Missing $E_{\mathrm{T}}$), the missing transverse energy results from taking the magnitude of the missing transverse momentum, which is the negative vectorial sum of the transverse momentum of all reconstructed objects:

\begin{equation}
    E^{\mathrm{miss}}_{\mathrm{T}} = \left\|\overrightarrow{p_{\mathrm{T}}^\textrm{miss}} \right\|\, ,
    \label{eq:metEnergy}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    \overrightarrow{p_{\mathrm{T}}^\textrm{miss}} = -\sum_i \overrightarrow{p_{\mathrm{T},i}} \, .
    \label{eq:metpT}
\end{equation}

As the momentum of colliding partons is mostly longitudinal, this vectorial sum in the transverse plane is supposed to be close to zero if all particles are detected. MET probes the presence of weakly or non-interacting particles such as neutrinos, or possibly new physics phenomena. Note that it cannot reveal the exact number of invisible particles, but exclusively the magnitude of the missing $E_{\mathrm{T}}$. In addition, MET may originate from detector inefficiency, limited acceptance, miscalibrations, or PF candidates reconstructed with high kinematic uncertainties.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The MET definition introduced previously refers to PF-based missing transverse energy (PF MET), which uses information from all sub-detectors. In contrast, the calorimeter MET (Calo MET) is defined as the negative vectorial sum of the energies of all calorimeter towers (in the ECAL and HCAL) projected onto the transverse plane. Calo MET is therefore only sensitive to the electromagnetic and hadronic activity of the event. Particles that traverse the calorimeters while depositing only a small fraction of their energy, such as muons or HSCPs, are essentially invisible to it. For events containing such particles, Calo MET and PF MET can differ significantly. PF MET accounts for their momentum through the tracker and muon system, whereas Calo MET treats it as missing. The two quantities thus exhibit a global imbalance at the event level (discussed in \cref{sec:trigger}).

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Closely related to the missing transverse energy are several scalar quantities derived from the event's hadronic activity. The scalar sum of the transverse momenta of all selected jets $H_{\mathrm{T}}$ quantifies the total hadronic energy in the event:
\begin{equation}
    H_{\mathrm{T}} = \sum_{\mathrm{jets}} \left\|\overrightarrow{p_{\mathrm{T}_j}}\right\| \, .
    \label{eq:ht}
\end{equation}
By analogy with the MET, the missing $H_{\mathrm{T}}$ (MHT) is defined as the magnitude of the negative vectorial sum of the transverse momenta of the selected jets:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{MHT} = H_{\mathrm{T}}^\mathrm{miss} = \left\| -\sum_{\mathrm{jets}} \overrightarrow{p_{\mathrm{T}_j}} \right\| \, .
    \label{eq:mht}
\end{equation}
Unlike MET, which is built from all PF candidates, MHT relies exclusively on reconstructed jets and is therefore more sensitive to jet selection and thresholds. A variant, MHT$^\mathrm{NoMu}$, is computed by excluding muons from the vectorial sum, so that real muon activity does not contribute to the MHT.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Since the PF MET is built from the vectorial sum of all PF candidates, any miscalibration of the jet energy scale propagates directly into it. The standard correction procedure, referred to as \textit{Type-1}, propagates the full JEC chain to the PF MET by replacing the raw jet momenta with their corrected counterparts in the sum~\cite{METcalib}. An additional \textit{XY-shift} correction is then applied to remove a spurious sinusoidal modulation of the MET azimuthal angle $\phi$, arising from anisotropic detector response, inactive calorimeter cells, detector misalignment, and beam-spot displacement~\cite{METcalib}. The recommended MET estimator for Run--3 physics analyses is the PUppi-based MET (PUppi MET), which benefits from the pile-up weighting applied at the particle level before jet clustering, resulting in improved resolution and reduced sensitivity to pile-up fluctuations compared to the PF MET.



\subsubsection{Kinematic measurements using the Particle-Flow algorithm}
\label{sec:PFvsTrack}

A track candidate is the result of the pattern-recognition and fitting procedure applied to the hits recorded by the inner tracker. It characterises the trajectory of a charged particle through the magnetic field, and provides a direct measurement of its kinematics: $p_{\mathrm{T}}$, $\eta$ and $\phi$. A track candidate carries no hypothesis (except the particle charge to be $|Q|=1$) on the particle species: it is a purely geometric and kinematic object.

A PF candidate, by contrast, is the outcome of the global event reconstruction algorithm described above. As a consequence, the kinematics associated with a PF candidate depends on its assigned type~\cite{PFalgo}. For charged hadrons, the PF algorithm uses both tracker and calorimeter information, ensuring good PF-$p_{\mathrm{T}}$ reconstruction even at low $p_{\mathrm{T}}$. For electrons, $p_{\mathrm{T}}$ is derived from the track curvature and the energy deposited in the ECAL. Muons rely almost exclusively on tracker and muon-system measurements, as they are essentially non-interacting in the calorimeters.

This type dependence also governs the reconstruction efficiency. Isolated muons are reconstructed with an efficiency close to 99\%. Charged hadrons, by contrast, are reconstructed with substantially lower efficiency using solely the reconstructed track, reaching only 70--80\% for tracks with transverse momenta above 1~GeV/$c$~\cite{PFalgo}, as their interactions with the detector material and nuclear interactions in the tracker can fail the track reconstruction. By combining tracker and calorimeter information for the assigned particle type, the PF approach recovers part of this loss. It yields a more robust identification of charged hadrons than the track measurement alone. This is illustrated in \cref{fig:PFcompTrack}, which compares the $p_{\mathrm{T}}$, $\eta$ and $\phi$ obtained from the PF algorithm and from the track reconstruction alone on the 2024 MET \textsc{MiniAOD} dataset without any upstream selections. The angular variables $\eta$ (bottom left) and $\phi$ (bottom right) are in close agreement between the two approaches, since both rely on the same inner tracker geometry; a few tracks are nevertheless misplaced at $|\phi|=\pi/4$ and $3\pi/4$. The transverse momentum, on the other hand, can differ significantly depending on the PF type: for muons (top left), the PF-$p_{\mathrm{T}}$ agrees with the reconstructed track-$p_{\mathrm{T}}$ most of the time, except at low PF-$p_{\mathrm{T}}$ where the track-$p_{\mathrm{T}}$ is overestimated as it relies solely on the silicon tracker information; for charged pions (top right), the track-$p_{\mathrm{T}}$ is poorly reconstructed at low PF-$p_{\mathrm{T}}$ compared to their high-PF-$p_{\mathrm{T}}$ counterparts, for the reasons discussed above.

\section{Monte Carlo simulation}
\label{sec:MC}

Monte Carlo (MC) simulations are central to high-energy physics analyses. They model both the signal (events of interest) and the background (other processes that mimic the signal). MC simulations allow evaluation of detector acceptance (the fraction of events detected), reconstruction efficiencies, and systematic uncertainties. The simulation chain reproduces the full evolution of a proton--proton collision, from the hard interaction to detector response.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Within the simulation chain, the hard-scattering process is first described using perturbative quantum chromodynamics (pQCD), in which the scattering amplitudes are expanded in powers of the strong coupling constant $\alpha_s$. Possible interactions are represented by Feynman diagrams, pictorial representations of particle interactions from which the corresponding matrix elements\footnote{Matrix elements are expressions that determine the probability amplitude for a given process.} are calculated. The cross section of a given process is obtained by integrating the squared matrix element over all possible final states and then convolving the result with the proton parton distribution functions (PDFs), which describe the probability of finding a specific parton carrying a fraction of the proton's momentum.

Event generators such as \textsc{MadGraph5\_aMC@NLO}~\cite{Alwall:2014hca} compute the hard-process matrix elements and generate the outgoing particles. The subsequent evolution of the event, including initial- and final-state radiation, parton showering, and hadronisation, is typically handled by \textsc{Pythia}~\cite{Sjostrand:2014zea}. During this stage, coloured partons evolve into parton showers and eventually form colour-neutral hadrons through phenomenological hadronisation models.

After hadronisation, the interaction of the produced particles with the detector material is then simulated with \textsc{Geant4}~\cite{Agostinelli:2003hh} within the CMS software (CMSSW). \textsc{Geant4} models the passage of particles through matter, and the detector response (digitisation) is then emulated by CMSSW. This toolkit models the detector response (signal digitisation) of particles travelling through matter. The resulting simulated detector signals are processed with the same reconstruction algorithms as collision data, allowing simulated events to be analysed in the same way as recorded data.


\section[CMS Phase-2 upgrade for HL-LHC campaigns]{CMS Phase-2 upgrade for HL-LHC \\ campaigns}
\label{sec:HLLHC}

On June 14 2026, the last collisions took place in the LHC before LS3. This initiates the Phase-2 upgrade of the CMS detector to sustain the future high-luminosity LHC program, namely the HL-LHC, expected to last until 2041. The instantaneous luminosity is predicted to be at $5{-}7.5 \times 10^{34} \, \textrm{cm}^{-2}\,\textrm{s}^{-1}$, which, integrated over the ten-year period of time, would give an integrated luminosity of 3\,000 $\textrm{fb}^{-1}$ \cite{HLLHCplans}. 

This upgrade will result in an increase in pile-up up to 200 simultaneous interactions per bunch crossing. Therefore, most detector upgrades aim to improve granularity to handle this particle flux and higher radiation levels.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Silicon tracker}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

An all-silicon detector will fully replace the current inner tracker, offering increased granularity and a reduced material budget. A sketch of the future silicon tracker is shown in \cref{fig:phase2tracker}, its pseudorapidity coverage will be significantly extended from $|\eta| < 2.5$ to $|\eta| < 4$, improving the reconstruction of forward tracks and the pile-up rejection capabilities in the forward region~\cite{Phase2Tracker}. In addition, the electronics and data transfer will be optimised to use tracker information for the L1 trigger~\cite{Phase2L1trigger}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The Phase-2 inner tracker will continue to provide energy-loss-per-unit-length $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x$ measurements using time-over-threshold readout. In contrast, the outer tracker will switch to a binary readout, losing the analogue pulse-height information. To partially compensate for this loss and retain sensitivity to highly ionising particles such as HSCPs, a dedicated high-ionisation pixel (HIP) flag has been implemented specifically in the pixel-strip (PS) modules (the innermost part of the outer tracker) while the strip-strip (2S) modules, further out, remain purely binary. A hit is flagged as HIP when its deposited charge exceeds a programmable, per-module threshold, initially set at 1.4 times the minimum-ionising-particle (MIP) value in simulation studies~\cite{Phase2Tracker}. Tracks that accumulate a significant number of HIP-flagged clusters in the PS layers can then be distinguished from standard minimum-ionising background particles, providing an effective $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x$-like discriminator for HSCP searches in the region covered by PS modules, even in the absence of full analogue readout.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Barrel calorimeters}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

The front-end and back-end electronics of the barrel calorimeters will be replaced to cope with the increased data rates. The ECAL will additionally benefit from an improved timing resolution of 30~ps for electrons and photons, enabling time-of-flight-based pile-up discrimination~\cite{CERN-LHCC-2017-011}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Endcap calorimeters -- HGCAL}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

The existing endcap calorimeters will be replaced by the High-Granularity Calorimeter (HGCAL), a sampling calorimeter providing three-dimensional shower reconstruction with precise timing information at the 30~ps level. Its fine transverse and longitudinal segmentation enables a so-called ``5D'' particle flow reconstruction\footnote{The five dimensions correspond to the reconstruction of the three spatial coordinates ($x$,$y$,$z$), the deposited energy $E$ and the arrival time $t$.} of jets, electrons, and photons, with significantly improved pile-up rejection \cite{Phase2HGCAL}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{MIP timing detector}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

A new dedicated timing layer, the MIP Timing Detector (MTD), will be installed between the tracker and the calorimeters, covering $|\eta| < 3$. It will provide a time-of-flight resolution of 30--60~ps per track, enabling assignment of each charged particle to its originating bunch-crossing vertex and thereby substantially reducing the impact of pile-up on reconstruction~\cite{Phase2MTD}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Muon system}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

The muon system will be equipped with new front-end and back-end electronics, and its pseudorapidity acceptance will be extended from $|\eta| < 2.4$ to $|\eta| < 2.8$ by adding new GEM detector stations in the forward region~\cite{CERN-LHCC-2017-012}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Trigger, DAQ and PF algorithm}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

The level--1 trigger will be deeply redesigned to incorporate tracking information already at the hardware trigger stage, enabling more refined online selections. The L1 output rate will be increased from 100 kHz to 750 kHz, and the HLT output rate from 2.6 kHz to 7.5 kHz, to accommodate the higher event complexity expected under HL-LHC conditions \cite{Phase2L1trigger}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The inclusion of tracking information at the L1 stage will enable the PF algorithm to be extended to the hardware-trigger level. A dedicated \textit{correlator trigger} will combine the output of the global track trigger with calorimeter and muon primitives to perform a PF~+~PUppi reconstruction online, significantly lowering the trigger thresholds for jets and MET at fixed output rate compared to calorimeter-only or track-only approaches~\cite{Phase2L1trigger}.
