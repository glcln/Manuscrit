\section{Natural units and conventions}
\label{sec:units}

The International System of Units (SI) is used in macroscopic sciences and groups the second (s, the unit of time), metre (m, length), kilogram (kg, mass), ampere (A, electric current), kelvin (K, thermodynamic temperature), mole (mol, amount of substance), and candela (cd, luminous intensity). However, when it comes to small, fast, and highly energetic objects, manipulating those units might not be relevant (because they are too small or have too high values). Therefore, another set of units is preferred in particle physics to reflect the natural length and time scales encountered: the \textit{natural units}. Thus, [kg, m, s] is replaced by [$\hbar,\,c,$ GeV]. For instance, mass are now expressed in GeV/$c^2$ and time in $\mathrm{GeV}^{-1}\hbar^{-1}$. To simplify algebraic expressions, $\hbar$ and $c$ are set to unity. Taking the natural units, combined with the Heaviside-Lorentz ones to get Maxwell's equations taking the same form as with SI units, we get $\hbar=c=\varepsilon_0=\mu_0=1$, leading to:
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{[energy]=[mass]=[momentum]=[time]^{-1}=[length]^{-1}=eV}
\end{equation*}
where the electronvolt (eV) is the unit of choice. In these units, a proton has a mass of $m_p=1.673\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}=938\times10^6\,\rm eV = 938\,\mathrm{MeV}$.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

From now on, natural units will be the default. 


\section{The Standard Model of particle physics}
\label{sec:sm}

\subsection{General overview}

The Standard Model (SM) of particle physics~\cite{Glashow1961, Weinberg1967, Salam1968} is the theoretical framework that describes all known elementary particles and their interactions, except gravity. Built on the mathematical foundation of quantum field theory and local gauge symmetries, it describes three of the four fundamental forces: the electromagnetic, weak, and strong interactions, described by the gauge group $\mathrm{SU}(3)_c \otimes \mathrm{SU}(2)_L \otimes \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$. Developed primarily during the 1960s and 1970s, the SM was completed with the discovery of the Higgs boson by the ATLAS and CMS
collaborations in 2012~\cite{ATLAS_Higgs, CMS_Higgs}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Elementary particles are classified into two categories according to their spin. Fermions carry half-integer spin and constitute the matter content of the universe, while bosons carry integer spin and mediate the fundamental interactions.

\subsubsection{Gauge bosons}

The gauge bosons mediate the fundamental interactions. The photon $\gamma$, the massless mediator of the electromagnetic interaction, couples to all electrically charged particles. The weak interaction is mediated by the massive $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ bosons, whose masses arise from spontaneous electroweak symmetry breaking, as discussed in \cref{subsection:BEH}. The strong interaction is mediated by eight massless gluons, which carry colour charge and therefore interact among themselves in addition to coupling to quarks. An additional scalar field, the Higgs boson $H$, is responsible for the electroweak symmetry breaking and the generation of the charged fermion masses. 

\subsubsection{Fermions}

The fermions are arranged in three generations with the same quantum numbers but different masses, each containing two quarks and two leptons.

Leptons are fermions which are not affected by the strong interaction. Each generation contains a charged lepton and an associated neutrino: the electron $e$ and $\nu_e$, the muon $\mu$ and $\nu_\mu$, and the tau $\tau$ and $\nu_\tau$. The charged leptons carry an electric charge of $-1$ and interact via the electromagnetic and weak interactions, while neutrinos are electrically neutral and interact only via the weak interaction. 

Quarks, on the other hand, are fermions that are sensitive to all SM interactions. Each generation contains an up-type quark of electric charge $+2/3$ and a down-type quark of electric charge $-1/3$: the up $u$ and down $d$, the charm $c$ and strange $s$, and the top $t$ and bottom $b$. Due to colour confinement, quarks are never observed alone but are bound into colour-neutral composite objects called hadrons. The top quark is an exception, as its very short lifetime means it decays before hadronisation can occur.

Every fermion has an associated antiparticle of opposite electric charge. All charged particles of the second and third generations are unstable and decay to lower-mass states through weak interactions.



\subsection{Foundations: quantum field theory}

As the SM describes elementary particles at high energy scales, its formalism relies on quantum mechanics, special relativity, and field theory. 

Quantum mechanics, in its original formulation, is a non-relativistic theory. The Schrödinger equation treats time and space asymmetrically and assumes that the number of particles is fixed. This framework becomes inconsistent at high energies, where special relativity must be taken into account.

One needs to consider special relativity when dealing with particles moving at speeds far beyond those encountered in everyday life. This formalism imposes that the rest energy and mass are related by $E=mc^2$, so that when the energy of a process exceeds the rest mass energy of a particle, there is sufficient energy to create new particle-antiparticle pairs from the vacuum. The particle number is therefore no longer conserved, and a single-particle wave function $\psi(\mathbf{x},t)$ is no longer an adequate description of the system.

The natural resolution is to get rid of the wave function as a fundamental object and instead use the dynamical variable as a field $\phi(\mathbf{x},t)$, defined at every point in space-time, so that particle creation and annihilation are naturally accommodated as excitations of the underlying field.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

These considerations form the foundation of quantum field theory (QFT). In this framework, the dynamics are governed by a Lagrangian density $\mathcal{L}(\phi_i, \partial_\mu \phi_i)$, and the central object is the action
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{S} = \int \mathcal{L}\left( \phi_i, \partial_\mu \phi_i \right) \, \mathrm{d}^4x \,,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}$ must be a scalar for $\mathcal{S}$ to be invariant under Lorentz transformations. The principle of least action, $\delta \mathcal{S} = 0$, then yields the Euler-Lagrange equations of motion,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \phi_i} = \partial_\mu \left( \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \left(\partial_\mu \phi_i\right)} \right),
\end{equation}
which governs the classical evolution of each field $\phi_i$. 

Quantisation is subsequently achieved by imposing canonical commutation and anti-commutation relations on the fields and their conjugate momenta, extending the classical field theory to a full quantum field theory.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

From the Euler-Lagrange equations, one could extract continuous quantities. This is achieved using Emmy Noether's theorem, presented in 1915 \cite{Noether1918}, which states that every continuous symmetry of the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}$ leads to a conserved quantity. Canonical examples include time-translation invariance, which implies energy conservation; spatial-translation invariance, which implies momentum conservation; and rotational invariance, which implies conservation of angular momentum. This result is particularly important in the context of gauge invariance, as it provides the fundamental link between local symmetries and the conservation laws that support modern quantum field theories.


\subsection{Free fields and wave equations}

Describing the behaviour of a field interacting with other fields in space-time cannot be handled using the Schrödinger equation, because the asymmetry between the first derivative in time and the second derivative in space makes it non-Lorentz-invariant. Therefore, this equation is irrelevant for describing relativistic particles.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The foundation of relativistic quantum mechanics started with Oskar Klein and Walter Gordon, who introduced in 1926 the Klein-Gordon equation that took the Einstein relation dispersion, $E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2+m^2$, and applied it to a field $\phi_i(\textbf{x},t)$ as an upgraded version of the Schrödinger equation, taking the relativistic aspect. This led to the Klein-Gordon (KG) equation, which describes the behaviour of a scalar (spin-0) particle with a non-zero mass. 

Despite the apparent success of combining quantum mechanics and special relativity, the KG equation yields a probability density $\rho=2|N|^2E$ that can be negative depending on the sign of $E$, with $N$ the normalisation factor. As the dispersion relation $E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2+m^2$ allows negative-energy solutions, it is nonsense for physical objects with negative energy to have a negative probability of being present.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

This led to Paul Dirac's 1928 work, which resolved the issue by considering an energy-mass-momentum equation that included a first-order derivative. The new dispersion relation becomes $E\mathds{1}_{4\times4}=\mathbi{\alpha}_{4\times4}\cdot\textbf{p}+\beta_{4\times4}m$, where $\alpha_i=\alpha_{4\times4\,,\,i}$ and $\beta=\beta_{4\times4}$ are $4\times4$ matrices and $\mathds{1}$ the identity matrix. After working out the details of the calculations, the matrices can be expressed as follows:

\begin{equation}
    \alpha_i = \left( \begin{array}{cc} 0 & \sigma_i \\ \sigma_i & 0 \end{array} \right) 
    \quad \mathrm{and} \quad \beta= \left( \begin{array}{cc} \mathds{1}_2 & 0 \\ 0 & -\mathds{1}_2 \end{array} \right)
\end{equation}
where $i=x,y,z$ and $\sigma_i$ the three Pauli matrices. Those matrices acts on four wave-functions $\psi_i$ forming the Dirac spinor $\psi=(\psi_1 \, \psi_2 \, \psi_3 \, \psi_4)^\mathrm{T}$.

Following that, the Dirac equation is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi(\mathbf{x},t)-m\psi(\mathbf{x},t)=0
\end{equation}
arising from the Dirac Lagrangian:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_\mathrm{D} = i \overline{\psi}\gamma^\mu \partial_\mu \psi - m \overline{\psi} \psi
\end{equation}
with $\gamma^\mu$ the Dirac matrices such that $\gamma^0=\beta$ and $\gamma^i=\beta\alpha^i$, and $\overline{\psi}=\psi^\dagger\gamma^0$, the adjoint spinor.

Those new degrees of freedom, introduced by the gamma matrices, naturally describe the spin of the fundamental fermions and antifermions introduced in 1925 by Wolfgang Pauli. The negative-energy solutions are interpreted as negative-energy particles propagating backwards in time, or equivalently, as the physical positive-energy antiparticles propagating forwards in time. The prediction of antimatter was later confirmed with the discovery of the positron in 1932 by Carl David Anderson \cite{Anderson1932}.

Therefore, solving the Dirac equation yields a relativistic quantum-mechanical framework for describing spin-half particles and their corresponding antiparticles.


\subsection{Quantum electrodynamics}

One missing point of the Dirac equation is the absence of interactions between the fields. If one wants to include them, the effects of local and global field transformations on the associated Lagrangian must be described. 

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The Dirac Lagrangian remains unchanged under a global transformation, $\psi \rightarrow e^{i\theta}\psi$. Still, it's not the case anymore for a local transformation, $\psi \rightarrow e^{i\theta(\textbf{x})}\psi$, where the phase $\theta(\textbf{x})$ depends on the space-time coordinates.

Imposing this invariance leads to the introduction of a vectorial field $A_\mu$ which transforms as $A_\mu \rightarrow A_\mu + \partial_\mu\theta/Q_\mathrm{em}$, such that the Dirac Lagrangian is added to an interaction term $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}$ described as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}} = - Q_\mathrm{em} \overline{\psi} \gamma^\mu A_\mu \psi \, ,
\end{equation}
where the variable $Q_\mathrm{em}$ denotes the electric charge, a conserved quantity under Noether's theorem for the U(1) invariance of the Dirac Lagrangian. This additional $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}$ term denotes the interaction between a fermion and the vector field. The vector field (spin-1 boson) $A_\mu$ has no mass term and can be interpreted as a massless gauge boson.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The dynamics of this gauge boson are captured by its kinematic term in the Lagrangian. It follows that this kinematic term arises from the Maxwell Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Maxwell}}$ defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Maxwell}} = -\frac{1}{4}F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu} \quad \mathrm{with} \, F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu
\end{equation}

One could note that this vector field is, in fact, the massless photon, the gauge boson of electromagnetism. 

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The consistent QFT description of the electromagnetic interaction was developed independently by Richard Feynman, Julian Schwinger and Shin'ichir\={o} Tomonaga between 1946 and 1950, a work collectively known as quantum electrodynamics (QED) and summarised by the Lagrangian:

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QED}} 
    &= \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{D}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Maxwell}} \\
    &= i \overline{\psi}\gamma^\mu \partial_\mu \psi 
    - m \overline{\psi} \psi 
    - Q_\mathrm{em} \overline{\psi} \gamma^\mu A_\mu \psi 
    -\frac{1}{4}F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

This theory is referred to as the U(1)$_\mathrm{em}$ because the QED Lagrangian is invariant under any local U(1) transformation, and its formalism explains fermion pair creation and annihilation, as well as the Drell-Yan process.


\subsection{Weak interaction}

One thing that QED failed to describe is the change in the particles' flavour in a given process.

First modelled by Enrico Fermi in 1933, neutron decay and later muon decay were explained by introducing a coupling constant $G_F\, \mathrm{[GeV^{-2}]}$ that describes the interaction. As this vision breaks unitarity at high energies, with each new Fermi vertex adding a square-energy term, one could say that this theory was a first step toward a hidden interaction that could be probed at higher energies.

To address the unitarity issue and follow QED examples, it was supposed that the interaction was mediated by a massive charged gauge boson, later identified as the $W^\pm$ bosons. Because these interactions occur at short distances, the term "weak" has been adopted to describe such processes.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Despite QED, the weak interaction mediated by the $W^\pm$ bosons is not symmetric under parity transformation\footnote{In particle physics, parity is a discrete symmetry corresponding to the spatial inversion $\mathbf{x} \to -\mathbf{x}$, under which a physical system is said to
be parity-symmetric if its governing laws are invariant under this transformation.}. The Chien-Shiung Wu experiment \cite{Wu1957} demonstrated in 1956 that the weak interaction violates parity symmetry and is therefore chiral. By studying the $\beta$-decay of polarised $^{60}$Co nuclei, Wu provided the first direct experimental evidence for parity violation in nature: certain physical processes occur at different rates than their mirror-image counterparts.

Based on this, a pure vector current is not allowed --- as in QED, where $j^\mu=\overline{\psi}\gamma^\mu\psi$ --- otherwise the weak interaction would not violate parity. Under parity transformation, a pure vector current $\bar{\psi}\gamma^\mu\psi$ is even, while an axial-vector current $\bar{\psi}\gamma^\mu\gamma^5\psi$, with  $\gamma^5=i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3$, is odd; consequently, any mixed $V \pm A$ coupling contains a cross term that is odd under parity, leading to maximal parity violation. The $V-A$ structure is the one that matches the experiment observations, and can be rewritten using the left-handed chiral\footnote{The chirality is defined as the eigenvalue of the $\gamma^5$ matrix, with eigenstates projected by $P_{L,R} = (1 \mp \gamma^5)/2$; it is an intrinsic property that is a direct consequence of the Lorentz structure under which the field transforms.} projector $P_L = (1 - \gamma^5)/2$, yielding $\bar{\psi}\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma^5)\psi = 2\bar{\psi}_L\gamma^\mu\psi_L$, which shows that the weak interaction couples exclusively to left-handed fermions and right-handed antifermions.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The weak interaction is also mediated by a neutral current carried by the $Z^0$ boson. The existence of the $Z^0$ boson is necessary to cancel the $W^\pm$ production divergences at high energy and to preserve unitarity. Experimental evidence for neutral currents was first observed at the Gargamelle bubble chamber at CERN in 1973, which subsequently led to the direct discovery of the $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ bosons in 1983 \cite{WandZ}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Weak interactions manifest across a broad range of energy scales, from low-energy processes such as nuclear beta decay and pion decay to intermediate scales with $\mathrm{K}^0$ oscillations, and up to the direct production of $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ bosons at colliders.


\subsection{Quantum chromodynamics}

One last piece that needs to be modelled is the strong interaction. It is responsible for binding quarks into hadrons and for the cohesion of atomic nuclei. It is described by quantum chromodynamics (QCD), a non-abelian gauge theory based on the colour symmetry group $\mathrm{SU}(3)_c$. This non-abelian structure is required because the gauge bosons themselves carry the charge of the interaction and must therefore interact with themselves. This is in contrast to $\mathrm{U}(1)_{\mathrm{em}}$ QED, where the photon is electrically neutral and carries no self-coupling.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The first evidence for a strong nuclear force came from the simple observation that atomic nuclei are stable: the electromagnetic repulsion between protons must be overcome by a stronger, short-range interaction. In 1935, Hideki Yukawa proposed the first model in which the pion mediated the force, later discovered in 1947, explaining the force's confinement to nuclear scales. With the proliferation of hadron discoveries in the 1950s and 1960s, Murray Gell-Mann and George Zweig proposed the quark model independently in 1964. In this structure, hadrons are composite objects composed of a small number of fundamental constituents, called quarks, organised according to the SU(3) flavour symmetry group. Mesons are quark--antiquark pairs $q\bar{q}$, while baryons are three-quark bound states $qqq$. All observed hadrons are colour-neutral (colour singlets), a property known as colour confinement.

The reality of quarks as physical constituents was established in the late 1960s, when the structure of the proton was probed with electrons and revealed that the electrons scattered off point-like, essentially free constituents. Feynman's parton model identified these as quarks and gluons, each carrying a momentum fraction $x$ of the proton, with the distribution governed by the parton distribution functions (PDFs) $f_i(x, Q^2)$.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The formalism of QCD, developed in the early 1970s, introduced eight gauge bosons associated with the eight generators of $\mathrm{SU}(3)_c$. Since these bosons bind quarks together, as glue holds a nucleus together, they were named gluons. These gluons carry colour charge and therefore couple only to other colour-charged particles, such as quarks and gluons themselves.

The QCD Lagrangian is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{QCD}} = \sum_{f} \bar{\psi}^f \left( i\gamma^\mu D_\mu - m_f \right) \psi^f
    - \frac{1}{4} G^{a}_{\mu\nu}G^{\mu\nu}_{a},
\end{equation}
where the sum runs explicitly over quark flavours $f$, each with mass $m_f$. The covariant
derivative
\begin{equation}
    D_\mu = \partial_\mu - i g_s \frac{\lambda^a}{2} A^a_\mu
\end{equation}
ensures local $\mathrm{SU}(3)_c$ gauge invariance, where $g_s$ is the strong coupling constant, $A^a_\mu$ are the eight gluon fields, and $\lambda^a/2$ the eight Gell-Mann matrices, generators of $\mathrm{SU}(3)_c$. The gluon field strength tensor is:

\begin{equation}
    G^a_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A^a_\nu - \partial_\nu A^a_\mu
    + g_s f^{a}_{{\,\,\,bc}} A^b_\mu A^c_\nu,
\end{equation}
where $f^{abc}$ are the structure constants of $SU(3)_c$. The non-linear term generates three- and four-gluon vertices, a direct consequence of the non-Abelian nature of the group.


\subsection{Electroweak unification}

So far, the QED and QCD formalisms contain only massless gauge bosons; thus, their Lagrangians can be constructed straightforwardly from gauge invariance alone, without any additional complication. The weak theory, otherwise, while successfully describing charged-current weak processes via $W^\pm$ exchange, is not a complete theory on its own. Introduced without an underlying gauge principle, the boson masses must be inserted by hand, breaking gauge invariance and rendering the theory non-renormalisable\footnote{Renormalisation is a procedure in QFT that addresses infinities arising from additional Feynman diagram loops by redefining a finite set of physical parameters, including masses and coupling constants.}. Furthermore, the requirement of a neutral current mediator $Z^0$ and the restoration of unitarity in weak scattering amplitudes cannot be accommodated without extending the symmetry group. 

\vspace{0.5 cm}

These shortcomings motivated the description of the weak interaction in terms of an $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$ gauge theory, which naturally accommodates its chiral structure and introduces three gauge boson $W^{(1)}$, $W^{(2)}$ and $W^{(3)}$, with linear combination of the first two leading to the two massive gauge bosons $W^\pm$. The subscript $L$ indicates that the SU(2) gauge symmetry acts exclusively on left-handed fermion doublets, reflecting the maximal parity violation of the weak interaction.

However, $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$ alone cannot reproduce the correct electric charges of the fermions, nor does it accommodate a massless neutral gauge boson that can be identified with the photon. This observation indicates that electromagnetism and the weak interaction are not independent phenomena, but rather two manifestations of a single underlying symmetry. The introduction of an additional $\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ gauge factor, associated with weak hypercharge $Y$, allows the $Z^0$ boson and the photon to emerge as a linear combination of $W^{(3)}$ and the $\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ boson $B$. This unification of the weak and electromagnetic interactions into the gauge group $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L \otimes \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$, within which boson masses arise dynamically, and renormalisability is restored, was achieved independently by Sheldon Glashow, Steven Weinberg, and Abdus Salam in the 1960s \cite{Glashow1961, Weinberg1967, Salam1968}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The electroweak theory assigns two quantum numbers to each fermion: the weak isospin $T$ and the weak hypercharge $Y$. Left-handed fermions are grouped into $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$ doublets of weak isospin $T = 1/2$:

\begin{equation}
    L = \begin{pmatrix} \nu_\ell \\ \ell^- \end{pmatrix}_L \qquad \mathrm{and} \qquad
    Q = \begin{pmatrix} u \\ d' \end{pmatrix}_L,
\end{equation}
while right-handed fermions $e_R$, $u_R$ and $d_R$ are $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$ singlets with $T = 0$ and therefore do not couple to the $W^\pm$ bosons, consistently with the $V-A$ structure of the weak interaction discussed previously. The weak hypercharge $Y$ is linked through the electric charge $Q_{\mathrm{em}}$ and the third component of weak isospin $T_3$ with $Q_{\mathrm{em}} = T_3 + \frac{Y}{2}$.

The $\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ gauge boson $B$ couples to all fermions with a strength proportional to their hypercharge, while the three $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$ gauge bosons $W^a$ couple only to left-handed doublets. Therefore, to ensure gauge invariance, the covariant derivative takes different forms depending on the chirality of the fermions:

\begin{align}
    {D_\mu}_L &= \partial_\mu - i g_W T_a W^a_\mu - i g' \frac{Y}{2} B_\mu \,, \\
    \label{eq:covariantDerivativeWeak}
    {D_\mu}_R &= \partial_\mu - i g' \frac{Y}{2} B_\mu \,,
\end{align}
where $g_W$ and $g'$ are the $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$ and $\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ coupling constants respectively, and $T_a = \sigma_a/2$ the generators of $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$ with $\sigma_a$ the Pauli matrices. 

The kinetic terms in the Lagrangian describe the interaction between the fermions:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \mathcal{L}_\mathrm{fermions} &= 
        \overline{Q}_j i \gamma^\mu {D_\mu}_L Q_j + \overline{u}_j i \gamma^\mu {D_\mu}_R u_j + \overline{d}_j i \gamma^\mu {D_\mu}_R d_j \\
        & \quad + \overline{L}_j i \gamma^\mu {D_\mu}_L L_j + \overline{e}_j i \gamma^\mu {D_\mu}_R e_j
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
where the subscript $j$ sums over the three generations of fermions; $Q$, $u$ and $d$ being the left-handed doublet, right-handed singlet up and down quark fields; $L$ and $e$ the left-handed doublet and right-handed singlet electron fields.

The kinetic terms for the gauge fields are:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{gauge}} = -\frac{1}{4} W^a_{\mu\nu} W^{a\,\mu\nu}
    - \frac{1}{4} B_{\mu\nu} B^{\mu\nu},
\end{equation}
where $W^a_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu W^a_\nu - \partial_\nu W^a_\mu + g_W\, \varepsilon^{a}{}_{bc} W^b_\mu W^c_\nu$ and $B_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu B_\nu - \partial_\nu B_\mu$ are the $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$ and $\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ field strength tensors respectively, with $\varepsilon$ the antisymmetric structure constant of the SU(2) group.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

However, the total Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{fermions}+\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{gauge}$, contains no mass terms: an explicit Dirac mass $m\bar{\psi}\psi = m(\bar{\psi}_R\psi_L + \bar{\psi}_L\psi_R)$ couples a left-handed doublet to a right-handed singlet and is therefore not invariant under $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L \otimes \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$. Similarly, an explicit mass term $m^2 W^\mu_a W_{a\mu}$ for the gauge bosons breaks gauge invariance. Since fermions and the weak bosons are observed to be massive, a dynamical mechanism is required to generate these masses without spoiling the gauge symmetry: this is achieved by introducing a scalar field whose potential admits a non-zero vacuum expectation value, spontaneously breaking the $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L \otimes \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ symmetry down to $\mathrm{U}(1)_{\mathrm{em}}$.


\subsection{Electroweak symmetry breaking}
\label{subsection:BEH}

The problem of mass generation in gauge theories was resolved in the 1960s through a series of independent contributions. Yoichiro Nambu and Jeffrey Goldstone, later joined by Philip W. Anderson, showed in 1960-1962 that the spontaneous breaking of a continuous global symmetry leads to the appearance of massless scalar bosons (the Goldstone bosons). In the context of a local gauge symmetry, however, three independent groups, namely Robert Brout and François Englert; Peter Higgs; Gerald Guralnik, Carl Hagen, and Tom Kibble, demonstrated in 1964 that these would-be Goldstone bosons are absorbed by the gauge bosons, which acquire a longitudinal degree of freedom and thus a mass. This remaining physical degree of freedom manifests as a massive scalar particle, subsequently named the 'Higgs boson' $H$. This process is referred to as the Brout--Englert--Higgs (BEH) mechanism\footnote{The mechanism is commonly referred to as the BEH mechanism, though Peter Higgs himself preferred the acronym ABEGHHK'tH, acknowledging the contributions of Anderson, Brout, Englert, Guralnik, Hagen, Higgs, Kibble, to the mechanism itself, as well as 't~Hooft, who later established the renormalisability of the theory.}. In the history of the universe, this symmetry breaking is believed to have occurred shortly after the Big Bang, when the universe's temperature dropped below approximately $160$~GeV~\cite{ElectroWeak_Temperature}, triggering the electroweak phase transition and giving rise to the massive gauge bosons observed today.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The BEH mechanism is implemented by introducing a complex scalar $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$ doublet with weak hypercharge $Y = 1$:
\begin{equation}
    \phi = \begin{pmatrix} \phi^+ \\ \phi^0 \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with the scalar potential

\begin{equation}
    V(\phi) = \mu^2 \phi^\dagger \phi + \lambda (\phi^\dagger \phi)^2,
\end{equation}
where $\lambda > 0$ ensures non divergence of the minimal value of $V(\phi)$. For $\mu^2~<~0$, the potential develops a continuous family of degenerate minima at $|\phi|^2 = -\mu^2 / 2\lambda \equiv v^2/2$, forming a ''Mexican hat'' shape depicted in \cref{fig:higgsshape}. The vacuum is not unique, as it lies on a circle of radius $v$ referred to as the vacuum expectation value, and the choice of a particular ground state spontaneously breaks the $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L \otimes \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ symmetry. Selecting the unitary gauge, the field is expanded around the vacuum expectation value as:

\begin{equation}
    \phi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ v + h(x) \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $h$ is the physical Higgs boson field. The three would-be Goldstone bosons associated with the broken generators are absorbed as longitudinal polarisations of the $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ bosons. At the same time, the $\mathrm{U}(1)_{\mathrm{em}}$ symmetry remains unbroken, leaving the photon massless.

In contrast, the continuous circle of degenerate minima at $|\phi| = v/\sqrt{2}$ (point B) is where the field settles, spontaneously breaking the $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L \otimes \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ symmetry.}
    \label{fig:higgsshape}
\end{figure}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The masses of the gauge bosons arise from the covariant kinetic term of the Higgs doublet:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Higgs}} = ({D^\mu}_L \phi)^\dagger ({D_\mu}_L \phi) - V(\phi),
\end{equation}
where ${D_\mu}_L$ is the electroweak covariant derivative acting on a left-handed doublet. Substituting the vacuum expectation value into this term generates quadratic terms in the gauge fields:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Higgs}} =& \quad \, \frac{1}{2} (\partial_\mu h) (\partial^\mu h) \\
    &+ \frac{1}{8} g_W^2 \left( W_\mu^{(1)}+iW_\mu^{(2)} \right) \left( W^{(1) \,\mu}-iW^{(2) \,\mu} \right) \left(v+h(x)\right)^2 \\
    &+ \frac{1}{8} \left( g_W W_\mu^{(3)}-g'B_\mu \right) \left( g_W W^{(3) \,\mu}-g'B^\mu \right) \left(v+h(x)\right)^2 \\
    &- \lambda v^2 h^2 - \lambda v h^3 -\frac{1}{4}\lambda h^4 \, .
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

The first term expresses the kinematics of the Higgs field itself. The second term is in fact the kinematics of the two $W^\pm$ bosons, which can be expressed as a linear combination of $W^{(1)}$ and $W^{(2)}$:
\begin{equation}
    W^\pm_\mu = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left( W^{(1)}_\mu \mp i W^{(2)}_\mu \right)\,.
\end{equation}

Therefore, developing the second term of $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Higgs}}$ brings to light the mass of the W$^\pm$ bosons with $m_W=\frac{1}{2}g_Wv$ and also an interaction between the Higgs field and the weak gauge field.

Developing the term involving the neutral $W_\mu^{(3)}$ and $B_\mu$ bosons yields a $2\times 2$ mass matrix in the $(W^{(3)}_\mu, B_\mu)$ basis:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{v^2}{8}\left(W^{(3)}_\mu \,\,\, B_\mu\right) 
    \begin{pmatrix} g_W^2 & -g_Wg' \\ -g_Wg' & g'^2 \end{pmatrix}
    \left( \begin{array}{c} W^{(3) \,\mu} \\ B^\mu \end{array} \right)
    \label{eq:massZandA}
\end{equation}
where this matrix is real and symmetric, and can therefore be diagonalised in an orthonormal eigenbasis. The eigenvalues give the physical boson masses, while the eigenvectors $Z_\mu$ and $A_\mu$ (corresponding to the $Z^0$ boson and the photon) are, by linear algebra, a combination of $W_\mu^{(3)}$ and $B_\mu$. 

In this mass eigenstate basis, \cref{eq:massZandA} becomes:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{v^2}{8}\left(Z_\mu \,\,\, A_\mu\right) 
    \begin{pmatrix} g_W^2+g'^2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
    \left( \begin{array}{c} Z^\mu \\ A^\mu \end{array} \right) = \frac{1}{2}\left(Z_\mu \,\,\, A_\mu\right) 
    \begin{pmatrix} m_Z^2 & 0 \\ 0 & m_A^2 \end{pmatrix}
    \left( \begin{array}{c} Z^\mu \\ A^\mu \end{array} \right)
\end{equation}
and

\begin{equation}
    \left( \begin{array}{c} Z^\mu \\ A^\mu \end{array} \right) = 
    \begin{pmatrix} \cos \theta_W & -\sin \theta_W \\ \sin \theta_W & \cos \theta_W \end{pmatrix}
    \left( \begin{array}{c} W^{(3) \,\mu} \\ B^\mu \end{array} \right) \, .
\end{equation}
with $\theta_W$, the Weinberg angle, satisfying $\tan\theta_W=g'/g_W$. While the photon remains massless as $m_A=0$, the $Z^0$ boson acquires a non-zero mass $m_Z=\frac{1}{2}v\sqrt{g_W^2+g'^2}=m_W/\cos\theta_W$.

One could also remark that, after performing this basis change, substituting $W_\mu^{(3)}$ and $B_\mu$ with their expressions in terms of $Z_\mu$ and $A_\mu$ in \cref{eq:covariantDerivativeWeak} requires that the electromagnetic coupling constant $Q_\mathrm{em}$ is related to $g_W$ and $g'$ by:

\begin{equation}
    Q_\mathrm{em} = g_W \sin\theta_W = g' \cos\theta_W,
\end{equation}
showing that electromagnetism is not an independent interaction but emerges after electroweak symmetry breaking.

Finally, the last terms of $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Higgs}}$ describe the Higgs self-interactions and give the Higgs boson a mass, with $m_H~=~v\sqrt{2\lambda}$.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

So far, the BEH mechanism generates masses for the three weak bosons while leaving the photon massless. However, fermions remain massless after symmetry breaking, as an explicit Dirac mass term $-m\bar{\psi}\psi = -m\left(\bar{\psi}_R\psi_L + \bar{\psi}_L\psi_R\right)$ couples left-handed doublets to right-handed singlets and is therefore not invariant under $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L \otimes \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$. The same Higgs doublet $\phi$, however, allows the construction of gauge-invariant Yukawa couplings among left-handed doublets, right-handed singlets, and the Higgs field, which dynamically generate fermion masses after symmetry breaking.

The Yukawa Lagrangian takes the general form:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Yukawa}} = - y^f_{ij}\, \overline{\psi}_{L,i}\, \phi\, \psi_{R,j} + \mathrm{h.c.} \, ,
\end{equation}
where $y^f_{ij}$ are complex coupling constants, the indices $i,j$ run over the three fermion generations, and h.c refers to the Hermitian conjugate, added to make the Lagrangian real. After spontaneous symmetry breaking, the Yukawa couplings generate Dirac mass matrices $m^f_{ij} = y^f_{ij}\, v/\sqrt{2}$ and describe the coupling between the Higgs boson and the fermions.:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Yukawa}}^\mathrm{EWSB}=  -\frac{v}{\sqrt{2}} y^f_{ij}\,\overline{\psi}_i \psi_j - \frac{y^f_{ij}}{\sqrt{2}}\, \overline{\psi}_i \psi_j\, h + \mathrm{h.c.}
\end{equation}

The physical fermion masses are obtained by diagonalising these matrices via independent unitary rotations in the up- and down-type quark sectors. Since the left-handed rotations of the up-type and down-type quarks do not generally coincide, the $W^\pm$ bosons can mediate transitions between quark generations, a mismatch parametrised by the Nicola Cabibbo--Makoto Kobayashi--Toshihide Maskawa (CKM) matrix. In the lepton sector, neutrinos are massless in the SM, so the single unitary rotation can always be absorbed into a field redefinition, leaving no physical mixing matrix.


\subsection{The Standard Model Lagrangian}

The full SM Lagrangian can now be written as the sum of four terms:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SM}} = 
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gauge}}
    + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fermions}}
    + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Higgs}}
    + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Yukawa}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gauge}}$ contains the kinetic and self-interaction terms of the gauge bosons of $\mathrm{SU}(3)_c \otimes \mathrm{SU}(2)_L \otimes \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ when adding the last term of $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{QCD}$ to it. $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fermions}}$ is the gauge-covariant kinetic terms of the fermion fields, with consideration of the quark interactions of $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{QCD}$ in addition. $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Higgs}}$ is the Higgs kinetic term and potential, and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Yukawa}}$, the Yukawa couplings responsible for fermion mass generation. This Lagrangian, invariant under $\mathrm{SU}(3)_c \otimes \mathrm{SU}(2)_L \otimes \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ and spontaneously broken to $\mathrm{SU}(3)_c \otimes \mathrm{U}(1)_{\mathrm{em}}$ by the Higgs mechanism, constitutes the complete theoretical framework of the SM.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The SM contains 19 free parameters that must be determined experimentally:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gauge couplings (3):} the strong coupling $g_s$, the $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$     coupling $g_W$, and the $\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ coupling $g'$;
    \item \textbf{Higgs sector (2):} the vacuum expectation value $v$ and the Higgs boson mass $m_H$;
    \item \textbf{Fermion masses (9):} the six quark masses $m_u,\, m_d,\, m_s,\, m_c,\, m_b,\, m_t$ and the three charged lepton masses $m_e,\, m_\mu,\, m_\tau$, all generated via Yukawa couplings;
    \item \textbf{CKM matrix (4):} three mixing angles $\theta_{12},\, \theta_{13},\, \theta_{23}$ and one $CP$-violating phase\footnote{$CP$ violation refers to the breaking of the combined symmetry of charge conjugation $C$, which swaps particles with antiparticles, and parity $P$, implying that the laws of physics are not identical for matter and antimatter. Concretely, $CP$ violation manifests as a difference in the decay rates of a particle and its antiparticle, for instance $B^0 \to f$ occurring at a different rate than $\bar{B}^0 \to \bar{f}$.} $\delta$;
    \item \textbf{Strong $CP$ parameter (1):} $\theta_{\mathrm{QCD}}$, the parameter controlling $CP$ violation in the strong sector, arising from a topologically allowed gauge-invariant term in the QCD Lagrangian, experimentally consistent with zero.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations of the Standard Model}
\label{subsec:limitSM}

Despite its remarkable predictive success, the SM leaves several experimental observations unexplained and suffers from several theoretical shortcomings, providing strong motivation for physics beyond the Standard Model (BSM).

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent\textbf{Neutrino masses and oscillations}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

In the SM, neutrinos are strictly massless, as lepton number is an accidental symmetry and no Yukawa coupling can be constructed for them. However, the observation of the transformation of a neutrino of one flavour into another during propagation~\cite{NeutrinoOscillations} constitutes direct experimental evidence that neutrinos are massive, since oscillations require at least two non-zero mass eigenstates with non-degenerate masses. Likewise to the quark sector, the mismatch between the neutrino mass eigenstates and the weak interaction eigenstates is parametrised by the Bruno Pontecorvo -- Ziro Maki -- Masami Nakagawa -- Shoichi Sakata (PMNS) matrix, a $3\times 3$  unitary mixing matrix whose parameters, including a potentially $CP$-violating phase, are the subject of ongoing experimental investigation. The SM must therefore be extended to accommodate neutrino masses, either via a Dirac mechanism requiring right-handed neutrinos, or via a Majorana mass term that would make neutrinos their own antiparticles.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent\textbf{Matter--antimatter asymmetry}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

The SM predicts that matter and antimatter should have been produced in equal amounts at the Big Bang, while the observable universe is composed almost entirely of matter. This asymmetry requires, as formulated by Andrei Sakharov in 1967, baryon-number violation, a departure from thermal equilibrium, and $C$ and $CP$ violation. While the SM contains a source of $CP$ violation through the complex phase of the CKM matrix, it is insufficient by several orders of magnitude to account for the observed baryon asymmetry of the universe. Additional sources of $CP$ violation that would also satisfy the other two Sakharov conditions, beyond those present in the SM, are therefore required.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent\textbf{Dark matter and dark energy}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

Multiple independent cosmological observations indicate the existence of a cold, non-interacting, non-luminous, non-baryonic form of matter that accounts for approximately 27\% of the energy content of the universe. None of the SM particles has the required properties: stability on cosmological timescales, small electromagnetic and strong interactions, and the correct density, making dark matter one of the most convincing arguments for BSM physics.

Observations of Type Ia supernovae in 1998 revealed that the expansion of the universe is accelerating \cite{Riess1998, Perlmutter1999}, attributed to a form of energy of unknown origin, namely dark energy, which constitutes approximately 68\% of the total energy budget. Similarly, no SM particle can account for dark energy, and the vacuum energy estimated from QFT exceeds the observed value by some 120 orders of magnitude.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent\textbf{The hierarchy problem}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

The measured Higgs boson mass $m_H \approx 125$~GeV is some seventeen orders of magnitude below the Planck scale $\Lambda \approx 10^{19}$~GeV. In QFT, the Higgs mass receives loop corrections proportional to the square of the highest energy scale in the theory; if that scale is $\Lambda$, these corrections are of order $10^{38}$~GeV$^2$, vastly exceeding $m_H^2 \approx 10^4$~GeV$^2$. Obtaining the correct physical mass, therefore, requires the bare mass parameter and the radiative corrections to cancel to one part in $10^{34}$, with no theoretical justification for such a precise cancellation. This unnatural fine-tuning is the hierarchy problem, and its resolution is one of the central motivations for BSM theories such as supersymmetry, which introduces a symmetry that exactly cancels the quadratic divergences.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent\textbf{Absence of quantum gravity}
\vspace{0.5 cm}


The SM does not describe gravitational interactions at the quantum level. General relativity, which describes gravity at macroscopic scales, is not renormalisable as a QFT, and the two frameworks are fundamentally incompatible at energy scales approaching the Planck scale $\Lambda$. While gravity is negligible at collider energies, its absence from the SM underscores the theory's incompleteness. Physical confirmation of a theoretical particle known as a graviton would account for it to a degree.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

On top of those main problems, the strong $CP$ problem persists, as does the almost unification of the SM gauge couplings: the three constants $g_s$, $g_W$, and $g'$ run with energy but do not converge to a single value within the SM, hinting at a deeper unified structure. This non-exhaustive list of issues further emphasises the theory's incompleteness.



\section{Supersymmetry, a theory beyond the Standard Model}
\label{sec:bsm}

The convergence of these experimental and theoretical arguments strongly suggests that the SM is an effective theory, valid at the energy scales currently accessible, but incomplete. The search for BSM physics is therefore a central goal of modern high-energy physics. One of the most theoretically motivated BSM frameworks is supersymmetry (SUSY), which addresses several of the shortcomings discussed above simultaneously and serves as the theoretical foundation for the analysis presented in this thesis.

\subsection{Particle content and structure of the minimal supersymmetric extension of the Standard Model}

SUSY postulates a symmetry relating bosons and fermions: to every bosonic degree of freedom corresponds a fermionic one with identical gauge quantum numbers and couplings. A first question is whether the known particles of the SM could already be paired into supermultiplets. The answer is negative, as the internal quantum numbers of fermions and bosons do not match.

The minimal solution is therefore to double the spectrum: each SM particle is associated with a new superpartner (or \textit{spartner}) differing by half a unit of spin. Quarks are paired with scalar \textit{squarks}, leptons with \textit{sleptons}, gauge bosons with spin-half \textit{gauginos} (photino, zino, wino, gluino), and the Higgs sector with a weak isospin doublet of spin-half \textit{higgsinos} $\tilde{H}_{1,2}^0$ and $\tilde{H}^\pm$.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The formalism of any supersymmetric model is built from a Lagrangian containing a supersymmetric part and a soft supersymmetry-breaking part:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_\mathrm{SUSY} 
                + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SUSY}\, \times} .
\end{equation}

The supersymmetric piece $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{SUSY}$ of the minimal supersymmetric extension of the Standard Model (MSSM) shares the same gauge structure as the SM. At the same time, its Yukawa interactions are derived from a \textit{superpotential} $W$, a cubic holomorphic function containing the left-handed fermions. This superpotential generates the masses of the charged fermions and accounts for the structure of the CKM matrix. A key feature of the MSSM is that holomorphy of $W$ and anomaly cancellation require the introduction of two Higgs doublets, $H_{1,2}$, rather than the single doublet of the SM.

After electroweak symmetry breaking, the superpartners of the electroweak gauge and Higgs bosons mix. The supersymmetric partners of the $W^\pm$ and of the charged Higgs bosons $H^\pm$ combine into two charged mass eigenstates, the \textit{charginos} $\chi_{1,2}^\pm$. Similarly, the partners of the $\gamma$, $Z^0$ and of the neutral Higgs bosons $H_{1,2}^0$ mix into four neutral mass eigenstates, the \textit{neutralinos} $\chi_{1,2,3,4}^0$. The lightest neutralino $\chi \equiv \chi_1^0$ is, in $R$-parity conserving scenarios\footnote{$R$-parity is a quantum number defined as $R=(-1)^{3(B-L)+2s}$, where $B$ is the baryon number, $L$ the lepton number and $s$ the spin, equal to $+1$ for SM particles and $-1$ for their superpartners; its conservation forbids proton decay and stabilises the lightest supersymmetric particle.}, the natural lightest supersymmetric particle (LSP) and a prime candidate for astrophysical cold dark matter~\cite{ELLIS1984453}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

In this model, the physical chargino $\chi^\pm$ and neutralino $\chi^0$ states are combinations of the higgsinos and gauginos. The full content of the MSSM is summarised in \cref{tab:susy_particles}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The supersymmetry-breaking piece $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SUSY}\, \times}$ must be carefully chosen so as not to spoil the cancellation of quadratic divergences described above. The breaking is therefore required to be 'soft', meaning that it does not reintroduce any quadratic (nor unwanted logarithmic) divergence in scalar masses. The allowed soft terms include gaugino masses, scalar mass factors, and trilinear scalar couplings.

The formalism of the MSSM extends the SM by introducing 105 free parameters, the vast majority of which originate from the 'soft' supersymmetry-breaking sector. Measuring such a large parameter space using colliders is daunting, and no single analysis can hope to constrain it entirely. For this reason, the experimental search strategy relies on simplified models that isolate specific subsets of parameters, often organised around the conservation or violation of $R$-parity.

\subsection{Hints for TeV-scale SUSY}

Such a symmetry is particularly attractive because it provides a natural mechanism to stabilise the Higgs mass against quadratic divergences. Since bosonic and fermionic loops contribute with opposite signs, the residual one-loop correction to the Higgs mass takes the form \cite{Ellis:591472}:
\begin{equation}
    \delta m_H^2 \simeq \mathcal{O}\!\left( m_B^2 - m_F^2 \right) 
    \lesssim m_H^2 
    \;\Longleftrightarrow\; 
    \left| m_B^2 - m_F^2 \right| \lesssim 1 \, \mathrm{TeV}^2 ,
\end{equation}
where $m_B$ and $m_F$ denote the masses of a given particle and its supersymmetric partner (a boson and its fermionic superpartner, or vice versa), this relation makes it natural to expect the masses of the superpartners to lie around the TeV scale, which falls within the LHC's operational range.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Beyond the hierarchy problem, several independent indications suggest that supersymmetry could indeed appear near the TeV scale.

The strengths of the three SM gauge interactions can be evolved to high energies via the renormalisation-group equations to test whether they unify into a single coupling, as predicted by Grand Unified Theories (GUTs). Without supersymmetry, this unification fails: GUTs would then require $\sin^2\theta_W \approx 0.214$, in clear tension with the experimental value of the effective neutral weak mixing parameter measured at the $Z^0$ peak, $\sin^2\theta_W \approx 0.23147 \pm 0.00049$ \cite{weakangle_lhcb}. Once the MSSM is included in the running, minimal supersymmetric GUTs predict $\sin^2\theta_W \approx 0.232$, where the residual error depends on the assumed sparticle masses, with a preferred scale around $1\,\mathrm{TeV}$~\cite{KELLEY1991423}.

Moreover, the MSSM Higgs sector comprises two complex doublets, $H_1$ and $H_2$, yielding eight real degrees of freedom \cite{Ellis:591472}. After electroweak symmetry breaking, three are absorbed as the longitudinal modes of the $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ gauge bosons, leaving five physical states: two charged Higgs bosons $H^\pm$, one $CP$-odd scalar $A^0$, and two $CP$-even scalars $H^0$ and $h^0$. While the first four lie around the TeV scale, the lightest, $h^0$, is predicted to weigh less than about $130\,\mathrm{GeV}$~\cite{Carena_2003}, in excellent agreement with the measured SM Higgs boson mass $m_H^{SM} \approx 125.04 \pm 0.12\,\mathrm{GeV}$~\cite{Vagnerini:2905108}.

Astrophysical and cosmological observations indicate the necessity of cold dark matter, which may be accounted for by a neutral, weakly interacting massive particle with a mass below approximately $1\,\mathrm{TeV}$~\cite{ELLIS1984453}.



\section{Long-lived particles}
\label{sec:llp}

In the MSSM, as in other extensions of the SM, new states are predicted with decay lengths long enough to leave a distinct experimental signature relative to the prompt particles of the SM. These long-lived particles (LLPs) span an enormous range of lifetimes, from the picosecond to cosmological scales, and require dedicated reconstruction strategies that go well beyond those used in standard prompt searches. This section motivates the search for LLPs at colliders, explores the experimental signatures they leave in a general-purpose detector such as CMS, and ultimately focuses on the class of long-lived states relevant to this thesis: heavy stable charged particles.


\subsection{Lifetime regimes}

The lifetimes of SM particles span more than fifty orders of magnitude, from $\approx 10^{-25}\,$s for the top quark to a lower bound of $\geq 10^{34}\,$years on the proton~\cite{ParticleDataGroup:2024cfk}. Translated into proper decay lengths $c\tau$, this range covers everything from a few attometres to cosmological scales. 

In collider experiments, the comparison between the particle's proper decay length and the detector geometry is crucial. A particle is classified as \textit{detector-prompt} when $c\tau$ is significantly below the spatial resolution of the inner tracker, rendering its decay vertex indistinguishable from the primary interaction point. In contrast, particles with $c\tau$ much greater than the outer radius of the muon system ($\approx 10\,$m) are considered \textit{detector-stable}, as they traverse the apparatus before decaying. The region between these two extremes surrounds long-lived particles whose decay vertices are displaced by distances ranging from hundreds of micrometres to several metres from the primary vertex.

Within the SM, this intermediate regime is sparsely populated, essentially restricted to weakly-decaying mesons or baryons. \cref{fig:llp_lifetimes_sm} illustrates the typical lifetime and decay lengths of SM particles as a function of their mass.

\subsection{Mechanisms producing long lifetimes}

The decay width $\Gamma = 1/\tau$ of a particle is determined by two factors: the strength of the coupling that drives the decay and the available phase space, which is controlled by the mass splitting between the parent and its decay products. In BSM scenarios, these two factors can take very different values, thereby pushing a particle's lifetime into the displaced or stable regime. Three mechanisms contribute to this effect, each illustrated below by a representative supersymmetric example.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Small coupling}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

If the only allowed decay channel proceeds through a strongly suppressed coupling, the resulting width can be small enough to yield a measurable displacement. The canonical example is R-parity-violating (RPV) supersymmetry, in which the neutralino LSP $\tilde{\chi}^0_1$ decays into SM fermions via a single RPV coupling $\lambda$. Since no other channel is available, the three-body decay proceeds through a virtual sfermion of mass $\tilde{m}$, and the width takes the form~\cite{Sierra_2008}:
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_{\tilde{\chi}^0_1} \;=\;
    \frac{c_f\, g^{2}\, \lambda^{2}\, m_{\tilde{\chi}^0_1}^{5}}
    {1536\,\pi^{3}}\; f(m_{\tilde{f}})\;\,,
    \label{eq:rpv_neutralino}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ denotes one of the trilinear RPV couplings, $c_f$ is a colour factor, $g$ the relevant gauge coupling, and $f(m_{\tilde{f}})$ a function of dimension $m^{-4}$ encoding the dependence on the intermediate sfermion masses.

What makes the neutralino long-lived is the smallness of $\lambda$, with the lifetime inversely proportional to $1/\lambda^2$. For couplings in the range $\lambda \lesssim 10^{-2}$, the decay length becomes macroscopic and can leave a displaced vertex in the tracker; for even smaller values, the $\tilde{\chi}^0_1$ escapes the detector entirely, behaving as an effectively stable particle contributing to missing transverse energy.


\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Heavy mediators}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

When a decay proceeds through an off-shell heavy mediator, the width acquires an additional propagator suppression $\propto 1/M_{\mathrm{med}}^{4}$. The Split-SUSY scenario~\cite{ArkaniHamed:2004fb, Giudice:2004tc} provides a textbook example: all sfermion masses are raised to a common scale $m_{S} \gg 1\,\mathrm{TeV}$, so that the three-body channel $\tilde{g} \to q\bar{q}\,\tilde{\chi}_{i}^{0}$, mediated by virtual squarks of mass $\approx m_S$, becomes the only kinematically accessible gluino decay mode. The resulting gluino lifetime,
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{\tilde{g}} \; [\text{s}] \,\simeq\; 8\;
    \left(\frac{m_{S}}{10^{9}\,\mathrm{GeV}}\right)^{4}
    \left(\frac{1\,\mathrm{TeV}}{m_{\tilde{g}}}\right)^{5}\,,
    \label{eq:gluino_lifetime}
\end{equation}
varies between a nanosecond and $10^{25}\,$s as $m_{S}$ ranges from $10^{7}\,\mathrm{GeV}$ up to the GUT scale ($\approx10^{16} \, \mathrm{GeV}$), leaving the gluino effectively stable across most of the parameter space.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Compressed spectra}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

If the decaying particle and the heaviest of its decay products are nearly degenerate in mass, the available phase space shrinks rapidly, and the lifetime grows steeply with the mass splitting. In the MSSM with a stop NLSP nearly degenerate with the lightest neutralino, the stop lifetime can stretch from $10^{-26}$~s up to several seconds as $|m_{\tilde{t}} - m_{\tilde{\chi}_{1}^{0}}|$ approaches zero~\cite{Aebischer:2014mma}. A closely related mechanism is involved in anomaly-mediated SUSY breaking (AMSB)~\cite{Ibe:2012sx}, where the small radiative chargino-neutralino mass splitting
$|m_{\tilde{\chi}_{1,2}^{\pm}} - m_{\tilde{\chi}_{1}^{0}}|\approx 160\,\mathrm{MeV}$ leads to charginos with proper decay lengths in the centimetre range, thus, long enough to be reconstructed as short tracks before disappearing in the inner tracker~\cite{ATLAS:2022rme}.



\section{Long-lived particle searches at the CMS detector}

\subsection{Overview of CMS long-lived particle signatures}

LLP searches within the CMS detector are, by construction, signature-driven: the diversity of theoretical scenarios is too large for any single analysis to cover, and the lifetimes of interest span many orders of magnitude. A generic strategy based solely on experimental signatures is therefore the natural way to probe a broad range of BSM models simultaneously, with each lifetime regime giving rise to a distinct search topology. Those main classes, summarised schematically in \cref{fig:llp_signatures}, are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Displaced vertices.} A neutral LLP decaying inside the tracker volume into charged SM particles produces a vertex displaced by hundreds of micrometres to several metres from the PV. Specialised algorithms identify groups of objects that converge towards a common secondary point, among which the specific final states are displaced dijets~\cite{PhysRevD.109.112005, Collaboration_2025}, displaced leptons~\cite{CMS:2019qjk, 2024baababazed} or displaced photons~\cite{PhysRevD.100.112003}.
    \item \textbf{Disappearing tracks.} In compressed spectra scenarios, a charged particle decays into a nearly invisible neutral state plus a very soft pion of negligible $p_T$. This results in a high-quality track in the innermost pixel layers that abruptly stops in the strip tracker, with no associated calorimeter or muon-system activity~\cite{2020135502}, but with MET~\cite{PhysRevD.109.112005}.
    \item \textbf{Delayed signals.} Heavy LLPs are typically produced with $\beta\gamma\lesssim 1$, leading to measurable delays in the calorimeter timing or in dedicated muon-system measurements. Such searches could be done using the scouting dataset, without being constrained by the trigger~\cite{cmscollaboration2026searchheavylonglivedcharged}, or using new triggers developed for Run--3~\cite{cmscollaboration2026strategyperformancecmslonglived}.
    \item \textbf{Long isolated tracks.} Charged LLPs whose decay length exceeds the detector size traverse the entire apparatus and leave a single, continuous, high-momentum track from the pixel layers to the muon chambers. This is the topology relevant to this analysis, which is discussed in detail later.
    \\item \textbf{Muon detector showers.} A neutral LLP traversing the calorimeters and solenoid without interacting can decay inside the steel flux-return yoke of the muon system. The resulting hadronic or electromagnetic shower deposits a high-multiplicity cluster of hits in the surrounding chambers, using the muon detector as a sampling calorimeter sensitive to sub-GeV masses and long decay lengths beyond the reach of inner-tracker techniques~\cite{PhysRevD.110.032007, PhysRevD.110.012004}.
\end{itemize}

The CMS collaboration has developed dedicated analyses targeting each of these signatures. The experimental challenges differ markedly across the decay length range. Still, the underlying logic is the same: identify a kinematic or topological feature that has no counterpart in the prompt SM background.

A summary of the current CMS reach is given in \cref{fig:LLP_summary_search}, which compiles the proper decay lengths excluded at 95\% CL by a representative selection of long-lived search analyses. The searches are organised into three blocks: R-parity-violating SUSY, R-parity-conserving SUSY, and exotic Higgs decays together with dark-sector models. Together, they span roughly ten orders of magnitude in $c\tau$, from a few tens of nanometres to several kilometres, which gives a sense of how diverse the experimental strategies spectrum is.

\subsection{Heavy stable charged particles}
\label{sec:hscp_intro}

The signature on which this thesis is based is the conceptually simplest of the list above: a single, isolated, charged track that traverses the entire CMS detector. The particles producing it are referred to as \textit{heavy stable charged particles} (HSCPs), where ''stable'' should be understood at the experimental scale: the proper decay length is large enough for the particle to leave the detector before decaying; and ''heavy'' refers to masses well above the electroweak scale, typically several hundred GeV to a few TeV. HSCPs are produced at the LHC either through direct pair production or as the end of a longer cascade~\cite{Fairbairn:2006gg}, and they can carry an electric charge of $\pm 1\,e$, a fractional charge, or a multiple of $e$, depending on the underlying model.

This thesis does not focus on fractional- or multiply-charged HSCPs, for which the latest CMS results can be found in \cite{PhysRevLett.134.131802} and \cite{MCP_status}, respectively.


\subsubsection{Hadron-like and lepton-like HSCPs}

HSCPs are conventionally split into two broad categories~\cite{Fairbairn:2006gg}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Hadron-like HSCPs}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

This thesis focuses on the MSSM long-lived stop $\tilde{t}$ and the Split-SUSY gluino $\tilde{g}$, which are the canonical hadron-like HSCP candidates. The leading-order diagrams contributing to the dominant pair-production modes of these two hadron-like HSCPs are shown in \cref{fig:hscp_prod_gluino} and \cref{fig:hscp_prod_stop}. Those hadron-like HSCPs can be produced with additional gluons: one emitted by an incoming quark, referred to as an \textit{initial-state radiation} (ISR), and one emitted by the outgoing HSCP, referred to as a \textit{final-state radiation} (FSR). Such emissions are not accidental: they are generic consequences of QCD, in which any coloured leg of a hard process radiates additional gluons. ISR and FSR jets play an essential role in HSCP searches, as they often allow events to meet trigger requirements (typically MET-based).

Such HSCPs carry colour charge and therefore hadronise upon production into bound states with SM partons, generically called \textit{R-hadrons}~\cite{Fairbairn:2006gg}. Their composition depends on the colour representation of the heavy parton: a squark binds like a heavy quark, whereas a gluino additionally binds to a gluon or a quark--antiquark pair. One thus expects R-mesons ($\tilde{q}\bar{q}$ or $\tilde{g}q\bar{q}$), R-baryons ($\tilde{q}qq$ or $\tilde{g}qqq$) and, for the gluino, gluino--gluon states $\tilde{g}g$. The composite structure of these R-hadrons for one mass point example of a gluino and a stop is summarised in \cref{tab:Rhadron_composite}.

As an R-hadron propagates through the detector, hadronic interactions with detector nuclei can change its valence parton content and, in particular, modify its electric charge: this \textit{charge flipping} phenomenon may turn a charged R-hadron into a neutral one, or vice versa~\cite{Mackeprang:2009ad}. An example of this phenomenon is illustrated in \cref{fig:rhadron_charge_flip}, where the Feynman diagram of an R-hadron interacting with the material is drawn, causing the latter to flip charge.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Lepton-like HSCPs}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

Another type of HSCPs interacts only via the electroweak force and propagates through the detector much like heavy muons, with no hadronic interactions perturbing their trajectories. The most studied example is the long-lived stau $\tilde{\tau}$ predicted in GMSB scenarios, considered both as a directly pair-produced state and as the end product of all sparticle cascade decays. Long-lived fourth-generation leptons $\tau'$, predicted by extensions of the SM with additional $\mathrm{U}(1)'$ gauge symmetries~\cite{Hewett:2009bw}, fall into the same category and can carry single ($|Q|=1\,e$) or even double ($|Q|=2\,e$) electric charge, the latter being produced either via a modified Drell-Yan process or through the resonant decay of a heavy $Z'$ boson as shown in \cref{fig:hscp_prod_lepton}.

The theoretical cross-section for HSCP production spans more than ten orders of magnitude at $\sqrt{s} = 13.6\,\mathrm{TeV}$, depending on the particle types and their masses; \cref{fig:xsec_theory} shows these results. 
The contrasts arise from strong versus electroweak production. At this energy, quark-antiquark fusion with gluon emission is favoured over Drell-Yan processes, producing more hadron-like HSCPs. On top of that, the gluino-gluon interaction, due to its colour-octet structure, boosts gluino production cross sections to about ten times those of squarks at equal mass, making these hypothetical particles the favoured HSCPs at the LHC. On the other hand, tau' couplings increase with electric charge, whereas stau couplings are lower than those of tau.

\subsubsection{HSCP signal simulation}

The HSCP signal samples used in this analysis are generated using the standard CMS Monte Carlo chain described in \cref{sec:MC}. Hadron-like HSCPs require two additional ingredients beyond the standard chain. Before the \textsc{Geant4} simulation, dedicated fragmentation routines hadronise them into R-hadrons, with a specified partitioning among mesonic, baryonic, and gluino-ball bound states; during propagation, their nuclear interactions with the detector material are simulated explicitly to reproduce the associated charge-flipping. For lepton-like HSCPs, these steps reduce to the propagation of a free charged particle.



\subsubsection{Observable signatures}

Because of their large mass, HSCPs are non-relativistic, with typically $0.1 \lesssim \beta\gamma \lesssim 1$. Different physical consequences follow directly from this fact and constitute the experimental handles used to identify them:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anomalously high ionisation.} The Bethe-Bloch description of energy loss by ionisation in matter predicts a steep rise of d$E/$d$x$ for $\beta\gamma\lesssim 1$, before reaching the broad minimum characteristic of MIPs around $\beta\gamma\approx 3$--$4$. A heavy, slow-moving particle traversing the silicon tracker, therefore, deposits substantially more charge per unit length than any prompt SM track of comparable momentum, providing a powerful discriminating variable across nearly the full HSCP mass range. This is the primary handle exploited in the analysis presented in this thesis.

    \item \textbf{Extended time-of-flight.} For the same kinematic reason, the time taken by an HSCP to traverse the muon system is significantly longer than for a relativistic muon. This delay can be measured using drift-tube and CSC timing information and complements the d$E/$d$x$ signature, particularly for lepton-like HSCPs, which leave clean, isolated, high-momentum tracks. The same late arrival also motivates dedicated muon-scouting streams with relaxed timing requirements, which preserve sensitivity to slow candidates that would otherwise fail the standard prompt-muon trigger logic \cite{cmscollaboration2026searchheavylonglivedcharged}.

    \item \textbf{Missing transverse energy.} A third, complementary handle is essential for hadron-like HSCPs, which hadronise into R-hadrons and may flip their electric charge through successive nuclear interactions in the calorimeter. A state produced as charged can, in particular, exit the calorimeter as neutral, leaving no hit in the muon system and thus escaping muon-based reconstruction, thus making the muon triggers less efficient. These corresponding events could be triggered and selected through a MET-based path. This topology plays an exciting role for gluino and squark R-hadrons when hadronisation favours the neutral state. As a result, it serves as a key pillar of the analysis presented in this thesis.
\end{itemize}

Together, these three handles define the experimental signatures on which the LHC HSCP programme rests. The next section reviews the current state of the art in HSCP searches in the ATLAS and CMS collaborations, outlines the complementary roles of their respective analysis channels, and situates the work presented in this thesis within the broader landscape.


\subsection{Current experimental status of HSCP searches}
\label{sec:hscp_status}

Searches for HSCPs at colliders date back to the LEP era, but the most stringent constraints today come from the two general-purpose LHC experiments, CMS and ATLAS. Both use the slow-velocity signature expected for any heavy charged particle produced, but the two collaborations employ different sub-detectors. CMS reconstructs the anomalous ionisation $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x$ in the silicon tracker, optionally combined with the time-of-flight (TOF) measured in the muon chambers, while ATLAS uses the pixel $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x$ together with the time-of-flight of the hadronic calorimeter and muon sub-detector. The two strategies are complementary in the regions of lifetime they probe and in the signal models for which they are most sensitive.

\subsubsection{Latest CMS results}

The early Run--2 CMS HSCP analysis~\cite{HSCP_Run2_early} was performed on $2.5~\text{fb}^{-1}$ of data recorded in 2015 at $\sqrt{s} = 13~\text{TeV}$, combining the high ionisation search within the silicon tracker with one that adds the muon TOF under muon and MET triggers. No significant excess was observed, and the resulting lower mass exclusion limits at 95\% CL reached $1610~\text{GeV}$ for stable gluinos hadronising into R-hadrons (with a $10\%$ $\tilde{g}\tilde{g}$ bound-state fraction), $1040~\text{GeV}$ for stable top squarks, $490~\text{GeV}$ for GMSB tau sleptons, and $510~\text{GeV}$ ($680~\text{GeV}$) for Drell-Yan fourth-generation leptons of charge $|Q| = 1e$ ($|Q| = 2e$).

The full Run--2 results~\cite{HSCP_Run2} extends the search to the 2017--2018 dataset, corresponding to $101~\text{fb}^{-1}$ of $\sqrt{s} =13~\text{TeV}$ collisions collected after the Phase-1 pixel upgrade. Two independent methods are deployed in parallel: a mass-reconstruction strategy that combines strip ionisation with measured momentum, and a strictly ionisation-based approach that exploits the independence of the strip and pixel measurements via a newly developed pixel discriminator. Both methods benefit from the larger event sample, the additional pixel layer and data-driven background estimation techniques. HSCPs are selected through muon triggers, making the selection more sensitive to lepton-like particles. No significant excess over the SM expectation is observed, and the upper cross-section limits, and thus the corresponding mass exclusions, are improved relative to the early Run--2 results~\cite{HSCP_Run2_early} for all signal hypotheses considered. The mass exclusion limits for each signal benchmark are obtained at the intersection of theory and observation, as illustrated in \cref{fig:massexclu_hscp_run2}, and are listed in \cref{tab:MassLimits_Run2}. The mass method provides slightly stronger expected limits for signal hypotheses with large mass because it benefits from a very low background in this regime. However, the background in the mass spectrum is not negligible below 600~GeV, resulting in better limits from the ionisation method.

This analysis also includes a dedicated interpretation in which a heavy $Z'$ boson decays into a pair of doubly charged $\tau'$ leptons. This signal hypothesis is specifically introduced to provide sensitivity to the excess reported by ATLAS (described below), whose kinematics ($\beta\gamma \approx 1$) disfavour conventional slow-moving HSCP interpretations but are consistent with boosted $\tau'$ of charge $|Q|=2$ produced from the decay of a much heavier $Z'$~\cite{Giudice:2022bpq}. The configuration that would best account for the excess ($m_{Z'} \approx 5~\text{TeV}$ and $m_{\tau'} = 600~\text{GeV}$) is directly probed: for $m_{\tau'} = 600~\text{GeV}$, CMS sets a 95\% CL upper limit on the signal cross section of $0.047~\text{fb}$, which lies below the production rate required to reproduce the ATLAS observation. The $Z' \to \tau'\tau'$ interpretation of the ATLAS excess is therefore not confirmed by CMS.

\subsubsection{Complementary ATLAS searches}

The full Run--2 analysis based on $139~\text{fb}^{-1}$~\cite{HSCP_ATLAS_2022} reported seven events in the inclusive signal region, inside the mass window $[1.1, 2.8]~\text{TeV}$, against an expectation of $0.7 \pm 0.4$, corresponding to a local (global) significance of $3.6\,(3.3)~\sigma$. The shape of the excess is consistent with a $2.2~\text{TeV}$ gluino with a $10~\text{ns}$ lifetime, but the measured $\beta\gamma \approx 1$ for the candidate events disfavours a slow-moving R-hadron interpretation. Four of the seven candidates are nonetheless found to be muon-like, and would therefore yield a similar experimental signature in CMS. The associated mass limits on stable gluino R-hadrons reach $\approx 2.05~\text{TeV}$ and extend up to $2.27~\text{TeV}$ for a gluino lifetime of $20~\text{ns}$ in the scenario $m(\tilde{\chi}^0_1) = 100~\text{GeV}$.

A more recent ATLAS analysis~\cite{HSCP_ATLAS_2025} reprocesses the same $140~\text{fb}^{-1}$ Run--2 dataset with an updated selection that combines the pixel ionisation and the calorimeter TOF in two distinct signal regions: a $\beta$-search targeting single high-$p_T$ tracks with anomalous $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x$ and delayed TOF, and a di-track search selecting opposite-sign track pairs with large ionisation. The analysis is sensitive to lifetimes greater than 3 ns and to masses between $200~\text{GeV}$ and $3~\text{TeV}$, providing updated constraints on long-lived R-hadrons, charginos and tau sleptons that extend beyond the previous limits over a broad lifetime range.

The corresponding exclusion contours are shown in \cref{fig:massexclu_atlas_run2}. The di-track search excludes pair-produced staus up to $\approx 600~\text{GeV}$ around $\tau \approx 30~\text{ns}$ (top left), the $\beta$-search excludes charginos up to a plateau of $\approx 1.3~\text{TeV}$ (top right, no CMS interpretations so far), and gluino R-hadrons are constrained up to $\approx 2.27~\text{TeV}$ both in the detector intermediate (bottom left) and stable regime (bottom right). The sensitivity drops at the shortest lifetimes ($\lesssim 3~\text{ns}$), where the HSCP decays before reaching the hadronic calorimeter, rendering the TOF measurement ineffective. For comparison, the CMS Run--2 mass limits, summarised in~\cref{tab:MassLimits_Run2}, are obtained under the detector-stable hypothesis only: the Run--2 ATLAS analysis provides better exclusion for gluinos, while CMS yields the strongest constraints on lepton-like and Drell-Yan signals, including the $Z' \to \tau'\tau'$ interpretation that has no ATLAS counterpart.
