\section{Natural units and conventions}

The International System of Units (SI) is used in macroscopic sciences and groups the second (s, the unit of time), metre (m, length), kilogram (kg, mass), ampere (A, electric current), kelvin (K, thermodynamic temperature), mole (mol, amount of substance), and candela (cd, luminous intensity). However, when it comes to small, fast and highly energetic objects, manipulating those units might not be relevant (too small or too high values). Therefore, another set of units is preferred in particle physics to reflect the natural length and time scales encountered: the \textit{natural units}. Thus, [kg, m, s] is replaced by [$\hbar,\,c,$ GeV]. For instance, mass are now expressed in GeV/$c^2$ and time in $\mathrm{GeV}^{-1}\hbar^{-1}$. To simplify algebraic expressions, $\hbar$ and $c$ are set to unity. Taking the natural units, combined with the Heaviside-Lorentz ones to get Maxwell's equations taking the same form as with SI units, we get $\hbar=c=\varepsilon_0=\mu_0=1$, leading to:

\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{[energy]=[mass]=[momentum]=[time]^{-1}=[length]^{-1}=eV}
\end{equation*}
where the electronvolt (eV) is the norm. As an example, a proton of mass $m_p=1.673\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}=938\,\mathrm{GeV}$.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

From now on, natural units will be the default. 


\section{The Standard Model of particle physics}

\subsection{General overview}

The Standard Model of particle physics~\cite{Glashow1961, Weinberg1967, Salam1968}  is the theoretical framework describing all known elementary particles and their interactions, except gravity. Built on the mathematical foundation of quantum field theory and local gauge symmetries, it describes three of the four fundamental forces: the electromagnetic, weak, and strong interactions, unified under the gauge group $\mathrm{SU}(3)_c \otimes \mathrm{SU}(2)_L \otimes \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$. Developed primarily during the 1960s and 1970s, the Standard Model was completed with the discovery of the Higgs boson by the ATLAS and CMS
collaborations in 2012~\cite{ATLAS_Higgs, CMS_Higgs}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Elementary particles are classified into two categories according to their spin. Fermions carry half-integer spin and constitute the matter content of the universe, while bosons carry integer spin and mediate the fundamental interactions.

\subsubsection{Gauge bosons}

The gauge bosons mediate the fundamental interactions. The photon $\gamma$, the massless mediator of the electromagnetic interaction, couples to all electrically charged particles. The weak interaction is mediated by the massive $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ bosons, whose masses arise from spontaneous electroweak symmetry breaking, as discussed in Section~\ref{subsection:BEH}. The strong interaction is mediated by eight massless gluons, which carry colour charge and therefore interact among themselves in addition to coupling to quarks. An additional scalar boson, the Higgs boson $H$, is a direct consequence of the symmetry-breaking mechanism and is responsible for the generation of particle masses. 

\subsubsection{Fermions}

The fermions are arranged in three generations of increasing mass, each containing two quarks and two leptons.

Leptons are fermions which are not affected by the strong interaction. Each generation contains a charged lepton and an associated neutrino: the electron $e$ and $\nu_e$, the muon $\mu$ and $\nu_\mu$, and the tau $\tau$ and $\nu_\tau$. The charged leptons carry electric charge $-1$ and interact via the electromagnetic and weak interactions, while neutrinos are electrically neutral and interact only weakly. 

Quarks, on the other hand, are fermions sensitive to all the standard model interactions. Each generation contains an up-type quark of electric charge $+2/3$ and a down-type quark of electric charge $-1/3$: the up $u$ and down $d$, the charm $c$ and strange $s$, and the top $t$ and bottom $b$. Due to colour confinement, quarks are never observed in isolation but are bound into colour-neutral composite objects called hadrons. The top quark is an exception, as its exceptionally short lifetime causes it to decay before hadronisation can occur.

Every fermion has an associated antiparticle of opposite charge and opposite spin projection. All charged particles of the second and third generations are unstable, decaying to lower-mass states through weak interactions.



\subsection{Foundations: quantum field theory}

As the Standard Model describes elementary particles at a high-energy scale, its formalism relies on quantum mechanics, special relativity, and field theory. 

Quantum mechanics, in its original formulation, is a non-relativistic theory. The Schrödinger equation treats time and space asymmetrically and assumes that the number of particles is fixed. This framework becomes inconsistent at high energies, where special relativity must be taken into account.

One needs to consider special relativity when dealing with particles moving at speeds far beyond those of everyday experience. This formalism imposes that the rest energy and mass are related by $E=mc^2$, so that when the energy of a process exceeds the rest mass energy of a particle, there is sufficient energy to create new particle-antiparticle pairs from the vacuum. Particle number is therefore no longer conserved, and a single-particle wave-function $\psi(\mathbf{x},t)$ is no longer an adequate description of the system.

The natural resolution is to abandon the wave-function as a fundamental object and instead promote the dynamical variable to a field $\phi(\mathbf{x},t)$, defined at every point in spacetime, so that particle creation and annihilation are naturally accommodated as excitations of the underlying field.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

These considerations form the foundation upon which Quantum Field Theory (QFT) is built. In this framework, the dynamics are governed by a Lagrangian density $\mathcal{L}(\phi_i, \partial_\mu \phi_i)$, and the central object is the action
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{S} = \int \mathcal{L}\left( \phi_i, \partial_\mu \phi_i \right) \, \mathrm{d}^4x \,,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}$ must be a scalar for $\mathcal{S}$ to be invariant under Lorentz transformations. The principle of least action, $\delta \mathcal{S} = 0$, then yields the Euler-Lagrange (EL) equations of motion,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \phi_i} = \partial_\mu \left( \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \left(\partial_\mu \phi_i\right)} \right),
\end{equation}
which governs the classical evolution of each field $\phi_i$. 

Quantisation is subsequently achieved by imposing canonical commutation relations on the fields and their conjugate momenta, promoting the classical field theory to a full quantum field theory.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

According to Emmy Noether's theorem, presented in 1915 \cite{Noether1918}, every continuous symmetry of the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}$ leads to a conserved quantity. Canonical examples include time-translation invariance implying energy conservation, spatial-translation invariance implying momentum conservation, and rotational invariance implying conservation of angular momentum. This result is of particular importance in the context of gauge invariance, as it provides the fundamental link between local symmetries and the conservation laws that support modern quantum field theories.



\subsection{Free fields and wave equations}

Describing the behaviour of a field interacting with other fields in the space-time could not be handled using the Schrödinger equation, as an asymmetry between the first derivative in time and the second derivative in space makes it non-Lorentz invariant. Therefore, this equation is irrelevant for describing relativistic particles.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The foundation of relativistic quantum mechanics started with Oskar Klein and Walter Gordon, who introduced in 1926 the Klein-Gordon equation that took the Einstein relation dispersion, $E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2+m^2$, and applied it to a field $\phi_i(\textbf{x},t)$ as an upgraded version of the Schrödinger equation, taking the relativistic aspect. This led to the Klein-Gordon (KG) equation that explains the behaviour of a scalar field (spin-0) particle with a non-zero mass. 

Despite an apparent success of joining together quantum mechanics and special relativity, the KG equation leads to a density of probability $\rho=2|N|^2E$ that can be negative depending on the sign of $E$, with $N$ being the normalisation factor. As the dispersion relation $E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2+m^2$ allows for negative energy solutions, it is nonsense for physical objects with negative energies to have a negative probability of presence.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

This led to Paul Dirac's work in 1928, which resolved this issue by considering an energy-mass-momentum equation that introduced a first-order derivative. The new dispersion relation becomes $E\mathds{1}_{4\times4}=\mathbi{\alpha}_{4\times4}\cdot\textbf{p}+\beta_{4\times4}m$, where $\alpha_i=\alpha_{4\times4\,,\,i}$ and $\beta=\beta_{4\times4}$ are $4\times4$ matrices and $\mathds{1}$ the identity matrix. After working out the details of the calculations, the matrices can be expressed as follows:

\begin{equation}
    \alpha_i = \left( \begin{array}{cc} 0 & \sigma_i \\ \sigma_i & 0 \end{array} \right) 
    \quad \mathrm{and} \quad \beta= \left( \begin{array}{cc} \mathds{1}_2 & 0 \\ 0 & -\mathds{1}_2 \end{array} \right)
\end{equation}
where $i=x,y,z$ and $\sigma_i$ the three Pauli matrices. Those matrices acts on four wave-functions $\psi_i$ forming the Dirac spinor $\psi=(\psi_1 \, \psi_2 \, \psi_3 \, \psi_4)^\mathrm{T}$.

Following that, the Dirac equation is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi(\mathbf{x},t)-m\psi(\mathbf{x},t)=0
\end{equation}
arising from the Dirac Lagrangian:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_\mathrm{D} = i \overline{\psi}\gamma^\mu \partial_\mu \psi - m \overline{\psi} \psi
\end{equation}
with $\gamma^\mu$ the Dirac matrices such that $\gamma^0=\beta$ and $\gamma^i=\beta\alpha^i$, and $\overline{\psi}=\psi^\dagger\gamma^0$, the adjoint spinor.

Those new degrees of freedom introduced by the gamma matrices naturally describe the spin of the fundamental fermions and anti-fermions introduced in 1925 by Wolfgang Pauli. The negative-energy solutions are interpreted as negative-energy particles propagating backwards in time, or equivalently, as the physical positive-energy antiparticles propagating forwards in time. The prediction of antimatter was later confirmed with the discovery of the positron in 1932 by Carl David Anderson \cite{Anderson1932}.

Consequently, the solution of the Dirac equation provides the relativistic quantum mechanical description of spin-half particles and antiparticles.


\subsection{Quantum electrodynamics}

One missing point of the equations listed above is the absence of interactions between the fields. If one wants to include interactions between fields, the impact of local and global transformations of the field on the associated Lagrangian must be described. 

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The Dirac Lagrangian remains unchanged under a global transformation, $\psi \rightarrow e^{i\theta}\psi$, but it's not the case anymore for a local transformation, $\psi \rightarrow e^{i\theta(\textbf{x})}\psi$, where the phase $\theta(\textbf{x})$ depends on the space-time coordinates.

Imposing this invariance leads to the introduction of a vectorial field $A_\mu$ which transforms as $A_\mu \rightarrow A_\mu + \partial_\mu\theta/Q_\mathrm{em}$, such that the Dirac Lagrangian is added to an interaction term $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}$ described as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}} = - Q_\mathrm{em} \overline{\psi} \gamma^\mu A_\mu \psi \, ,
\end{equation}
where the variable $Q_\mathrm{em}$ corresponds to the electrical charge that is a conserved quantity through Noether's theorem for the U(1) invariance of the Dirac Lagrangian. This additional $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}$ term denotes the interaction between a fermion and the vector field. The vector field (spin-1 boson) $A_\mu$ has no mass term and can be interpreted as a massless gauge boson.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The dynamics of this gauge boson are captured by a kinematic term in the Lagrangian. It comes that this kinematic therm arise from the Maxwell Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Maxwell}}$ defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Maxwell}} = -\frac{1}{4}F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu} \quad \mathrm{with} \, F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu
\end{equation}

One could mention that this vector field is, in fact, the massless photon, which is found to be the gauge boson of electromagnetism. 

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The consistent quantum field theoretical description of the electromagnetic interaction was developed independently by Richard Feynman, Julian Schwinger and Shin'ichir\={o} Tomonaga between 1946 and 1950, a body of work collectively known as Quantum electrodynamics (QED) and summarised by the Lagrangian:

\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QED}} & = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{D}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Maxwell}} \\
    & = i \overline{\psi}\gamma^\mu \partial_\mu \psi - m \overline{\psi} \psi - Q_\mathrm{em} \overline{\psi} \gamma^\mu A_\mu \psi -\frac{1}{4}F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}
\end{align}

This theory is referred to as the U(1)$_\mathrm{em}$ as the QED Lagrangian is invariant under any local U(1) transformation. This formalism explains fermion pair creation and annihilation, as well as the Drell-Yan process.


\subsection{Weak interaction}

One thing that QED failed to describe is the change in the particles' flavour in a given process. 

\vspace{0.5 cm}

First modelled by Enrico Fermi in 1933, he explained the neutron decay and later the muon decay, introducing a coupling constant $G_F\, \mathrm{[GeV^{-2}]}$ describing the interaction. As this vision breaks unitarity, with each new Fermi vertex adding a square-energy term to it, one could say that this theory was a first step toward a hidden interaction that could be probed at higher energies.

To address the unitarity issue and follow QED examples, it was supposed that the interaction was mediated by a massive charged gauge boson, later identified as the $W^\pm$ bosons. As this interaction occurs at a short distance, the term "weak" has been adopted to describe such processes.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Despite QED, the weak interaction mediated by the $W^\pm$ bosons is not symmetric under parity transformation\footnote{In particle physics, parity is a discrete symmetry corresponding to the spatial inversion $\mathbf{x} \to -\mathbf{x}$, under which a physical system is said to
be parity-symmetric if its governing laws are invariant under this transformation.}. The Chien-Shiung Wu experiment \cite{Wu1957} demonstrated in 1956 that the weak interaction violates parity symmetry and is therefore chiral. By studying the $\beta$-decay of polarised $^{60}$Co nuclei, Wu provided the first direct experimental evidence for parity violation in nature: certain physical processes occur at different rates than their mirror-image counterparts.

Based on this, a pure vector current is not allowed --- as is the case in QED with $j^\mu=\overline{\psi}\gamma^\mu\psi$ --- otherwise the weak interaction would not violate parity. Under parity transformation, a pure vector current $\bar{\psi}\gamma^\mu\psi$ is even, while an axial-vector current $\bar{\psi}\gamma^\mu\gamma^5\psi$, with  $\gamma^5=i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3$, is odd; consequently, any mixed $V \pm A$ coupling contains a cross term that is odd under parity, leading to maximal parity violation. The $V-A$ structure is the one that matches the experiment observations, and can be rewritten using the left-handed chiral\footnote{The chirality is defined as the eigenvalue of the $\gamma^5$ matrix, with eigenstates projected by $P_{L,R} = (1 \mp \gamma^5)/2$; it is an intrinsic property that is a direct consequence of the Lorentz structure under which the field transforms.} projector $P_L = (1 - \gamma^5)/2$, yielding $\bar{\psi}\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma^5)\psi = 2\bar{\psi}_L\gamma^\mu\psi_L$, which shows that the weak interaction couples exclusively to left-handed fermions and right-handed anti-fermions. As a direct consequence of the impact of this $V-A$ structure, one could compute the matrix element of the charged pion decay and observe that it would preferably decay into a muon instead of an electron (suppressed by a factor $10^{-4}$).

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Similarly, the weak interaction is completed by a neutral current mediated by the $Z^0$ boson, whose existence is required to preserve $W^\pm$ production divergences at high energy, thereby preserving unitarity. The first experimental evidence for neutral currents was found at the Gargamelle bubble chamber at CERN in 1973, leading to the direct discovery of the $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ bosons in 1983 \cite{WandZ}.

Weak interactions manifest across a broad range of energy scales, from low-energy processes such as nuclear beta decay and pion decay to intermediate scales with $\mathrm{K}^0$ oscillations, and up to the direct production of $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ bosons at colliders.


\subsection{Quantum chromodynamics}

One last piece that needs to be modelled is the strong interaction. It is responsible for binding quarks into hadrons and for the cohesion of atomic nuclei, and is described by Quantum chromodynamics (QCD), a non-abelian gauge theory based on the colour symmetry group $\mathrm{SU}(3)_c$. Note that a non-abelian structure is required because the gauge bosons themselves carry the charge of the interaction and must therefore self-interact. This is in contrast to $\mathrm{U}(1)_{\mathrm{em}}$ QED, where the photon is electrically neutral and carries no self-coupling.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The first evidence for a strong nuclear force came from the simple observation that atomic nuclei are stable: the electromagnetic repulsion between protons must be overcome by a stronger, short-range interaction. In 1935, Hideki Yukawa proposed the first model where the force was mediated by the pion, later discovered in 1947, which could explain the confinement of the force to nuclear scales. With the proliferation of hadron discoveries in the 1950s and 1960s, Murray Gell-Mann and George Zweig proposed the quark model independently in 1964. In this structure, hadrons are composite objects built from a small number of fundamental constituents called quarks, organised according to the SU(3) flavour symmetry group. Mesons are quark--antiquark pairs $q\bar{q}$, while baryons are three-quark bound states $qqq$. All observed hadrons are colour-neutral (colour singlets), a property known as colour confinement.

The reality of quarks as physical constituents was established in the late 1960s, when the structure of the proton was probed by electrons and revealed scattering off point-like, essentially free constituents. Feynman's parton model identified these with quarks and gluons, each carrying a momentum fraction $x$ of the proton, distributed according to the parton distribution functions (PDFs) $f_i(x, Q^2)$.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The formalism of QCD, developed in the early 1970s, introduced eight gauge bosons associated with the eight generators of $\mathrm{SU}(3)_c$. Since these bosons bind quarks together, analogous to a glue holding the nucleus cohesive, they are called gluons. Gluons carry colour charge and therefore couple only to other colour-charged particles, such as quarks and gluons themselves.

The QCD Lagrangian is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{QCD}} = \sum_{f} \bar{\psi}^f \left( i\gamma^\mu D_\mu - m_f \right) \psi^f
    - \frac{1}{4} G^{a}_{\mu\nu}G^{\mu\nu}_{a},
\end{equation}
where the sum runs explicitly over quark flavours $f$, each with mass $m_f$. The covariant
derivative
\begin{equation}
    D_\mu = \partial_\mu - i g_s \frac{\lambda^a}{2} A^a_\mu
\end{equation}
ensures local $\mathrm{SU}(3)_c$ gauge invariance, where $g_s$ is the strong coupling constant, $A^a_\mu$ are the eight gluon fields, and $\lambda^a/2$ the eight Gell-Mann matrices, generators of $\mathrm{SU}(3)_c$. The gluon field strength tensor is:

\begin{equation}
    G^a_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A^a_\nu - \partial_\nu A^a_\mu
    + g_s f^{a}_{{\,\,\,bc}} A^b_\mu A^c_\nu,
\end{equation}
where $f^{abc}$ are the structure constants of $SU(3)_c$. The non-linear term generates three- and four-gluon vertices, a direct consequence of the non-Abelian nature of the group.


\subsection{Electroweak unification}

So far, the QED and QCD formalisms contain only massless gauge bosons; thus, their Lagrangians can be constructed straightforwardly from gauge invariance alone, without any additional complication. The weak theory otherwise, while successfully describing charged-current weak processes via $W^\pm$ exchange, is not a complete theory on its own. Introduced without an underlying gauge principle, the boson masses must be inserted by hand, breaking gauge invariance and rendering the theory non-renormalisable. Furthermore, the requirement of a neutral current mediator $Z^0$ and the restoration of unitarity in weak scattering amplitudes cannot be accommodated without extending the symmetry group. 

\vspace{0.5 cm}

These shortcomings motivated the description of the weak interaction in terms of an $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$ gauge theory, which naturally accommodates its chiral structure and introduces three gauge boson W$^{(1)}$, W$^{(2)}$ and W$^{(3)}$, with linear combination of the first two leading to the two massive gauge bosons $W^\pm$. The subscript $L$ indicates that the SU(2) gauge symmetry acts exclusively on left-handed fermion doublets, reflecting the maximal parity violation of the weak interaction.

However, $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$ alone cannot reproduce the correct electric charges of the fermions, nor does it accommodate a massless neutral gauge boson to be identified with the photon. Thus, suggesting that electromagnetism and the weak interaction are not independent, but two manifestations of a single underlying symmetry. The introduction of an additional $\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ gauge factor, associated with weak hypercharge $Y$, allows the $Z^0$ boson and the photon to emerge as a linear combination of W$^{(3)}$ and the $\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ boson B. This unification of the weak and electromagnetic interactions into the gauge group $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L \otimes \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$, within which boson masses arise dynamically, and renormalisability is restored, was achieved independently by Sheldon Glashow, Steven Weinberg, and Abdus Salam in the 1960s \cite{Glashow1961, Weinberg1967, Salam1968}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The electroweak theory assigns two quantum numbers to each fermion: the weak isospin $T$ and the weak hypercharge $Y$. Left-handed fermions are grouped into $SU(2)_L$ doublets of weak isospin $T = 1/2$,

\begin{equation}
    L = \begin{pmatrix} \nu_\ell \\ \ell^- \end{pmatrix}_L, \qquad
    Q = \begin{pmatrix} u \\ d' \end{pmatrix}_L,
\end{equation}
while right-handed fermions $e_R$, $u_R$ and $d_R$ are $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$ singlets with $T = 0$ and therefore do not couple to the $W^\pm$ bosons, consistently with the $V-A$ structure of the weak interaction discussed previously. The weak hypercharge $Y$ is linked through the electric charge $Q_{\mathrm{em}}$ and the third component of weak isospin $T_3$ with $Q_{\mathrm{em}} = T_3 + \frac{Y}{2}$.

The $\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ gauge boson $B_\mu$ couples to all fermions with a strength proportional to their hypercharge, while the three $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$ gauge bosons $W^a_\mu$ couple only to left-handed doublets. Therefore, to ensure gauge invariance, the covariant derivative takes different forms depending on the chirality of the fermions:

\begin{align}
    {D_\mu}_L &= \partial_\mu - i g_W T_a W^a_\mu - i g' \frac{Y}{2} B_\mu \,, \\
    \label{eq:covariantDerivativeWeak}
    {D_\mu}_R &= \partial_\mu - i g' \frac{Y}{2} B_\mu \,,
\end{align}
where $g_W$ and $g'$ are the $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$ and $\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ coupling constants respectively, and $T_a = \sigma_a/2$ the generators of $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$ with $\sigma_a$ the Pauli matrices. 

The interaction between the fermions is described by the Lagrangian:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \mathcal{L}_\mathrm{fermions} &= 
        \overline{Q}_j i \gamma^\mu {D_\mu}_L Q_j + \overline{u}_j i \gamma^\mu {D_\mu}_R u_j + \overline{d}_j i \gamma^\mu {D_\mu}_R d_j \\
        & \quad + \overline{L}_j i \gamma^\mu {D_\mu}_L L_j + \overline{e}_j i \gamma^\mu {D_\mu}_R e_j
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
where the subscript $j$ sums over the three generations of fermions; $Q$, $u$ and $d$ being the left-handed doublet, right-handed singlet up and down quark fields; $L$ and $e$ the left-handed doublet and right-handed singlet electron fields.

The kinetic terms for the gauge fields are:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{gauge}} = -\frac{1}{4} W^a_{\mu\nu} W^{a\,\mu\nu}
    - \frac{1}{4} B_{\mu\nu} B^{\mu\nu},
\end{equation}
where $W^a_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu W^a_\nu - \partial_\nu W^a_\mu + g_W\, \varepsilon^{a}{}_{bc} W^b_\mu W^c_\nu$ and $B_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu B_\nu - \partial_\nu B_\mu$ are the $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$ and $\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ field strength tensors respectively, with $\varepsilon$ the antisymmetric structure constant of the SU(2) group.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

However, the total Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{fermions}+\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{gauge}$, contains no mass terms: an explicit Dirac mass $m\bar{\psi}\psi = m(\bar{\psi}_R\psi_L + \bar{\psi}_L\psi_R)$ couples a left-handed doublet to a right-handed singlet and is therefore not invariant under $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L \otimes \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$. Similarly, an explicit mass term $m^2 W^\mu_a W_{a\mu}$ for the gauge bosons breaks gauge invariance. Since the weak bosons are observed to be massive, a dynamical mechanism is required to generate these masses without spoiling the gauge symmetry: this is achieved by introducing a scalar field whose potential admits a non-zero vacuum expectation value, spontaneously breaking the $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L \otimes \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ symmetry down to $\mathrm{U}(1)_{\mathrm{em}}$.


\subsection{Electroweak symmetry breaking}
\label{subsection:BEH}

The problem of mass generation in gauge theories was resolved in the 1960s through a series of independent contributions. Yoichiro Nambu and Jeffrey Goldstone had shown in 1960-1961 that the spontaneous breaking of a continuous global symmetry leads to the appearance of massless scalar bosons (the Goldstone bosons). In the context of a local gauge symmetry, however, Robert Brout and François Englert, Peter Higgs, and Gerald Guralnik, Carl Hagen, and Tom Kibble independently demonstrated in 1964 that these would-be Goldstone bosons are absorbed by the gauge bosons, which acquire a longitudinal degree of freedom and thus a mass. This is the Brout--Englert--Higgs (BEH) mechanism\footnote{The mechanism is commonly referred to as the BEH mechanism, though Peter Higgs himself preferred the acronym ABEGHHK'tH, acknowledging the contributions of Anderson, Brout, Englert, Guralnik, Hagen, Higgs, Kibble, and 't~Hooft.}. The remaining physical degree of freedom manifests as a massive scalar particle, namely the Higgs boson $H$. In the history of the universe, this symmetry breaking is believed to have occurred shortly after the Big Bang, when the temperature of the universe dropped below approximately $160$~GeV~\cite{ElectroWeak_Temperature}, triggering the electroweak phase transition and giving rise to the massive gauge bosons observed today.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The BEH mechanism is implemented by introducing a complex scalar $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$ doublet with weak hypercharge $Y = 1$:

\begin{equation}
    \phi = \begin{pmatrix} \phi^+ \\ \phi^0 \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with the scalar potential

\begin{equation}
    V(\phi) = \mu^2 \phi^\dagger \phi + \lambda (\phi^\dagger \phi)^2,
\end{equation}
where $\lambda > 0$ ensures non divergence of the minimal value of $V(\phi)$. For $\mu^2~<~0$, the potential develops a continuous family of degenerate minima at $|\phi|^2 = -\mu^2 / 2\lambda \equiv v^2/2$, forming a ''Mexican hat'' shape depicted in \cref{fig:higgsshape}. The vacuum is not unique, as it lies on a circle of radius $v$ referred to as the vacuum expectation value, and the choice of a particular ground state spontaneously breaks the $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L \otimes \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ symmetry. Selecting the unitary gauge, the field is expanded around the vacuum expectation value as:

\begin{equation}
    \phi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ v + h(x) \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $h$ is the physical Higgs boson field. The three would-be Goldstone bosons associated with the broken generators are absorbed as longitudinal polarisations of the $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ bosons, while the $\mathrm{U}(1)_{\mathrm{em}}$ symmetry remains unbroken, leaving the photon massless.

    \label{fig:higgsshape}
\end{figure}

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The masses of the gauge bosons arise from the covariant kinetic term of the Higgs doublet:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Higgs}} = ({D^\mu}_L \phi)^\dagger ({D_\mu}_L \phi) - V(\phi),
\end{equation}
where ${D_\mu}_L$ is the electroweak covariant derivative acting on a left-handed doublet. Substituting the vacuum expectation value into this term generates quadratic terms in the gauge fields:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Higgs}} =& \quad \, \frac{1}{2} (\partial_\mu h) (\partial^\mu h) \\
    &+ \frac{1}{8} g_W^2 \left( W_\mu^{(1)}+iW_\mu^{(2)} \right) \left( W^{(1) \,\mu}-iW^{(2) \,\mu} \right) \left(v+h(x)\right)^2 \\
    &+ \frac{1}{8} \left( g_W W_\mu^{(3)}-g'B_\mu \right) \left( g_W W^{(3) \,\mu}-g'B^\mu \right) \left(v+h(x)\right)^2 \\
    &- \lambda v^2 h^2 - \lambda v h^3 -\frac{1}{4}\lambda h^4 \, .
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

The first therm express the kinematics of the Higgs field itself. The second term is in fact the kinematics of the two W$^\pm$ bosons, which can be expressed as a linear combination of $W^{(1)}$ and $W^{(2)}$:
\begin{equation}
    W^\pm_\mu = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left( W^{(1)}_\mu \mp i W^{(2)}_\mu \right),
\end{equation}

Therefore, developing the second term of $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Higgs}}$ brings to light the mass of the W$^\pm$ bosons with $m_W=\frac{1}{2}g_Wv$ and also an interaction between the Higgs field and the weak gauge field.

Finally the developing the last term involving the neutral $W_\mu^{(3)}$ and $B_\mu$ bosons yields a $2\times 2$ mass matrix in the $(W^{(3)}_\mu, B_\mu)$ basis:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{v^2}{8}\left(W^{(3)}_\mu \,\,\, B_\mu\right) 
    \begin{pmatrix} g_W^2 & -g_Wg' \\ -g_Wg' & g'^2 \end{pmatrix}
    \left( \begin{array}{c} W^{(3) \,\mu} \\ B^\mu \end{array} \right)
    \label{eq:massZandA}
\end{equation}
where this matrix is real and symmetric, and can therefore be diagonalised in an orthonormal eigenbasis. The physical boson masses are given by the eigenvalues, while the eigenvectors $Z_\mu$ and $A_\mu$ --- corresponding to the $Z^0$ boson and the photon ---, are, by linear algebra, a combination of $W_\mu^{(3)}$ and $B_\mu$. 

In this mass eigenstate basis, \cref{eq:massZandA} becomes:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{v^2}{8}\left(Z_\mu \,\,\, A_\mu\right) 
    \begin{pmatrix} g_W^2+g'^2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
    \left( \begin{array}{c} Z^\mu \\ A^\mu \end{array} \right) = \frac{1}{2}\left(Z_\mu \,\,\, A_\mu\right) 
    \begin{pmatrix} m_Z^2 & 0 \\ 0 & m_A^2 \end{pmatrix}
    \left( \begin{array}{c} Z^\mu \\ A^\mu \end{array} \right)
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
    \left( \begin{array}{c} Z^\mu \\ A^\mu \end{array} \right) = 
    \begin{pmatrix} \cos \theta_W & -\sin \theta_W \\ \sin \theta_W & \cos \theta_W \end{pmatrix}
    \left( \begin{array}{c} W^{(3) \,\mu} \\ B^\mu \end{array} \right) \, .
\end{equation}

Introducing the Weinberg angle $\theta_W$ satisfying $\tan\theta_W=g'/g_W$. 

The photon remains massless as $m_A=0$ and the $Z^0$ boson acquire a non-zero mass $m_Z~=~\frac{1}{2}v\sqrt{g_W^2+g'^2}=m_W/\cos\theta_W$.

One could also remark that after this basis change, if $W_\mu^{(3)}$ and $B_\mu$ are substituted by their expression in terms of $Z_\mu$ and $A_\mu$ in \cref{eq:covariantDerivativeWeak}, it imposes the electromagnetic coupling constant $Q_\mathrm{em}$ to be related to $g_W$ and $g'$ by:

\begin{equation}
    Q_\mathrm{em} = g_W \sin\theta_W = g' \cos\theta_W,
\end{equation}
showing that electromagnetism is not an independent interaction but emerges after electroweak symmetry breaking.

Finally, the last terms of $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Higgs}}$ describe the Higgs self-interactions and give a mass for the Higgs boson, with $m_H~=~v\sqrt{2\lambda}$.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

So far, the BEH mechanism generates masses for the three weak bosons while leaving the photon massless. However, fermions remain massless after symmetry breaking, as an explicit Dirac mass term $-m\bar{\psi}\psi = -m\left(\bar{\psi}_R\psi_L + \bar{\psi}_L\psi_R\right)$ couples left-handed doublets to right-handed singlets and is therefore not invariant under $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L \otimes \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$. The same Higgs doublet $\phi$, however, allows the construction of gauge-invariant Yukawa couplings between left-handed doublets, right-handed singlets, and the Higgs field, which generate fermion masses dynamically after symmetry breaking.

The Yukawa Lagrangian takes the general form
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Yukawa}} = - y^f_{ij}\, \overline{\psi}_{L,i}\, \phi\, \psi_{R,j} + \mathrm{h.c.} \, ,
\end{equation}
where $y^f_{ij}$ are complex coupling constants and the indices $i,j$ run over the three fermion generations. After spontaneous symmetry breaking, the Yukawa couplings generate Dirac mass matrices $m^f_{ij} = y^f_{ij}\, v/\sqrt{2}$.:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Yukawa}}^\mathrm{EWSB}=  -\frac{v}{\sqrt{2}} y^f_{ij}\,\overline{\psi}_i \psi_j - \frac{y^f_{ij}}{\sqrt{2}}\, \overline{\psi}_i \psi_j\, h + \mathrm{h.c.}
\end{equation}

The physical fermion masses are obtained by diagonalising these matrices via independent unitary rotations in the up- and down-type quark sectors. Since these rotations do not generally coincide, the $W^\pm$ bosons can mediate transitions between quark generations, a misalignment parametrised by the Nicola Cabibbo--Makoto Kobayashi--Toshihide Maskawa (CKM) matrix. In the lepton sector, neutrinos are massless in the Standard Model, so the single unitary rotation can always be absorbed into a field redefinition, leaving no physical mixing matrix. Note that the last term of $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Yukawa}}^\mathrm{EWSB}$ describes the coupling between the Higgs boson and the fermions.


\subsection{The Standard Model Lagrangian}

The full Standard Model Lagrangian can now be written as the sum of four terms:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SM}} = 
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gauge}}
    + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fermions}}
    + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Higgs}}
    + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Yukawa}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gauge}}$ contains the kinetic and self-interaction terms of the gauge bosons of $\mathrm{SU}(3)_c \otimes \mathrm{SU}(2)_L \otimes \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ when adding the last term of $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{QCD}$ to it. $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fermions}}$ is the gauge-covariant kinetic terms of the fermion fields, with consideration of the quark interactions of $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{QCD}$ in addition. $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Higgs}}$ the Higgs kinetic term and potential, and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Yukawa}}$ the Yukawa couplings responsible for fermion mass generation. This Lagrangian, invariant under $\mathrm{SU}(3)_c \otimes \mathrm{SU}(2)_L \otimes \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ and spontaneously broken to $\mathrm{SU}(3)_c \otimes \mathrm{U}(1)_{\mathrm{em}}$ by the Higgs mechanism, constitutes the complete theoretical framework of the Standard Model.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The Standard Model contains 19 free parameters that must be determined experimentally:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gauge couplings (3):} the strong coupling $g_s$, the $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$     coupling $g_W$, and the $\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ coupling $g'$;
    \item \textbf{Higgs sector (2):} the vacuum expectation value $v$ and the Higgs boson mass $m_H$;
    \item \textbf{Fermion masses (9):} the six quark masses $m_u,\, m_d,\, m_s,\, m_c,\, m_b,\, m_t$ and the three charged lepton masses $m_e,\, m_\mu,\, m_\tau$, all generated via Yukawa couplings;
    \item \textbf{CKM matrix (4):} three mixing angles $\theta_{12},\, \theta_{13},\, \theta_{23}$ and one $CP$-violating phase\footnote{$CP$ violation refers to the breaking of the combined symmetry of charge conjugation $C$, which swaps particles with antiparticles, and parity $P$, implying that the laws of physics are not identical for matter and antimatter. Concretely, $CP$ violation manifests as a difference in the decay rates of a particle and its antiparticle, for instance $B^0 \to f$ occurring at a different rate than $\bar{B}^0 \to \bar{f}$.} $\delta$;
    \item \textbf{Strong $CP$ parameter (1):} $\theta_{\mathrm{QCD}}$, the parameter controlling $CP$ violation in the strong sector, arising from a topologically allowed gauge-invariant term in the QCD Lagrangian, experimentally consistent with zero.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations of the Standard Model}

Despite its remarkable predictive success, the Standard Model leaves several experimental observations unexplained and suffers from several theoretical shortcomings, providing strong motivation for physics beyond the Standard Model (BSM).

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\textbf{Neutrino masses and oscillations}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

In the Standard Model, neutrinos are strictly massless, as no right-handed neutrino field is introduced and no Yukawa coupling can be constructed for them. However, the observation of the transformation of a neutrino of one flavour into another during propagation, namely the neutrino oscillation \cite{NeutrinoOscillations}, constitutes direct experimental evidence that neutrinos are massive, since oscillations require non-zero mass eigenstates. Analogously to the quark sector, the mismatch between the neutrino mass eigenstates and the weak interaction eigenstates is parametrised by the Bruno Pontecorvo--Ziro Maki--Masami Nakagawa--Shoichi Sakata (PMNS) matrix, a $3\times 3$  unitary mixing matrix whose parameters, including a potentially $CP$-violating phase, are the subject of ongoing experimental investigation. The Standard Model must therefore be extended to accommodate neutrino masses, either via a Dirac mechanism requiring right-handed neutrinos, or via a Majorana mass term that would make neutrinos their own antiparticles.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\textbf{Matter--antimatter asymmetry}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

The Standard Model predicts that matter and antimatter should have been produced in equal amounts at the Big Bang, yet the observable universe is composed almost entirely of matter. This asymmetry requires, as formulated by Andrei Sakharov in 1967, baryon number violation, departure from thermal equilibrium, and $C$ and $CP$ violation. While the Standard Model contains a source of $CP$ violation through the complex phase of the CKM matrix, it is insufficient by several orders of magnitude to account for the observed baryon asymmetry of the universe. Additional sources of $CP$ violation, beyond those present in the Standard Model, are therefore required.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\textbf{Dark matter and dark energy}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

Multiple independent cosmological observations indicate the existence of a non-luminous, non-baryonic form of matter that accounts for approximately 27\% of the energy content of the universe. None of the Standard Model particles possesses the required properties: stability on cosmological timescales, negligible electromagnetic and strong interactions, and the correct density, making dark matter one of the most compelling arguments for BSM physics.

Observations of Type Ia supernovae in 1998 revealed that the expansion of the universe is accelerating \cite{Riess1998, Perlmutter1999}, attributed to a form of energy of unknown origin, namely dark energy, which constitutes approximately 68\% of the total energy budget. Similarly, no Standard Model particle can account for dark energy, and the vacuum energy estimated from quantum field theory exceeds the observed value by some 120 orders of magnitude.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\textbf{The hierarchy problem}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

The measured Higgs boson mass $m_H \approx 125$~GeV is some seventeen orders of magnitude below the Planck scale $\Lambda \sim 10^{19}$~GeV. In quantum field theory, the Higgs mass receives loop corrections proportional to the square of the highest energy scale in the theory; if that scale is $\Lambda$, these corrections are of order $10^{38}$~GeV$^2$, vastly exceeding $m_H^2 \sim 10^4$~GeV$^2$. Obtaining the correct physical mass, therefore, requires the bare mass parameter and the radiative corrections to cancel to one part in $10^{34}$, with no theoretical justification for such a precise cancellation. This unnatural fine-tuning is the hierarchy problem, and its resolution is one of the central motivations for BSM theories such as supersymmetry, which introduces a symmetry that exactly cancels the quadratic divergences.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\textbf{Absence of quantum gravity}
\vspace{0.5 cm}


The Standard Model does not describe gravitational interactions at the quantum level. General relativity, which describes gravity at macroscopic scales, is not renormalisable as a quantum field theory, and the two frameworks are fundamentally incompatible at energy scales approaching the Planck scale $\Lambda$. While gravity is negligible at collider energies, its absence from the Standard Model underscores the incompleteness of the theory. Although physical confirmation of a theoretical particle known as a graviton would account for it to a degree.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

On top of those main problems, the strong $CP$ problem persists, as does the question of gauge coupling unification: the three constants $g_s$, $g_W$, and $g'$ run with energy but do not converge to a single value within the Standard Model, hinting at a deeper unified structure. This non-exhaustive list of issues further highlights the theory's incompleteness.



\section{Beyond the Standard Model: supersymmetry and long-lived particles}

The convergence of these experimental and theoretical arguments strongly suggests that the Standard Model is an effective theory, valid at the energy scales currently accessible, but incomplete. The search for BSM physics is therefore one of the central goals of modern high-energy physics. One of the most theoretically motivated BSM frameworks is supersymmetry (SUSY), which addresses several of the shortcomings discussed above simultaneously and constitutes the theoretical foundation of the HSCP analysis presented in this thesis.

\subsection{Particle content of the minimal supersymmetric extension of the Standard Model}

SUSY postulates a symmetry relating bosons and fermions: to every bosonic degree of freedom corresponds a fermionic one with identical gauge quantum numbers and couplings. A first question is whether the known particles of the Standard Model  (SM) could already be paired into supermultiplets. The answer is negative: none of the known fermions can be paired with any of the known bosons, because their internal quantum numbers do not match.

The minimal solution is therefore to double the spectrum: each SM particle is associated with a new superpartner (or \textit{spartner}) differing by half a unit of spin. Quarks are paired with scalar \textit{squarks}, leptons with \textit{sleptons}, gauge bosons with spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ \textit{gauginos} (photino, zino, wino, gluino), and the Higgs sector with \textit{higgsinos}. The full content of the minimal supersymmetric extension of the Standard Model (MSSM) is summarised in \cref{tab:susy_particles}.

\subsection{Hints for TeV-scale SUSY}

Such a symmetry is particularly attractive because it provides a natural mechanism to stabilise the Higgs mass against quadratic divergences. Since bosonic and fermionic loops contribute with opposite signs, the residual one-loop correction to the Higgs mass takes the form:
\begin{equation}
    \delta m_H^2 \simeq \mathcal{O}\!\left( m_B^2 - m_F^2 \right) 
    \lesssim m_H^2 
    \;\Longleftrightarrow\; 
    \left| m_B^2 - m_F^2 \right| \lesssim 1 \, \mathrm{TeV}^2 ,
\end{equation}
where $m_B$ and $m_F$ denote, respectively, the masses of supersymmetric partner bosons and fermions. This relation makes it natural to expect the masses of the superpartners to lie around the TeV scale, which falls within the LHC's operational range.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

Beyond the hierarchy problem, several independent indications suggest that supersymmetry could indeed appear near the TeV scale.

The strengths of the three SM gauge interactions can be evolved to high energies via the renormalisation-group equations to test whether they unify into a single coupling, as predicted by Grand Unified Theories (GUTs). Without supersymmetry, this unification fails: GUTs would then require $\sin^2\theta_W \approx 0.214$, in clear tension with the experimental value of the effective neutral weak mixing parameter measured at the $Z^0$ peak, $\sin^2\theta_W \approx 0.23147 \pm 0.00049$ \cite{weakangle_lhcb}. Once the MSSM spectrum is included in the running, minimal supersymmetric GUTs predict $\sin^2\theta_W \approx 0.232$, where the residual error depends on the assumed sparticle masses, with a preferred scale around $1\,\mathrm{TeV}$~\cite{KELLEY1991423}.

Moreover, the MSSM predicts the existence of two charged higgs bosons $H^\pm$, one $CP-$odd scalar $A^0$ and two $CP-$even scalars $H^0$ and $h^0$, all resulting from taking linear combinations of the higgsinos $\tilde{H}_1^0,\, \tilde{H}_2^0$ and $\tilde{H}^\pm$. While the first four have the same mass scale around the TeV scale, the last one, $h^0$, is predicted to weigh less than about $130\,\mathrm{GeV}$~\cite{Carena_2003}, which is in excellent agreement with the SM Higgs boson $m_H^{SM} \approx 125.04 \pm 0.12\,\mathrm{GeV}$ \cite{Vagnerini:2905108}.

Finally, astrophysical and cosmological observations require the existence of cold dark matter, which could naturally be provided by a neutral, weakly-interacting massive particle of mass below about $1\,\mathrm{TeV}$. In the MSSM, the lightest supersymmetric particle (LSP), generically denoted $\chi$, is a typical candidate~\cite{ELLIS1984453}.

\subsection{Structure of the MSSM}

The formalism of any supersymmetric model is built from a Lagrangian containing a manifestly supersymmetric part and a soft supersymmetry-breaking part:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_\mathrm{SUSY} 
                + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SUSY}\, \times} .
\end{equation}

The supersymmetric piece $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{SUSY}$ of the MSSM shares the same gauge structure as the Standard Model, while its Yukawa interactions are derived from a \textit{superpotential} $W$, a cubic holomorphic function of the chiral superfields containing the left-handed fermions. This superpotential generates the masses of the charged fermions and accounts for the structure of the CKM matrix. A key feature of the MSSM is that holomorphy of $W$ and anomaly cancellation require the introduction of \textit{two} Higgs doublets $H_{1,2}$, instead of the single doublet of the SM.

After electroweak symmetry breaking, the superpartners of the electroweak gauge and Higgs bosons mix among themselves. The supersymmetric partners of the $W^\pm$ and of the charged Higgs bosons $H^\pm$ combine into two charged mass eigenstates, the \textit{charginos} $\chi_{1,2}^\pm$. Similarly, the partners of the $\gamma$, $Z^0$ and of the neutral Higgs bosons $H_{1,2}^0$ mix into four neutral mass eigenstates, the \textit{neutralinos} $\chi_{1,2,3,4}^0$. The lightest neutralino $\chi \equiv \chi_1^0$ is, in $R$-parity conserving scenarios\footnote{$R$-parity is a quantum number defined as $R=(-1)^{3(B-L)+2s}$, where $B$ is the baryon number, $L$ the lepton number and $s$ the spin, equal to $+1$ for SM particles and $-1$ for their superpartners; its conservation forbids proton decay and stabilises the lightest supersymmetric particle.}, the natural LSP and a prime candidate for astrophysical cold dark matter~\cite{ELLIS1984453}.

The supersymmetry-breaking piece $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SUSY}\, \times}$ must be carefully chosen so as not to spoil the cancellation of quadratic divergences described above. The breaking is therefore required to be ''soft'', meaning that it does not reintroduce any quadratic (nor unwanted logarithmic) divergence in scalar masses. The allowed soft terms include gaugino masses, scalar mass factors, and trilinear scalar couplings.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

In brief, the MSSM extends the SM by introducing 105 free parameters, the vast majority of which arise from the ''soft'' supersymmetry-breaking sector. Measuring such a large parameter space using colliders is daunting, and no single analysis can hope to constrain it entirely. For this reason, the experimental search strategy relies on simplified models that isolate specific subsets of parameters, often organised around the conservation or violation of $R$-parity.







\section{Long-lived particles and exotic signatures}

In the MSSM, as in other various extensions of the SM, new states are predicted with a proper decay length that is large enough to leave a distinct experimental imprint compared to the prompt particles of the SM. These long-lived particles (LLPs) span an enormous range of lifetimes, from the picosecond to cosmological scales, and require dedicated reconstruction strategies that go well beyond those used in standard prompt searches. This section motivates the existence of LLPs at colliders, surveys the experimental signatures they leave in a general-purpose detector such as CMS, and ultimately focuses on the class of long-lived states relevant to this thesis: heavy stable charged particles.


\subsection{Lifetime regimes}

The lifetimes of Standard Model particles span more than fifty orders of magnitude, from $\sim 10^{-25}\,$s for the top quark to a lower bound of $\geq 10^{34}\,$years on the proton~\cite{ParticleDataGroup:2024cfk}. Translated into proper decay lengths $c\tau$, this range covers everything from a few attometres to cosmological scales. 

From the perspective of a collider experiment, what really matters is how this proper decay length compares to the detector geometry. A particle is referred to as \textit{detector-prompt} when $c\tau$ lies well below the spatial resolution of the inner tracker, so that its decay vertex is indistinguishable from the primary interaction point; conversely, particles with $c\tau$ much larger than the outer radius of the muon system ($\sim 10\,$m) are \textit{detector-stable}, traversing the apparatus before they have a chance to decay. Between these two extremes lies a broad intermediate window, populated by long-lived particles whose decay vertex is displaced by hundreds of micrometres to several metres from the primary vertex.

Within the SM, this intermediate regime is sparsely populated, essentially restricted to weakly-decaying mesons or baryons. \cref{fig:llp_lifetimes_sm} illustrates the typical lifetime and decay lengths of SM particles as a function of their mass.

\subsection{Mechanisms producing long lifetimes}

The decay width $\Gamma = 1/\tau$ of a particle is set by the strength of the coupling driving its decay and by the available phase space. Three generic mechanisms can therefore conspire to push the lifetime of a BSM particle into the macroscopic regime, each illustrated below by a representative supersymmetric example.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Small cooupling}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

If the only allowed decay channel proceeds through a strongly suppressed interaction, the resulting width can be small enough to yield a measurable displacement. The canonical example is provided by the minimal Gauge Mediated Supersymmetry Breaking (mGMSB) scenario~\cite{Giudice:1998bp}, in which the stau is the next-to-lightest superpartner (NLSP). For such particle with mass $m_{\tilde{\tau}}$ produced with energy $E$, the average distance traversed before decay is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    L \,[\mathrm{cm}]\;=\; \frac{10^{-2}}{\kappa_{\gamma}}
    \left(\frac{100\,\mathrm{GeV}}{m_{\tilde{\tau}}}\right)^{5}
    \left(\frac{\sqrt{F/k}}{100\,\mathrm{TeV}}\right)^{4}
    \sqrt{\frac{E^{2}}{m_{\tilde{\tau}}^{2}} - 1}\;\,,
    \label{eq:stau_lifetime}
\end{equation}
where $F$ is the SUSY-breaking scale, $k$ a model-dependent coefficient and $\kappa_{\gamma}$ the coupling strength to the photon. For $\sqrt{F/k} \,\gtrsim \,10^{6}\,\mathrm{GeV}$, the resulting decay length exceeds the size of the CMS detector, and the $\tilde{\tau}$ behaves as a stable particle from an experimental standpoint.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The lifetime of an unstable particle is governed by two factors: the coupling strength of the interaction mediating the decay and the available phase space, which is controlled by the mass splitting between the parent and its decay products. In BSM scenarios, those two factors can take very different values, leading to particles sitting in the displaced and stable regime.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Heavy mediators}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

When a decay proceeds through an off-shell heavy state, its width carries an additional suppression $\propto 1/M_{\mathrm{med}}^{4}$. The Split-SUSY scenario provides a textbook example~\cite{ArkaniHamed:2004fb, Giudice:2004tc}: all sfermion masses are pushed to a high scale $m_{S} \gg \,\mathrm{TeV}$, so that the only available gluino decay channel, $\tilde{g} \to q\bar{q}\,\tilde{\chi}_{i}^{0}$, is mediated by very heavy virtual squarks. The resulting gluino lifetime,
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{\tilde{g}} \;\simeq\; 8\;
    \left(\frac{m_{S}}{10^{9}\,\mathrm{GeV}}\right)^{4}
    \left(\frac{1\,\mathrm{TeV}}{m_{\tilde{g}}}\right)^{5}\,\mathrm{s},
    \label{eq:gluino_lifetime}
\end{equation}
varies between a nanosecond and $10^{25}\,$s as $m_{S}$ ranges from $10^{7}\,\mathrm{GeV}$ up to the GUT scale ($\sim10^{16} \, \mathrm{GeV}$), leaving the gluino effectively stable across most of the parameter space.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Compressed spectra}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

If the decaying particle and the heaviest of its decay products are nearly degenerate in mass, the available phase space shrinks rapidly, and the lifetime grows steeply with the mass splitting. In the MSSM with a stop NLSP nearly degenerate with the lightest neutralino, the stop lifetime can stretch from $10^{-26}\,$s up to several seconds as $|m_{\tilde{t}} - m_{\tilde{\chi}_{1}^{0}}|$ approaches zero~\cite{Aebischer:2014mma}. A closely related mechanism is at play in anomaly-mediated SUSY breaking (AMSB), where the small radiative chargino-neutralino mass splitting 
$|m_{\tilde{\chi}_{1,2}^{\pm}} - m_{\tilde{\chi}_{1}^{0}}|\sim 160\,\mathrm{MeV}$ leads to charginos with proper decay lengths in the centimetre range, thus, long enough to be reconstructed as short tracks before disappearing in the inner tracker.



\subsection{Long-lived particles searches at the CMS detector}

LLP searches within the CMS detector are, by construction, signature-driven: the diversity of theoretical scenarios is too large for any single analysis to cover them all, and the lifetimes of interest span many orders of magnitude. A generic strategy based solely on experimental signatures is therefore the natural way to probe a broad range of BSM models simultaneously, with each lifetime regime giving rise to a distinct search topology. Those main classes, summarised schematically in \cref{fig:llp_signatures}, are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Displaced vertices.} A neutral LLP decaying inside the tracker volume into charged SM particles produces a vertex displaced by hundreds of micrometres to several metres from the PV. Dedicated algorithms identify clusters of high-impact-parameter tracks that converge on a common secondary vertex; variants targeting specific final states include displaced dijets \cite{PhysRevD.109.112005, Collaboration_2025}, displaced leptons \cite{CMS:2019qjk, 2024baababazed} or displaced photons.
    \item \textbf{Disappearing tracks.} In compressed scenarios such as AMSB-like charginos, a charged particle decays into a nearly invisible neutral state plus a very soft pion of negligible $p_{T}$. The result is a high-quality track in the innermost pixel layers that abruptly stops in the strip tracker, with no associated calorimeter or muon-system activity \cite{2020135502}, and/or in addition to MET~\cite{PhysRevD.109.112005}.
    \item \textbf{Delayed signals.} Heavy LLPs are typically produced with $\beta\gamma\lesssim 1$, leading to measurable delays in the calorimeter timing or in dedicated muon-system measurements. Such searches could be done using the scooting dataset, without being constrained by the trigger \cite{cmscollaboration2026searchheavylonglivedcharged}.
    \item \textbf{Long isolated tracks.} Charged LLPs whose decay length exceeds the detector size traverse the entire apparatus and leave a single, continuous, high-momentum track from the pixel layers to the muon chambers. This is the topology relevant for HSCP searches, discussed in detail below.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{Images/Ch3/BSM_longlived-1.pdf}
    \caption{Schematic overview of the main long-lived particle signatures at the CMS detector. Adapted from \cite{tikz}.}
    \label{fig:llp_signatures}
\end{figure}

The CMS collaboration has developed dedicated analyses targeting each of these signatures. The experimental challenges differ markedly across the decay length range, but the underlying logic is the same: identify a kinematic or topological feature that has no counterpart in the prompt SM background.




\subsection{Heavy stable charged particles}
\label{sec:hscp_intro}

The signature on which this thesis is based is the conceptually simplest of the list above: a single, isolated, charged track that traverses the entire CMS detector. The particles producing it are referred to as \textit{heavy stable charged particles} (HSCPs), where ''stable'' should be understood at the experimental scale: the proper decay length is large enough for the particle to leave the detector before decaying; and ''heavy'' refers to masses well above the electroweak scale, typically several hundreds of GeV to a few TeV. HSCPs are produced at the LHC either through direct pair production or as the end of a longer cascade~\cite{Fairbairn:2006gg}, and they can carry an electric charge of $\pm 1\,e$ or fractional/multiple values, depending on the underlying model.

This thesis doesn't focus on fractional- or multiply-charged HSCPs, for which the latest CMS results can be found, respectively, in \cite{PhysRevLett.134.131802} and \cite{MCP_status}.



\subsubsection{Hadron-like and lepton-like HSCPs}

HSCPs are conventionally split into two broad categories~\cite{Fairbairn:2006gg}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Hadron-like HSCPs}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

This thesis focuses on the MSSM long-lived stop $\tilde{t}$ and the Split-SUSY gluino $\tilde{g}$, which are the canonical hadron-like HSCP candidates. The leading-order diagrams contributing to the dominant pair-production modes of these two hadron-like HSCPs are shown in \cref{fig:hscp_prod_gluino} and \cref{fig:hscp_prod_stop}. Both proceed through quark-antiquark annihilation into an $s$-channel gluon, which subsequently splits either into a pair of stops or a pair of gluinos. Note that two additional gluons appear in each diagram. The one attached to one incoming quark line is an example of an \textit{initial-state radiation} (ISR): a coloured parton emitted by one of the incoming partons before the hard scattering occurs. The one attached to one outgoing stop is a \textit{final-state radiation} (FSR), emitted by a coloured particle in the final state. Such emissions are not accidental: they are generic consequences of QCD, in which any coloured leg of a hard process radiates additional gluons with high probability. ISR and FSR jets play an essential role in HSCP searches, as they often allow events to meet trigger requirements (typically MET-based).

\vspace{0.5 cm}
\noindent \textbf{Lepton-like HSCPs}
\vspace{0.5 cm}

HSCPs can also interact only via the electroweak force and propagate through the detector much like heavy muons, with no hadronic interactions perturbing their trajectories. The most studied example is the long-lived stau $\tilde{\tau}$ predicted in GMSB scenarios, considered both as a directly pair-produced state and as the end product of all sparticle cascade decays. Long-lived fourth-generation leptons $\tau'$, predicted by extensions of the SM with additional $U(1)'$ gauge symmetries~\cite{Hewett:2009bw}, fall into the same category and can carry single ($|Q|=1\,e$) or even double ($|Q|=2\,e$) electric charge, the latter being produced either via a modified Drell-Yan process or through the resonant decay of a heavy $Z'$ boson as shown in \cref{fig:hscp_prod_lepton}.

\vspace{0.5 cm}

The theoretical cross-section for HSCP production spans more than ten orders of magnitude at $\sqrt{s} = 13.6\,\mathrm{TeV}$, depending on the particle types and their masses; \cref{fig:xsec_theory} shows these results. 
The contrasts arise from strong versus electroweak production. At this energy, quark-antiquark fusion with gluon emission is favoured over Drell-Yan processes, producing more hadron-like HSCPs. On top of that, the gluino-gluon interaction, due to its colour-octet structure, boosts gluino production cross sections to about ten times those of squarks at equal mass, leading these hypothetical particles to be the favoured HSCPs at the LHC. On the other hand, tau-prime couplings increase with electric charge, while stau couplings are lower than both tau-prime couplings.

\subsubsection{Observable signatures.}

Because of their large mass, HSCPs produced at the LHC are typically non-relativistic, with $0.1 \lesssim \beta\gamma \lesssim 1$. Two physical consequences follow directly from this fact and constitute the experimental handles used to identify them:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anomalously high ionisation (d$E/$d$x$).} The Bethe-Bloch description of energy loss by ionisation in matter predicts a steep rise of d$E/$d$x$ for $\beta\gamma\lesssim 1$, before reaching the broad minimum characteristic of MIPs around $\beta\gamma\approx 3$--$4$. A heavy, slow particle traversing the silicon tracker, therefore, deposits substantially more charge per unit length than any prompt SM track of comparable momentum, providing a powerful discriminating variable across nearly the full HSCP mass range. This is the primary handle exploited in the analysis presented in this thesis.

    \item \textbf{Extended time-of-flight (ToF).} For the same kinematic reason, the time taken by an HSCP to traverse the muon system is significantly longer than for a relativistic muon. This delay can be measured using drift-tube and CSC timing information and complements the d$E/$d$x$ signature, particularly for lepton-like HSCPs, which leave clean, isolated tracks of high momentum. The same late arrival also motivates dedicated muon-scouting streams with relaxed timing requirements, which preserve sensitivity to slow candidates that would otherwise fail the standard prompt-muon trigger logic \cite{cmscollaboration2026searchheavylonglivedcharged}.

    \item \textbf{Missing transverse energy ($E_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{miss}}$).} A third, complementary handle is essential for hadron-like HSCPs, which hadronise into R-hadrons and may flip their electric charge through successive nuclear interactions in the calorimeter. A state produced as charged can, in particular, exit the calorimeter as neutral, leaving no hit in the muon system and thus escaping muon-based reconstruction, thus making the muon triggers less efficient. These corresponding events could be triggered and selected through a MET-based path. This topology plays an exciting role for gluino and squark R-hadrons when hadronisation favours the neutral state. As a result, it serves as a key pillar of the analysis presented in this thesis.
\end{itemize}

Together, these three handles define the experimental signatures on which the LHC HSCP programme rests. The next section reviews the current state of the art in HSCP searches in the ATLAS and CMS collaborations, outlines the complementary roles of their respective analysis channels, and situates the work presented in this thesis within that broader landscape.
